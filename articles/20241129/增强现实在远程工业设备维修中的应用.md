### Step 2: Core Concepts and Relationships

To understand the application of AR in remote industrial equipment maintenance, it is essential to first clarify several core concepts:

- **Enhanced Reality (AR)**: A technology that overlays virtual information onto the real world, providing a mixed reality experience. It primarily relies on computer vision, image processing, sensor technology, and human-computer interaction.
  
- **Remote Equipment Maintenance**: A process that involves diagnosing and repairing equipment without the need for physical presence, leveraging remote communication and networking technologies. This approach is particularly beneficial for large, high-risk equipment.

- **Industrial Equipment State Monitoring**: Real-time monitoring of critical parameters such as temperature, pressure, and speed using sensors and monitoring systems to detect potential issues promptly.

- **Fault Diagnosis**: The process of analyzing monitoring data to determine the type, cause, and location of a fault, followed by the formulation of a repair plan.

### Step 3: Core Algorithm Explanation

In remote industrial equipment maintenance, core algorithms typically involve the following key components:

- **Image Recognition and Matching Algorithms**: Used to identify specific markings or defects on equipment, with common algorithms including Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs).

- **Sensor Data Fusion Algorithms**: Used to integrate data from multiple sensors, enhancing the accuracy and reliability of monitoring.

- **Fault Diagnosis Algorithms**: Common methods include Knowledge-Based Diagnosis Systems (KBDS), Support Vector Machines (SVMs), and Neural Networks (NNs).

### Step 4: Mathematical Models and Formulas

Mathematical models and formulas are often used in fault diagnosis and prediction for remote industrial equipment maintenance. Here is a simplified linear regression model used to predict equipment failure time:

$$
\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot x
$$

where $y$ is the failure time, $x$ is the sensor data, and $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ are the model parameters. These parameters are solved using ordinary least squares:

$$
\beta_0 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i - \beta_1 \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i}{n}
$$

$$
\beta_1 = \frac{n \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \cdot y_i - \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i}{n \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2 - (\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i)^2}
```

    tf.keras.layers.Conv2D(32, (3, 3), activation='relu', input_shape=(64, 64, 3)),
    tf.keras.layers.MaxPooling2D(2, 2),
    tf.keras.layers.Flatten(),
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(64, activation='relu'),
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(1, activation='sigmoid')
])

# Compile the model
model.compile(optimizer='adam', loss='binary_crossentropy', metrics=['accuracy'])

# Load and split the dataset
(x_train, y_train), (x_test, y_test) = tf.keras.datasets.mnist.load_data()

# Normalize and reshape the data
x_train = x_train / 255.0
x_test = x_test / 255.0
x_train = x_train.reshape(-1, 28, 28, 1)
x_test = x_test.reshape(-1, 28, 28, 1)

# Train the model
model.fit(x_train, y_train, epochs=5, batch_size=32, validation_data=(x_test, y_test))

# Evaluate the model
test_loss, test_acc = model.evaluate(x_test, y_test, verbose=2)
print('\nTest accuracy:', test_acc)
```
