#### K-Means Clustering Algorithm

**Algorithm Description**:

The K-Means clustering algorithm is one of the most commonly used techniques for partitioning a dataset into K clusters, where K is a user-defined number. The algorithm aims to minimize the within-cluster sum of squares, meaning it tries to group data points in such a way that the sum of the squared distances between each point and its cluster centroid is minimized.

**Visual Aid**:

Here is a Mermaid diagram illustrating the K-Means algorithm process:

```mermaid
sequenceDiagram
  participant A as K-Means Algorithm
  participant D as Dataset
  participant C as Cluster Centroids
  A->>D: Initialize centroids randomly
  loop Until convergence
      D->>A: Assign points to the nearest centroid
      A->>C: Update centroids to the mean of the assigned points
  end
```

**Python Code Snippet**:

Below is a Python code snippet using the K-Means algorithm with the popular `scikit-learn` library:

```python
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
import numpy as np

# Generate a synthetic dataset
data = np.random.rand(100, 2)

# Initialize KMeans with 3 clusters
kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=3, random_state=0).fit(data)

# Get cluster centroids
centroids = kmeans.cluster_centers_

# Print centroids
print("Cluster centroids:\n", centroids)

# Predict the cluster labels for each data point
labels = kmeans.predict(data)

# Print labels
print("Cluster labels:\n", labels)
```

**Mathematical Model**:

The mathematical model for K-Means can be expressed as:

$$
\min_{\mu_1, \mu_2, ..., \mu_K} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{k=1}^{K} (x_i - \mu_k)^2
$$

where \( x_i \) represents a data point, \( \mu_k \) is the centroid of the k-th cluster, and \( N \) and \( K \) are the number of data points and the number of clusters, respectively.

#### Support Vector Machine (SVM) Classification

**Algorithm Description**:

The Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a powerful supervised learning algorithm used for classification tasks. It works by finding the hyperplane that best separates two classes in a high-dimensional space. The SVM algorithm maximizes the margin between the hyperplane and the nearest data points from either class, known as support vectors.

**Python Code Snippet**:

Below is a Python code snippet using the SVM classifier with the `scikit-learn` library:

```python
from sklearn.svm import SVC
import numpy as np

# Generate a synthetic dataset
X = np.random.rand(100, 2)
y = np.random.randint(0, 2, 100)

# Initialize SVM with a linear kernel
svm_classifier = SVC(kernel='linear')

# Fit the model to the training data
svm_classifier.fit(X, y)

# Predict the class labels for new data points
new_data = np.random.rand(10, 2)
predictions = svm_classifier.predict(new_data)

# Print predictions
print("Predicted labels:", predictions)
```

**Mathematical Model**:

The SVM optimization problem can be formulated as:

$$
\min_{w, b} \frac{1}{2} ||w||^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^{N} \xi_i
$$

subject to:

$$
y_i (w \cdot x_i + b) \geq 1 - \xi_i
$$

where \( w \) is the weight vector, \( b \) is the bias term, \( C \) is the regularization parameter, \( \xi_i \) are slack variables, and \( x_i \) and \( y_i \) are the input features and labels, respectively.


**Mathematical Model**:

The decision tree construction process involves selecting the best split at each node based on the highest information gain or the highest reduction in Gini impurity. Information gain and Gini impurity are measures used to evaluate the quality of a split.

**Information Gain**:

$$
IG(D, A) = \sum_{v \in Values(A)} p(v) \cdot IG(V_v, A)
$$

where \( D \) is the dataset, \( A \) is the attribute being split, \( v \) are the values of attribute \( A \), \( p(v) \) is the probability of value \( v \), and \( IG(V_v, A) \) is the information gain for the subset \( V_v \).

**Gini Impurity**:

$$
Gini(D) = 1 - \sum_{v \in Values(A)} p(v)^2
$$

### Mathematical Formulas and Explanations

In the realm of data mining and dark matter research, mathematical models and formulas are essential tools for describing and analyzing complex phenomena. In this section, we will present several key mathematical formulas used in data mining techniques, along with detailed explanations and examples to enhance understanding.

#### Euclidean Distance Formula

**Formula**:
$$
d(x, y) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - y_i)^2}
$$

**Explanation**:
The Euclidean distance formula measures the straight-line distance between two points in n-dimensional space. It is used in clustering algorithms like K-Means to determine the similarity between data points.

**Example**:
Let's calculate the Euclidean distance between two points in a 2D space:
$$
x = (1, 2), \quad y = (4, 6)
$$
$$
d(x, y) = \sqrt{(1 - 4)^2 + (2 - 6)^2} = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5
$$
The Euclidean distance between the points \( x \) and \( y \) is 5 units.

#### K-Means Clustering Objective Function

**Formula**:
$$
J(\mu_1, \mu_2, ..., \mu_K) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{k=1}^{K} (x_i - \mu_k)^2
$$

**Explanation**:
The K-Means objective function, also known as the total within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS), measures the sum of squared distances between each data point and its corresponding cluster centroid. The goal of the K-Means algorithm is to minimize this function.

**Example**:
Consider a dataset with 5 points and 2 clusters. Let the centroids be \( \mu_1 = (1, 1) \) and \( \mu_2 = (5, 5) \). The WCSS can be calculated as:
$$
J(\mu_1, \mu_2) = \sum_{i=1}^{5} (x_i - \mu_1)^2 + (x_i - \mu_2)^2
$$
$$
= ((1-1)^2 + (2-1)^2 + (3-1)^2 + (4-1)^2 + (5-1)^2) + ((1-5)^2 + (2-5)^2 + (3-5)^2 + (4-5)^2 + (5-5)^2)
$$
$$
= (0 + 1 + 4 + 9 + 16) + (16 + 9 + 4 + 1 + 0)
$$
$$
= 30 + 30 = 60
$$
The total within-cluster sum of squares for this dataset and centroids is 60.

#### Support Vector Machine (SVM) Optimization Problem

**Formula**:
$$
\min_{w, b} \frac{1}{2} ||w||^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^{N} \xi_i
$$

subject to:
$$
y_i (w \cdot x_i + b) \geq 1 - \xi_i
$$

**Explanation**:
The SVM optimization problem aims to find the hyperplane that maximizes the margin between two classes while minimizing the classification error. The objective function consists of two parts: the regularization term (controlled by the parameter \( C \)) and the slack variables \( \xi_i \).

**Example**:
Let's consider a simple 2D space with two classes and the following constraints:
$$
w \cdot x_1 + b = 1 \quad \text{(positive class)}
$$
$$
w \cdot x_2 + b = -1 \quad \text{(negative class)}
$$
$$
C = 1
$$
To find the optimal values for \( w \) and \( b \), we can solve the above optimization problem using gradient descent or other optimization algorithms.

#### Decision Tree Information Gain

**Formula**:
$$
IG(D, A) = \sum_{v \in Values(A)} p(v) \cdot IG(V_v, A)
$$

**Explanation**:
Information gain is a measure used in decision trees to determine the best feature to split the data. It quantifies the reduction in impurity (such as entropy or Gini impurity) when a dataset is split based on a feature \( A \).

**Example**:
Consider a dataset with two attributes, \( A \) and \( B \), and three values for attribute \( A \):
$$
A = \{a_1, a_2, a_3\}
$$
$$
IG(D, A) = p(a_1) \cdot IG(V_{a_1}, A) + p(a_2) \cdot IG(V_{a_2}, A) + p(a_3) \cdot IG(V_{a_3}, A)
$$
Assuming the probabilities are equal:
$$
IG(D, A) = \frac{1}{3} \cdot IG(V_{a_1}, A) + \frac{1}{3} \cdot IG(V_{a_2}, A) + \frac{1}{3} \cdot IG(V_{a_3}, A)
$$
To calculate \( IG(V_{a_1}, A) \), we need to know the impurity of the subsets \( V_{a_1} \) and the overall impurity of the dataset \( D \). For simplicity, let's assume the impurity measures are entropy and Gini impurity:
$$
IG(V_{a_1}, A) = Entropy(V_{a_1}) - \frac{|V_{a_1}|}{|D|} \cdot Entropy(D)
$$
$$
IG(D, A) = \frac{1}{3} (Entropy(V_{a_1}) - \frac{|V_{a_1}|}{|D|} \cdot Entropy(D)) + \frac{1}{3} (Entropy(V_{a_2}) - \frac{|V_{a_2}|}{|D|} \cdot Entropy(D)) + \frac{1}{3} (Entropy(V_{a_3}) - \frac{|V_{a_3}|}{|D|} \cdot Entropy(D))
$$
By calculating the entropy and impurity measures for each subset, we can compute the information gain for attribute \( A \).
