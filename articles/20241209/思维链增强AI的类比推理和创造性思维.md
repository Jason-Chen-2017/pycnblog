#### Basic Principles of Analogical Reasoning

Analogical reasoning is a cognitive process that involves drawing similarities between two or more situations, and using these similarities to make inferences or predictions. This type of reasoning is fundamental to human thinking, and it plays a crucial role in problem-solving, learning, and understanding new concepts. In this section, we will explore the basic principles of analogical reasoning, including its definition, types, and the theory of analogical mapping.

##### Definition and Types

Analogical reasoning can be defined as the process of transferring knowledge or principles from one domain (the source domain) to another domain (the target domain) based on identified similarities. This process is often represented as follows:

$$
\text{Source Domain} \rightarrow \text{Target Domain}
$$

There are several types of analogical reasoning, each with its own characteristics and applications:

1. **Direct Analogies**: In direct analogy, the source and target domains are directly similar, and the relationship between them is explicitly stated. For example, if we know that "all swans are white," and we observe a bird that is white, we might infer that this bird is a swan.

2. **Structural Analogies**: In structural analogy, the underlying structure or organization of the source and target domains is similar, even if the specific elements are different. For example, if we know that "a triangle has three sides," and we encounter a shape with three sides, we might infer that this shape is also a triangle.

3. **Constraint-Based Analogies**: In constraint-based analogy, the similarities between the source and target domains are based on constraints or rules that they both satisfy. For example, if we know that "a car must have four wheels," and we encounter a vehicle with four wheels, we might infer that it is a car.

4. **Conditional Analogies**: In conditional analogy, the relationship between the source and target domains is based on a conditional statement. For example, if we know that "if it rains, the ground gets wet," and we observe that the ground is wet, we might infer that it has rained.

##### Theory of Analogical Mapping

The theory of analogical mapping, proposed by Douglas Hofstadter in his book "Gödel, Escher, Bach: An Eternal Golden Braid," provides a framework for understanding how analogical reasoning works. According to this theory, analogical reasoning involves creating a mapping between the properties of the source and target domains.

The process of analogical mapping can be broken down into several steps:

1. **Identification of Similarities**: The first step is to identify similarities between the source and target domains. These similarities can be based on various attributes, such as shape, color, size, or function.

2. **Representation of the Mapping**: Once the similarities are identified, they need to be represented in a way that allows for comparison. This often involves creating a conceptual or symbolic representation of the domains.

3. **Transfer of Principles**: After the mapping is established, principles or knowledge from the source domain are transferred to the target domain. This transfer can be based on direct relationships, structural similarities, or constraints.

4. **Inference and Prediction**: Finally, based on the transferred knowledge, inferences and predictions are made about the target domain. This can involve making predictions about the properties of the target domain or solving problems in the target domain using the principles from the source domain.

**Example: Similarity Measure for Analogical Reasoning**

One approach to analogical reasoning is to use similarity measures to quantify the degree of similarity between the source and target domains. A common similarity measure is the Euclidean distance, which can be used to compare the feature vectors of the source and target domains.

Given two feature vectors `x` and `y`, the Euclidean distance between them can be calculated as follows:

$$
d(x, y) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - y_i)^2}
$$

where `n` is the number of features, and `x_i` and `y_i` are the i-th components of the feature vectors `x` and `y`, respectively.

The lower the Euclidean distance between the feature vectors, the higher the degree of similarity between the source and target domains.

##### Theory of Creative Problem Solving

One of the most influential theories of creative thinking is the Creative Problem Solving (CPS) model, developed by Robert E. Storm. The CPS model provides a systematic approach to generating creative solutions to problems and can be applied in various domains, from business and engineering to science and the arts.

The CPS model consists of several stages, each involving specific techniques and methods to foster creative thinking:

1. **Clarification**: In this stage, the problem is defined and understood more deeply. Techniques such as brainstorming, mind mapping, and problem definition are used to clarify the problem and identify its key components.

2. **Idea Finding**: This stage focuses on generating a wide range of ideas and solutions to the problem. Techniques such as brainstorming, random entry, and forced connections are used to encourage free-thinking and explore different perspectives.

3. **Idea Evaluation**: Once a large number of ideas have been generated, they are evaluated based on criteria such as feasibility, potential impact, and creativity. Techniques such as ranking, scoring, and multi-criteria decision analysis are used to identify the most promising ideas.

4. **Development**: In this stage, the selected ideas are further developed and refined. Techniques such as sketching, modeling, and prototyping are used to explore and refine the ideas.

5. **Implementation**: The final stage involves implementing the chosen solution. Techniques such as planning, resource allocation, and project management are used to ensure the successful implementation of the solution.

```python
import tensorflow as tf
import numpy as np

# Define the RNN model
class RNN(tf.keras.Model):
    def __init__(self, vocab_size, embedding_dim, rnn_units):
        super(RNN, self).__init__()
        self.embedding = tf.keras.layers.Embedding(vocab_size, embedding_dim)
        self.rnn = tf.keras.layers.SimpleRNN(rnn_units)
        self.dense = tf.keras.layers.Dense(vocab_size)

    def call(self, inputs, states=None, return_state=False):
        x = self.embedding(inputs)
        if states is None:
            states = self.rnn.initialize_states(x.shape[1])
        x, states = self.rnn(x, initial_states=states)
        x = self.dense(x)
        if return_state:
            return x, states
        else:
            return x

# Create the model
model = RNN(vocab_size, embedding_dim, rnn_units=256)

# Compile the model
model.compile(optimizer='adam', loss=tf.keras.losses.SparseCategoricalCrossentropy(from_logits=True))
```
