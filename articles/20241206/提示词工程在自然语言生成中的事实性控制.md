#### 3.2 Detailed Explanation of Algorithm Steps

**3.2.1 Data Preprocessing**

The first step in the algorithm is data preprocessing. This involves cleaning and preparing the input data to ensure that it is free from noise and inconsistencies. The specific techniques used in data preprocessing depend on the nature of the input data and the requirements of the task.

Here are some common preprocessing techniques:

- **Tokenization:** This involves splitting the text into individual words or tokens. For example, the sentence "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog" would be tokenized into ["The", "quick", "brown", "fox", "jumps", "over", "the", "lazy", "dog"].

- **Lemmatization:** This involves reducing words to their base or dictionary form. For example, "running" would be lemmatized to "run".

- **Stop-word Removal:** This involves removing common words that do not contribute much meaning to the text, such as "is", "and", "the", etc.

**3.2.2 Fact Extraction**

The next step is fact extraction, where relevant factual information is identified and extracted from the input data. This step is crucial as it determines the accuracy and reliability of the generated text.

Here are some common techniques used for fact extraction:

- **Named Entity Recognition (NER):** This technique involves identifying and categorizing named entities in the text, such as persons, organizations, locations, and dates. For example, "Apple Inc. was founded in 1976" would identify "Apple Inc." as a named entity and categorize it as an organization.

- **Relation Extraction:** This technique involves identifying and extracting relationships between entities. For example, from the sentence "Elon Musk founded SpaceX," the relation "founded" would be extracted between the entities "Elon Musk" and "SpaceX".

- **Coreference Resolution:** This technique involves identifying when different parts of a text refer to the same entity. For example, in the sentence "Elon Musk founded SpaceX, and he is its CEO," "he" would be resolved as referring to "Elon Musk".

**3.2.3 Fact Verification**

Once the facts have been extracted, the next step is to verify them. Fact verification involves checking the extracted facts against external sources or databases to ensure their accuracy and reliability. This step is crucial to ensure that the generated text contains truthful and credible information.

Here are some common techniques used for fact verification:

- **Cross-Referencing:** This involves comparing the extracted facts with known facts from external sources or databases. For example, if the extracted fact states that "Apple Inc. was founded in 1976," this fact would be cross-referenced with a reliable database or encyclopedia to verify its accuracy.

- **Fact-checking:** This involves using specialized fact-checking services or algorithms to verify the accuracy of the extracted facts. For example, a fact-checking service might be used to verify the statement "COVID-19 was caused by a lab leak."

- **Consistency Checking:** This involves checking the consistency of the extracted facts within the text and with known facts. For example, if two extracted facts contradict each other, such as "Elon Musk founded SpaceX" and "Elon Musk did not found SpaceX," then a consistency check would identify this discrepancy.

**3.2.4 Fact Integration**

After the extracted facts have been verified, the next step is to integrate them into the NLG system. This involves incorporating the verified facts into the text generation process to ensure that the generated text contains accurate and coherent information.

Here are some common techniques used for fact integration:

- **Template-Based Generation:** This involves using predefined templates to insert verified facts into the text. For example, a template might be used to insert the fact "Apple Inc. was founded in 1976" into a sentence such as "Apple Inc., founded in [YEAR], is a technology company."

- **Rule-Based Generation:** This involves using a set of rules to guide the integration of verified facts into the text. For example, a rule might be used to ensure that dates are always presented in a consistent format.

- **Data-Driven Generation:** This involves using statistical models or machine learning techniques to generate text based on the verified facts. For example, a generative model might be trained to generate product descriptions based on verified product attributes.

**3.2.5 Factuality Assessment**

The final step in the algorithm is factuality assessment. This involves evaluating the factual accuracy of the generated text using factuality metrics and user feedback. This step is essential for ensuring that the generated text meets the required standards of accuracy, consistency, and reliability.

Here are some common techniques used for factuality assessment:

- **Factuality Metrics:** This involves using quantitative measures such as accuracy, consistency, and reliability to evaluate the factual accuracy of the generated text. For example, the accuracy of the generated text might be evaluated by comparing it to a ground truth dataset.

- **User Feedback:** This involves collecting feedback from users to assess the quality of the generated text. For example, users might be asked to rate the accuracy and relevance of the generated text on a scale of 1 to 5.

- **Machine Learning Models:** This involves using machine learning models to predict the factual accuracy of the generated text. For example, a supervised learning model might be trained to predict the accuracy of generated text based on its features.

#### 3.4 Mathematical Model and Formulas

The factuality control algorithm can be represented using a mathematical model that quantifies the accuracy, consistency, and reliability of the generated text. The model is as follows:

$$ F = \alpha \cdot A + \beta \cdot C + \gamma \cdot R $$

where:

- \( F \) is the overall factuality score.
- \( A \) is the accuracy of the generated text.
- \( C \) is the consistency of the generated text.
- \( R \) is the reliability of the sources used in the generated text.
- \( \alpha \), \( \beta \), and \( \gamma \) are weight factors that determine the relative importance of accuracy, consistency, and reliability in the factuality score.

The accuracy, consistency, and reliability can be measured using the following formulas:

**Accuracy (A):**
$$ A = \frac{\text{Number of correct facts}}{\text{Total number of facts}} $$

**Consistency (C):**
$$ C = \frac{\text{Number of consistent facts}}{\text{Total number of facts}} $$

**Reliability (R):**
$$ R = \frac{\text{Number of reliable sources}}{\text{Total number of sources}} $$
