This article aims to provide a comprehensive overview of these theories, highlighting their core principles, differences, and contributions to the field of humor studies.

### Chapter 1: Theoretical Foundations

In this chapter, we delve into the foundational theories of laughter and humor proposed by two seminal thinkers: Henri Bergson and Ludwig Wittgenstein. #### 1.1.1 Origins and Evolution of Laughter Theory

Laughter theory has a rich history that spans across various disciplines, including philosophy, psychology, sociology, and anthropology. The origins of laughter theory can be traced back to ancient civilizations, where scholars and philosophers began to speculate about the nature and functions of laughter. In ancient Greece, for example, the philosopher Aristotle discussed laughter as a form of emotional release and a social bond. Similarly, in ancient India, the Sanskrit texts contained references to laughter as a spiritual practice.

The scientific exploration of laughter began in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. French philosopher Henri Bergson was one of the first scholars to propose a comprehensive theory of laughter. Bergson's theory, which is often referred to as the "élan vital" theory, posits that laughter arises from the incongruity between our understanding of the world and the actual movements we observe. This theory emphasizes the role of social norms and the inhibition of death in humor.

In the mid-20th century, laughter research gained further momentum with the works of psychologists like Robert R. Provine, who conducted extensive studies on the evolutionary and social functions of laughter. Provine's research demonstrated that laughter is a universal human behavior, present in all cultures and closely related to emotional expression and social bonding. Additionally, sociologists like Peter McGraw and Joel Warner introduced the concept of "benign violation," which suggests that humor often arises from situations that violate our expectations in a non-harmful or benign way.

#### 1.1.2 Core Theories of Laughter in the Social Sciences

In the social sciences, laughter has been explored through various theoretical frameworks, each offering unique insights into its nature and functions. One prominent theory is the "incongruity theory," which posits that humor arises from incongruity between what we expect and what actually occurs. This theory is closely related to Bergson's concept of laughter as a response to incongruity between our understanding of the world and reality.

Another influential theory is the "benign violation theory," which suggests that humor often arises from situations that violate our expectations in a non-harmful or benign way. This theory emphasizes the role of surprise and the relaxation that follows as key components of humor. For example, a comedian may create humor by presenting a situation that is unexpected but ultimately harmless, leading to laughter as a form of emotional relief.

The "superiority theory," proposed by Thomas Hobbes and later developed by Henri Bergson, posits that laughter arises from a sense of superiority over the subject of the humor. This theory suggests that laughter serves as a social mechanism for establishing and maintaining social hierarchies.

In addition to these theories, contemporary social scientists have also explored the role of laughter in social bonding and emotional regulation. Research has shown that laughter can enhance social connections, foster teamwork, and reduce stress. Laughter has been associated with increased levels of oxytocin, a hormone that is known to play a key role in social bonding.

#### 1.2 Bergson's Theory of Humor

Henri Bergson, a French philosopher, is often considered one of the most influential figures in the development of laughter theory. His concept of humor is deeply rooted in his broader philosophical framework, which emphasizes the importance of movement, change, and the social order.

**1.2.1 Life, Movement, and Durée**

At the core of Bergson's philosophy is the concept of "élan vital," or vital force, which he describes as the inherent drive towards movement and change. He posits that life is fundamentally characterized by movement, and that this movement is essential for understanding the nature of reality. In contrast, Bergson argues that mechanistic and static views of the world fail to capture the true essence of life.

A key concept in Bergson's philosophy is "durée," which refers to the continuous flow of time and the experience of time as a series of fleeting moments. Durée contrasts with the notion of "کنش" or "intervals," which represents the discrete and measurable units of time. For Bergson, understanding durée is crucial for grasping the essence of life and the human experience.

**1.2.2 The Laughter of Superiority and Inhibition of Death**

Bergson's theory of humor revolves around the concepts of the "laughter of superiority" and the "inhibition of death." According to Bergson, laughter arises from a feeling of superiority over something or someone. This sense of superiority can be directed towards a variety of subjects, ranging from absurdity to the incongruity between our understanding of the world and reality.

The laughter of superiority serves a social function by reinforcing social hierarchies and norms. By laughing at others, we establish our own status and reaffirm social structures. However, Bergson also suggests that laughter has a deeper, more universal function: the inhibition of death. He argues that laughter is a response to the anxiety and fear of death, serving as a means of psychological defense and a reminder of the fleeting nature of life.

**1.2.3 Bergson's Influence on Modern Humor Theory**

Bergson's humor theory has had a significant impact on the development of modern humor studies. His emphasis on the role of incongruity and the social function of laughter has influenced subsequent theories and research in the field. Moreover, his concept of "élan vital" and the idea of movement as a key aspect of life have provided a foundational framework for understanding humor as a dynamic and evolving phenomenon.

Bergson's work has also sparked debates and discussions among scholars, leading to a deeper exploration of the complex nature of humor. Critics have questioned the universality of his theories and have highlighted the limitations of his approach. Despite these critiques, Bergson's contributions to humor theory remain a cornerstone in the field, offering valuable insights into the nature and functions of laughter.

#### 1.3 Wittgenstein's Theory of Humor

Ludwig Wittgenstein, an Austrian-British philosopher, offers a distinct perspective on humor through his concept of "language games" and the "form of life." Unlike Bergson's emphasis on social norms and movement, Wittgenstein's approach is more focused on the linguistic and contextual aspects of humor.

**1.3.1 Language Games and Form of Life**

Wittgenstein's philosophical project is centered around the idea that language should be understood in the context of specific "language games." A language game is a conceptual framework that defines the rules and purposes of language use in a particular situation. By analyzing language games, Wittgenstein seeks to uncover the diverse ways in which language functions in different contexts.

The concept of the "form of life" is central to Wittgenstein's understanding of humor. The form of life refers to the shared cultural and social practices that shape our understanding of the world. Wittgenstein argues that our understanding of humor is deeply rooted in these practices, and that humor arises from the recognition of these practices and their underlying rules.

**1.3.2 The Structure of Laughter in Wittgenstein's Perspective**

Wittgenstein's theory of humor focuses on the structure of laughter and its relationship to language games. He suggests that laughter is a form of linguistic communication that serves as a response to incongruity or the violation of expected norms within a specific language game. For example, a joke may create humor by presenting a situation that violates our expectations in a particular context, leading to laughter as a form of recognition and affirmation of these norms.

**1.3.3 Wittgenstein's Departure from Bergson's Theory**

Wittgenstein's approach to humor marks a departure from Bergson's theory in several key ways. While Bergson emphasizes the social and biological functions of laughter, Wittgenstein focuses on the linguistic and contextual aspects. Bergson's theory of humor is more deterministic, positing that laughter arises from a sense of superiority or the violation of social norms. In contrast, Wittgenstein's theory is more fluid and context-dependent, emphasizing the role of individual interpretation and the specific language games that shape our understanding of humor.

Moreover, Wittgenstein's focus on language games and the form of life highlights the diversity and complexity of humor, challenging the notion of a universal theory of humor. His approach encourages a more nuanced understanding of humor that recognizes the role of cultural and social practices in shaping our perceptions and responses to humor.

In summary, Wittgenstein's theory of humor offers a unique perspective on the nature of laughter, grounded in his broader philosophical project of understanding language and meaning. By emphasizing the role of language games and the form of life, Wittgenstein provides valuable insights into the complex nature of humor and its relationship to language and culture.

### Chapter 2: Comparing Bergson and Wittgenstein's Humor Theories

#### 2.1 Methodological Differences

The methodological approaches of Bergson and Wittgenstein diverge significantly, reflecting their different philosophical backgrounds and concerns. Bergson's method is largely empirical and grounded in the observation of social behaviors. He focuses on the physical and social aspects of laughter, drawing from his experiences and observations of everyday life. His theory is often described as "descriptive" and "phenomenological," aiming to capture the essence of laughter through direct observation and analysis of its manifestations in social interactions.

In contrast, Wittgenstein's method is more analytical and linguistic. He approaches humor through the lens of language games, which he views as the specific contexts in which language is used. Wittgenstein's method involves a meticulous examination of language use, focusing on the rules and purposes that govern different language games. His approach is characterized by a "philosophical analysis" that seeks to uncover the underlying structures and principles that shape our understanding of humor.

**2.1.1 Analytic and Continental Approaches**

Bergson's method can be seen as an example of the "continental" approach to philosophy, which emphasizes the importance of historical and cultural contexts in understanding concepts and ideas. Continental philosophy often engages with the works of great thinkers from the Western intellectual tradition, seeking to uncover the deeper structures of meaning and interpretation that underlie human experience.

In contrast, Wittgenstein's method is more aligned with the "analytic" approach to philosophy, which prioritizes logical analysis and the clarity of language. Analytic philosophy is characterized by its focus on rigorous argumentation and the clarification of concepts through the analysis of language. This approach is often associated with the development of formal logic and the study of language in its most precise and abstract forms.

**2.1.2 The Role of Philosophy in Understanding Humor**

The methodological differences between Bergson and Wittgenstein have significant implications for their understanding of humor. Bergson's focus on empirical observation and social behavior allows him to explore the practical functions of laughter in everyday life. His theory emphasizes the social and cultural dimensions of humor, highlighting the role of social norms, customs, and conventions in shaping our responses to humor.

Wittgenstein's analytical approach, on the other hand, allows him to delve into the deeper structures of meaning and interpretation that underlie our understanding of humor. His emphasis on language games and the form of life provides a nuanced understanding of humor that recognizes the diverse ways in which language and cultural practices shape our perceptions and responses to humor.

#### 2.2 Core Assumptions

While Bergson and Wittgenstein share a common interest in understanding the nature of humor, their theories are grounded in different core assumptions. These assumptions shape their approaches and influence their conclusions about the nature and functions of laughter.

**2.2.1 The Nature of Laughter and Its Functions**

Bergson's theory of humor is centered around the concept of "élan vital," which he views as the inherent drive towards movement and change. He posits that laughter arises from the incongruity between our understanding of the world and the actual movements we observe. This theory emphasizes the role of social norms, the inhibition of death, and the social function of laughter in reinforcing social hierarchies.

In contrast, Wittgenstein's theory is grounded in his concept of "language games" and the "form of life." He suggests that laughter is a form of linguistic communication that arises from the violation of expected norms within specific language games. This theory emphasizes the role of individual interpretation, the context-specific nature of humor, and the influence of cultural and social practices in shaping our understanding of humor.

**2.2.2 The Relationship Between Laughter and Culture**

Bergson's theory highlights the role of cultural and social factors in shaping our responses to humor. He argues that laughter is a universal human behavior, but its functions and manifestations can vary across different cultures and societies. This theory suggests that humor is deeply intertwined with social norms, customs, and traditions, and that our understanding of humor is shaped by the specific cultural contexts in which we live.

Wittgenstein's theory also acknowledges the influence of cultural and social factors on humor. However, his approach focuses more on the specific language games and the rules that govern them. He argues that humor is a complex phenomenon that cannot be fully understood without considering the diverse ways in which language and cultural practices shape our perceptions and interpretations of humor.

#### 2.3 Key Differences and Common Grounds

Despite their differences, Bergson and Wittgenstein's theories share certain commonalities and offer valuable insights into the nature of humor. Here, we will discuss the key differences between their approaches and the areas where their theories converge.

**2.3.1 Theoretical Breakthroughs and Limitations**

Bergson's theory of humor marks a significant breakthrough in the understanding of laughter as a social phenomenon. His emphasis on the role of incongruity and the social function of laughter has influenced subsequent theories and research in the field. However, critics argue that his theory is overly deterministic and fails to account for the individual interpretation and context-specific nature of humor.

Wittgenstein's theory, on the other hand, offers a more nuanced understanding of humor by emphasizing the role of language games and individual interpretation. His approach challenges the deterministic nature of Bergson's theory and highlights the diverse ways in which humor can be understood and experienced. **2.3.2 The Impact on Modern Humor Studies**

Both Bergson and Wittgenstein have had a significant impact on modern humor studies. Bergson's theory has influenced various fields, including psychology, sociology, and anthropology, by providing a framework for understanding the social functions of laughter. His emphasis on the role of incongruity and the social function of laughter has spurred further research into the cognitive and emotional aspects of humor.

Wittgenstein's theory has also influenced modern humor studies by challenging the notion of a universal theory of humor and emphasizing the role of cultural and social practices in shaping our understanding of humor. His focus on language games and the form of life has led to a deeper exploration of the complexities of humor and its relationship to language and culture.

In summary, while Bergson and Wittgenstein offer distinct perspectives on humor, their theories share certain commonalities and have had a significant impact on modern humor studies. **3.1.2 Emotional Regulation and Coping**
