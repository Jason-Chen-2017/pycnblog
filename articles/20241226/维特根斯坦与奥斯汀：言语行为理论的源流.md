**Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889-1951)**

Wittgenstein was an Austrian-British philosopher who is often considered one of the most influential figures in the history of Western philosophy. His work is characterized by a stark contrast between two distinct periods: the early and the later periods. His early work, primarily expressed in his seminal text "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus" (1921), is grounded in logical positivism and aims to delineate the relationship between language, thought, and reality. In this work, Wittgenstein proposes that the meaning of words is derived from their pictorial relationship with the world, and that philosophical problems arise from the misuse of language.

The later Wittgenstein, however, presents a markedly different approach in his work "Philosophical Investigations" (1953). Here, he abandons the formalistic and logical approach of his early work and instead focuses on the use of language in everyday life. He introduces the concept of "language games," which he uses to illustrate how language is contextually bound and how its meaning is determined by its use in specific situations. Wittgenstein argues that philosophical confusion stems from the misapplication of language to areas where it cannot be appropriately used, such as in metaphysics or in the search for universal definitions.

**John Langshaw Austin (1911-1960)**

John Langshaw Austin was an English philosopher, known primarily for his development of the speech act theory. He was a student of Bertrand Russell and later became a professor at Oxford University. Austin's philosophical work is deeply influenced by his engagement with ordinary language philosophy, which seeks to understand the meaning and use of language as it is actually employed in everyday life, rather than through abstract theoretical constructs.

Austin's seminal work, "How to Do Things with Words" (1962), co-authored with J.L. Searle, is a cornerstone of speech act theory. In this book, Austin introduces the concept of the performative utterance, which is a statement that accomplishes an action through the very act of uttering it. He distinguishes between three types of speech acts: locutionary acts (the actual utterance of words), illocutionary acts (the effects intended by the utterance), and perlocutionary acts (the subsequent consequences of the utterance). This framework provides a comprehensive model for understanding how language is used to perform actions and achieve specific communicative purposes.

One of the key insights of Wittgenstein's early work is the idea that language is a tool for thought. He argued that the confusion and paradoxes that arise in philosophy are often the result of using language in ways that are not appropriate or in contexts where it cannot provide meaningful answers. Wittgenstein's later work, "Philosophical Investigations," marks a significant departure from his earlier views. Wittgenstein's later philosophy also introduced the idea of "family resemblance" as a way to understand the complexities of meaning. Family resemblance is a concept that allows for flexibility and diversity in the use of language. **Austin's Speech Act Theory**

John Langshaw Austin's speech act theory is one of the most significant contributions to the field of linguistic philosophy. In his seminal work "How to Do Things with Words," co-authored with J.L. Searle, Austin introduced the concept of the performative utterance. A performative utterance is a statement that performs an action through the act of uttering it. For example, when a priest says, "I now pronounce you man and wife," the utterance itself performs the action of marriage.

Austin distinguished between three types of speech acts: locutionary acts, illocutionary acts, and perlocutionary acts. A locutionary act is the actual utterance of words, while an illocutionary act is the effect intended by the utterance. A perlocutionary act is the subsequent consequence of the utterance, such as the change in someone's mind or behavior that results from the communication.

**Performative Utterances and Speech Acts**

One of the key ideas in Austin's theory is the distinction between constative utterances and performative utterances. Constative utterances are statements that report or describe a state of affairs, such as "The sky is blue." In contrast, performative utterances are statements that perform an action, such as "I bet you $10 that the sky is blue."

Austin's theory also introduced the concept of the threefold division of speech acts. The first division is between utterances that assert or describe (constatives) and utterances that perform actions (performatives). The second division is between utterances that make a statement (representatives) and utterances that make a request, offer, or promise (directives). The third division is between utterances that express feelings or emotions (expresives) and utterances that provide information or make a commitment (commissives).

**Pragmatic Approach to Language**

Austin's speech act theory is grounded in a pragmatic approach to language, which emphasizes the importance of context and use in understanding meaning. This approach contrasts with more traditional views of language that focus on the meaning of words in isolation. By focusing on the actions performed through language, Austin's theory provides a more dynamic and interactive view of communication.
