## 2. Bergson's Interpretation of Free Will

### 2.1 Historical Context

Henri Bergson (1859-1941) was a prominent French philosopher who made significant contributions to various fields, including philosophy, psychology, and social sciences. Born in Paris, Bergson was influenced by the intellectual atmosphere of his time, which was characterized by the rise of Darwinism and the debate on the nature of consciousness.

Bergson's early work focused on the study of consciousness and its relationship to time. His seminal work, "Les données immédiates de la conscience" ("The Immediate Data of Consciousness"), published in 1889, laid the foundation for his later ideas on the nature of consciousness and free will. In this work, Bergson argued that consciousness is not a stream of moments but a continuous and evolving process.

Bergson's philosophy evolved over time, and he eventually developed his concept of élan vital, or vital force, which he believed was the underlying force driving both human consciousness and free will. This idea was further elaborated in his 1907 work, "L'Évolution créatrice" ("The Creative Evolution"), where he proposed that life is characterized by a continuous process of creation and innovation.

### 2.2 Core Concepts and Theories

#### 2.2.1 Time and Free Will

One of Bergson's central ideas is the distinction between two types of time: duration and time as measured by clocks. Duration, according to Bergson, is a continuous and evolving process that is inherently linked to consciousness. It is subjective and experienced as a flowing present, while time as measured by clocks is objective and quantifiable.

Bergson argued that free will is intimately connected to duration. Because free will requires the capacity to make choices in the present moment, it is dependent on the continuous and evolving nature of consciousness. This idea is captured in his famous analogy of the clock and the dancer:

Imagine a dancer moving rhythmically to the beat of a clock. The dancer's movements are determined by the clock's ticks, which represent objective time. Now, imagine the dancer trying to move to the beat of her own inner rhythm. In this scenario, the dancer's movements are not determined by an external clock but by her own inner sense of duration. This inner rhythm, or duration, is what allows the dancer to exercise free will in her movements.

#### 2.2.2 Élan Vital

Bergson's concept of élan vital, or vital force, is another crucial element in his interpretation of free will. Élan vital is the underlying force that drives the creative process and the continuous evolution of life. It is a non-material force that is inherent in all living beings and is responsible for their capacity to adapt and innovate.

For Bergson, élan vital is the source of free will. It represents the intrinsic drive within individuals to create new possibilities and to make choices that are not determined by external factors. This drive is not static but is constantly evolving, much like the continuous and evolving nature of consciousness.

### 2.3 Comparative Analysis

Bergson's interpretation of free will is distinct from other philosophical perspectives on the topic. For example, determinism, which posits that all events are determined by prior causes, is directly opposed to Bergson's view of free will. Similarly, compatibilism, which argues that free will is compatible with determinism, does not align with Bergson's understanding of free will as a creative and evolving process.

While Bergson's ideas share some similarities with existentialism, particularly in their emphasis on individual freedom and responsibility, there are also notable differences. Existentialism, as exemplified by Jean-Paul Sartre, often focuses on the subjective experience of freedom and the burdens that come with it. In contrast, Bergson's approach is more philosophical and scientific, emphasizing the underlying forces that drive human consciousness and free will.

### 2.4 Summary

In summary, Bergson's interpretation of free will is rooted in his philosophy of duration and élan vital. He posits that free will is an intrinsic part of human consciousness, dependent on the continuous and evolving nature of time. By understanding the dynamic relationship between duration and free will, we can gain a deeper appreciation of the philosophical and ethical implications of human agency. Bergson's ideas provide a unique perspective on free will that is both scientifically grounded and philosophically profound, offering valuable insights into the nature of human existence and the ethical considerations that arise from it.

## 3. Sartre's Interpretation of Free Will

### 3.1 Historical Context

Jean-Paul Sartre (1905-1980) was a French philosopher, playwright, and novelist who is widely regarded as one of the most influential thinkers of the 20th century. His work encompasses a wide range of topics, including existentialism, phenomenology, ethics, and political philosophy. Sartre's philosophical career was marked by a series of seminal works, including "L'Être et le Néant" ("Being and Nothingness"), published in 1943, which laid the foundation for his ideas on free will and existence.

Sartre was deeply influenced by the works of philosopher Edmund Husserl, who introduced him to the method of phenomenology. Phenomenology is a philosophical approach that focuses on the study of experience from the subjective perspective of the individual. This approach became a central component of Sartre's philosophical framework, particularly in his exploration of free will and consciousness.

### 3.2 Core Concepts and Theories

#### 3.2.1 Existentialism and Radical Freedom

Sartre's philosophy is closely associated with existentialism, a philosophical movement that emphasizes the importance of individual existence, freedom, and authenticity. For Sartre, existence precedes essence, which means that human beings do not have predetermined purposes or essences; instead, they create their own meanings through their choices and actions.

One of Sartre's central concepts is radical freedom, which he defines as the absolute freedom to make choices without being determined by external factors or predetermined natures. This idea is central to his interpretation of free will. According to Sartre, free will is not merely the ability to make choices but the awareness of one's absolute freedom and the responsibility that comes with it.

#### 3.2.2 Consciousness and Choice

For Sartre, consciousness is the foundation of human existence. He argues that consciousness is not a passive receiver of information but an active agent that creates its own experiences and interpretations of the world. This active engagement with the world is what allows individuals to exercise free will and make choices.

Sartre's concept of choice is closely linked to his idea of consciousness. He posits that every choice is an affirmation of one's being and simultaneously a rejection of alternative possibilities. This means that every choice is not only a decision but also an expression of one's identity and values. As such, free will is an inherent aspect of human consciousness, and the responsibility that comes with it is inextricably linked to our self-awareness.

### 3.3 Comparative Analysis

Sartre's interpretation of free will is distinct from other philosophical perspectives, such as determinism and compatibilism. Determinism posits that all events, including human actions, are determined by prior causes and are therefore not free. Compatibilism, on the other hand, argues that free will is compatible with determinism because, while our actions may be determined, they are still free in the sense that we have the ability to make different choices.

Sartre's view of free will diverges from both of these positions. He emphasizes the absolute nature of free will, arguing that human beings are not only free but also responsible for their actions. This means that free will is not limited by external factors or predetermined causes but is a fundamental aspect of human existence that requires us to take responsibility for our choices and their consequences.

While Sartre's existentialism shares some similarities with Bergson's ideas about the fluidity of time and the importance of individual agency, there are also important differences. For Bergson, free will is a product of the continuous and evolving nature of consciousness, while for Sartre, free will is an inherent aspect of human existence that requires us to confront the reality of our absolute freedom and the responsibility that comes with it.

### 3.4 Summary

Sartre's interpretation of free will is rooted in his existentialist philosophy, which emphasizes the importance of individual existence, freedom, and authenticity. He argues that free will is not only the ability to make choices but also the awareness of one's absolute freedom and the responsibility that comes with it. By understanding the role of consciousness and choice in shaping human existence, we can gain a deeper appreciation of the philosophical and ethical implications of free will. Sartre's ideas provide a compelling perspective on the nature of human agency and the profound responsibility that comes with it, offering valuable insights into the complexities of human existence and the ethical considerations that arise from it.
