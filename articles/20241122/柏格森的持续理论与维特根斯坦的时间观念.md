#### 1.1. Background and Significance of Bergson's Theory of Duration

Bergson's theory of duration, a central concept in his philosophical system, distinguishes his thought from other philosophers of his time. Henri Bergson (1859-1941), a French philosopher, mathematician, and sociologist, introduced the concept of "duration" to challenge the dominant idea of time as a linear sequence of events. His work has had a profound influence on various fields, including philosophy, psychology, and cognitive science.

##### 1.1.1. Historical Context of Bergson's Work

Bergson's contributions to philosophy emerged during a period when traditional metaphysical and epistemological frameworks were being questioned. His work was influenced by thinkers like Immanuel Kant, Friedrich Nietzsche, and William James. Bergson critiqued the mechanical view of time proposed by Isaac Newton and the resultant emphasis on determinism and causality in scientific explanations. He argued that this approach fails to account for the complexity and richness of human experience.

##### 1.1.2. Key Ideas in Bergson's Philosophy

Bergson's philosophy revolves around the idea of "élan vital," or vital force, which he believed is the fundamental property of life. This concept is closely intertwined with his theory of duration. According to Bergson, duration is a psychological and metaphysical reality that contrasts with the abstract and mathematical notion of time. Duration is characterized by its intensity and the feeling of passage, which he described as " Duration est l'intensité de l'état actuel par rapport aux états passés et à ceux qui peuvent suivre." (Duration is the intensity of the present state in relation to past and future states.)

Bergson asserted that human consciousness is structured around the perception of duration rather than the linear sequence of events. He proposed the concept of "memory" as a mechanism that allows us to experience the past and future in a continuous stream. This idea contrasts sharply with the notion of time as a series of isolated moments.

##### 1.1.3. Critiques and Impact of Bergson's Theory

Bergson's theory of duration has been both praised and criticized. Some philosophers have found his ideas challenging to reconcile with physical theories of time. For instance, Albert Einstein's theory of relativity presents a different view of time, one in which time is relative and not absolute. However, Bergson's emphasis on the subjective experience of time has influenced later philosophers, including Maurice Merleau-Ponty and Jean-Paul Sartre.

In the field of psychology, Bergson's ideas have found resonance in the study of perception and memory. His concept of "duration" has been used to explain how people experience time differently under various conditions, such as when they are in a rush or when they are engaged in an enjoyable activity. Cognitive science has also explored the implications of Bergson's theory, particularly in how we process and remember information.

#### 1.2. Introduction to Wittgenstein's Concept of Time

Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889-1951), an Austrian-British philosopher, is known for his influential work in the fields of philosophy of language, mathematics, and mind. Wittgenstein's views on time are diverse and complex, evolving throughout his career. His early work, particularly the "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus," presents a view of time as a series of static, atomic facts. In contrast, his later work, such as the "Philosophical Investigations," offers a more nuanced understanding of time and its relationship with language and thought.

##### 1.2.1. Wittgenstein's Life and Philosophical Influence

Wittgenstein's life was marked by periods of intense intellectual activity and contemplation. He initially studied mathematics and philosophy at the University of Cambridge, where he was influenced by figures like Bertrand Russell and G.E. Moore. His early work was heavily influenced by Russell's logical atomism and the idea that the world is composed of simple, atomic facts. However, Wittgenstein's philosophical journey took a different path as he became dissatisfied with this approach.

In his later life, Wittgenstein shifted his focus to the study of language and its relationship with reality. His later work emphasized the importance of language games and the context in which they are used. This perspective has had a significant impact on contemporary philosophy, particularly in the field of language philosophy and the philosophy of mind.

##### 1.2.2. Early and Later Wittgenstein on Time

In his early work, Wittgenstein presented a view of time as a series of static moments that are glued together by logic. The "Tractatus" asserts that time is an illusion and that reality is composed of timeless facts. This perspective is closely related to his view of logic and language as the primary means of understanding the world.

In contrast, Wittgenstein's later work offers a more dynamic and nuanced view of time. The "Philosophical Investigations" presents time as a series of interconnected events that are understood through language. Wittgenstein emphasizes the importance of the language we use to describe time and how this language shapes our understanding of the world.

##### 1.2.3. The Role of Language in Wittgenstein's Time Theory

A central theme in Wittgenstein's later philosophy is the role of language in shaping our understanding of reality. He argued that our concepts of time and space are not fixed or objective but are shaped by the language we use. Wittgenstein's concept of "language games" refers to the different ways in which language is used in various contexts. He asserted that the meaning of words and concepts is determined by their use in language games.

In the context of time, Wittgenstein's view is that our understanding of time is shaped by the language we use to describe it. He argued that there is no single, objective way to describe time; rather, our concepts of time are contingent on the language we employ. This perspective has implications for how we understand the nature of time and its relationship with other philosophical concepts.
