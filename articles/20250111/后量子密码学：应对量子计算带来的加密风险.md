### Background and Problem Statement

#### The Evolution of Cryptography

##### The Origin and Basic Concepts of Cryptography

Cryptography has been a fundamental tool in securing communication since ancient times. The term "cryptography" originates from the Greek words "kryptos," meaning hidden, and "graphia," meaning writing. The basic concept of cryptography involves converting ordinary plain text into unintelligible cyphertext and vice versa.

##### Classical Cryptographic Algorithms and Their Vulnerabilities

Historically, cryptography has evolved through various stages. Classical cryptographic algorithms include substitution ciphers, transposition ciphers, and polyalphabetic ciphers. These methods relied on the secrecy of the cipher's details and were secure against the computational power available at the time.

However, with the advent of computers and modern cryptanalysis techniques, many classical algorithms have been found to be vulnerable. For instance, the Caesar cipher, one of the simplest substitution ciphers, can be broken within seconds using modern computing power.

##### Introduction to Quantum Computing and Its Potential Impact on Cryptography

Quantum computing represents a paradigm shift in computational capabilities. Unlike classical computers that use bits to process information, quantum computers use quantum bits, or qubits, which can exist in multiple states simultaneously due to the property of superposition. This allows quantum computers to perform certain types of calculations exponentially faster than classical computers.

The potential impact of quantum computing on cryptography is profound. Quantum computers have the capability to break many currently used cryptographic algorithms, such as RSA and elliptic curve cryptography (ECC), which rely on the difficulty of factoring large numbers and computing logarithms, respectively.

#### Cryptographic Risks in the Era of Quantum Computing

##### The Threat of Quantum Computers to Current Cryptographic Systems

The advent of quantum computing poses a significant threat to the security of current cryptographic systems. Quantum computers can efficiently solve problems that are intractable for classical computers, such as integer factorization and discrete logarithm problems, which are the foundation of many widely used public-key cryptographic systems.

##### Overview of Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC)

To mitigate these risks, the field of Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) has emerged. PQC focuses on developing new cryptographic algorithms that are believed to be secure against attacks by quantum computers. These algorithms include lattice-based cryptography, hash-based cryptography, and multivariate polynomial cryptography, among others.

### Core Concepts and Principles of Post-Quantum Cryptography

#### Definition and Classification of PQC

Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) is a branch of cryptography that aims to provide security against quantum computer attacks. Unlike classical cryptography, which focuses on protecting against classical computers, PQC algorithms are designed to withstand attacks from both classical and quantum computers.

##### Overview of PQC Methods

PQC encompasses a wide range of cryptographic methods, each with its unique principles and mechanisms. The most common PQC methods include lattice-based cryptography, hash-based cryptography, multivariate polynomial cryptography, and code-based cryptography.

##### Classification of PQC Algorithms

PQC algorithms can be classified based on their underlying mathematical structures. Lattice-based cryptography relies on the hardness of lattice problems, such as the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) and the Closest Vector Problem (CVP). Hash-based cryptography uses cryptographic hash functions to build secure algorithms. Multivariate polynomial cryptography involves multivariate polynomials, and code-based cryptography uses error-correcting codes.

##### Key Characteristics of PQC Algorithms

PQC algorithms share several key characteristics, such as resistance to quantum attacks, efficiency in both software and hardware implementations, and compatibility with existing cryptographic infrastructure. These characteristics make PQC algorithms well-suited for deployment in a post-quantum world.

#### Mathematical Foundations of PQC

##### Lattice-based Cryptography

Lattice-based cryptography is one of the most promising PQC methods. It leverages the difficulty of solving problems related to lattices, such as the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) and the Closest Vector Problem (CVP). These problems are believed to be hard for both classical and quantum computers, making lattice-based cryptography a robust choice for post-quantum encryption.

##### Hash-based Cryptography

Hash-based cryptography utilizes cryptographic hash functions to build secure algorithms. The security of hash-based cryptography lies in the properties of the hash functions, such as collision resistance and preimage resistance. These properties ensure that it is computationally infeasible to find two distinct inputs that produce the same hash output or to find a specific input that produces a given hash output.

##### Multivariate Polynomial Cryptography

Multivariate polynomial cryptography involves multivariate polynomials, which are polynomials with multiple variables. The security of multivariate polynomial cryptography is based on the difficulty of solving systems of multivariate polynomial equations. This method has been shown to be resistant to both classical and quantum attacks, making it a promising candidate for post-quantum cryptography.

##### Other PQC Methods

In addition to the above methods, there are other promising PQC methods, such as code-based cryptography and isogeny-based cryptography. Code-based cryptography uses error-correcting codes to build secure algorithms, while isogeny-based cryptography leverages the properties of elliptic curves and isogenies to create secure cryptographic systems.

One of the critical factors in evaluating PQC algorithms is their performance and efficiency. This includes the speed of encryption and decryption operations, the size of keys, and the memory usage. Another crucial factor is the security and resistance to attacks. PQC algorithms must be designed to be secure against both classical and quantum attacks. ### PQC Algorithms and Architectural Designs

#### Lattice-based Cryptography

##### Introduction to Lattice-based Cryptography

Lattice-based cryptography is a family of cryptographic algorithms that rely on the hardness of lattice problems, such as the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) and the Closest Vector Problem (CVP). These problems are considered hard for both classical and quantum computers, making lattice-based cryptography a robust choice for post-quantum encryption.

##### Key PQC Algorithms in Lattice-based Cryptography

Several key PQC algorithms belong to the lattice-based category. Some of the prominent ones include:

- **NTRU Cryptography**: NTRU is a public-key cryptosystem that leverages the hardness of the NTRU problem, which is a variant of the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP).
- **Lattice-Based Encryption Schemes**: Examples include the Learning With Errors (LWE) and the Ring Learning With Errors (RLWE) problems, which are used to construct secure encryption schemes.
- **Lattice-Based Signature Schemes**: Algorithms such as the NTRU Signature Scheme (NTRUSIG) and the Learning With Errors Signature Scheme (LWES) provide secure digital signatures based on lattice problems.

##### Lattice-based Cryptography Architectures

The architectural design of lattice-based cryptography involves several components, including key generation, encryption, decryption, and signature generation. The architecture must be efficient and secure, ensuring that the algorithms can be implemented in both software and hardware environments.

#### Hash-based Cryptography

##### Basic Concepts of Hash Functions

Hash-based cryptography relies on cryptographic hash functions, which are mathematical functions that take an input (or 'message') and return a fixed-size string of bytes. The primary properties of cryptographic hash functions are:

- **Collision Resistance**: It should be computationally infeasible to find two distinct inputs that produce the same hash output.
- **Preimage Resistance**: Given a hash output, it should be computationally infeasible to find an input that produces that hash output.
- **Second Preimage Resistance**: Given a specific input and its hash output, it should be computationally infeasible to find another input that produces the same hash output.

##### Hash-based Cryptographic Algorithms

Hash-based cryptographic algorithms use these properties to construct secure cryptographic systems. Some prominent hash-based algorithms include:

- **Hash-Based Cryptographic Message Syntax (HMAC)**: HMAC is a construct built on top of a hash function, providing integrity and authenticity for messages.
- **Hash-based Key Derivation Functions (HKDF)**: HKDF is used to derive keys and other values from a secure hash function.
- **Hash-based One-Time Passwords (OTPs)**: Algorithms such as HOTP and TOTP use hash functions to generate one-time passwords for authentication.

##### Architectural Design of Hash-based Cryptography

The architectural design of hash-based cryptography typically involves hashing algorithms, key generation, and key management components. The architecture must be flexible enough to accommodate different hash functions and key derivation methods, ensuring adaptability to future developments in the field.

#### Multivariate Polynomial Cryptography

##### Background of Multivariate Polynomial Cryptography

Multivariate polynomial cryptography (MPC) is a cryptographic technique that involves multivariate polynomials, which are polynomials with multiple variables. The security of MPC is based on the difficulty of solving systems of multivariate polynomial equations over finite fields.

##### Key PQC Algorithms in Multivariate Polynomial Cryptography

Several PQC algorithms fall under the MPC category. Some notable examples include:

- **Multivariate Public Key Cryptosystem (MPKC)**: MPKC algorithms, such as HFE and SHELON, use multivariate polynomial equations to construct secure public-key cryptosystems.
- **Multivariate Signature Schemes**: Algorithms like HEMA and SFLASH provide secure digital signatures based on multivariate polynomial techniques.

##### Architectural Design of Multivariate Polynomial Cryptography

The architectural design of MPC involves key generation, encryption, decryption, and signature generation components. The architecture must be efficient and secure, ensuring that the algorithms can be implemented in various environments, from software to hardware.

#### Other PQC Methods

##### Introduction to Other PQC Methods

In addition to lattice-based, hash-based, and multivariate polynomial cryptography, there are other promising PQC methods. Some of these include:

- **Code-based Cryptography**: Code-based cryptography uses error-correcting codes to build secure cryptographic systems. The most well-known code-based cryptosystem is the McEliece cryptosystem.
- **Isogeny-based Cryptography**: Isogeny-based cryptography leverages the properties of elliptic curves and isogenies to create secure cryptographic systems. The most prominent example is the Supersingular Isogeny Key Encapsulation (SIKE) protocol.
- **Hash Function-based Cryptography**: This category includes algorithms like BLAKE2 and SHA-3, which are designed to be resistant to quantum attacks.

##### Overview of Key PQC Algorithms in Other Methods

Each of these PQC methods has its unique characteristics and algorithms. For example, code-based cryptography relies on the hardness of decoding error-correcting codes, while isogeny-based cryptography leverages the properties of elliptic curves and isogenies.

##### Architectural Design of Other PQC Methods

The architectural designs of these methods involve various components, including key generation, encryption, decryption, and signature generation. The architectures must be efficient, secure, and adaptable to different environments.

### Appendix

#### A. Glossary of Terms

- **Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC):** A branch of cryptography that focuses on developing cryptographic algorithms that are secure against both classical and quantum computer attacks.
- **Quantum Computing:** A type of computing that leverages the principles of quantum mechanics to perform operations much faster than classical computers.
- **Cryptographic Algorithm:** A set of rules for performing cryptographic operations, such as encryption and decryption, to secure information.
- **Hash Function:** A function that takes an input (or 'message') and returns a fixed-size string of bytes, often used in cryptography for data integrity and digital signatures.
- **Lattice-based Cryptography:** A family of cryptographic algorithms that rely on the hardness of problems related to lattices, such as the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) and the Closest Vector Problem (CVP).
- **Multivariate Polynomial Cryptography:** A cryptographic technique that involves multivariate polynomials, which are polynomials with multiple variables.
- **Error-correcting Codes:** Codes that can detect and correct errors that may occur during the transmission or storage of data.
- **Key Generation:** The process of creating a pair of public and private keys for cryptographic operations.
- **Encryption:** The process of converting plaintext into ciphertext using a cryptographic algorithm and a key.
- **Decryption:** The process of converting ciphertext back into plaintext using a cryptographic algorithm and a key.
