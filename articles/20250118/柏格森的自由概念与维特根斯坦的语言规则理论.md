### 1.1 Background and Problem Statement

The philosophical discourse on freedom has been a cornerstone of human thought throughout history. The concept of freedom, in its various forms and interpretations, has intrigued philosophers, scientists, and laypeople alike. However, the quest for understanding freedom has often led to profound questions and debates. What exactly is freedom? Is it a natural right, a product of society, or a condition of the human mind? These questions are not merely academic; they have real-world implications, affecting our understanding of individual rights, social structures, and political systems.

In the realm of philosophy, freedom has been examined from multiple perspectives. From the ancient Greeks, who considered freedom as an intrinsic aspect of the human condition, to modern philosophers, who explore the complexities of free will and determinism, the concept has evolved significantly. Immanuel Kant, for instance, viewed freedom as a central tenet of moral philosophy, emphasizing that autonomous agents make choices based on rational principles. Conversely, Friedrich Nietzsche challenged the notion of traditional morality, asserting that the will to power is the ultimate driving force of human freedom.

The historical and philosophical context of freedom is particularly relevant when considering the works of Henri Bergson and Ludwig Wittgenstein. Both philosophers made significant contributions to our understanding of freedom, albeit through different lenses. Bergson, with his concept of élan vital, proposed that freedom is an inherent aspect of life, a force that drives creativity and progress. On the other hand, Wittgenstein, in his examination of language rules, argued that the essence of freedom is deeply intertwined with the structures of language and social norms.

The problem at the heart of this discussion is the tension between individual freedom and external constraints. How can one be truly free when subjected to societal rules, natural laws, and personal limitations? Bergson and Wittgenstein offer contrasting perspectives on this dilemma. For Bergson, freedom is a creative force that arises from the inner vitality of life, a concept that challenges traditional notions of determinism. For Wittgenstein, freedom is a social construct, shaped by the rules of language and behavior that we internalize.

This article aims to explore these ideas by examining the core concepts and theories of Bergson and Wittgenstein, comparing their views on freedom, and analyzing the implications of their work for contemporary society. By delving into the intricacies of their philosophies, we hope to gain a deeper understanding of the nature of freedom and its role in shaping human experience.

### 1.2 Henri Bergson's Contribution to the Concept of Freedom

Henri Bergson's contribution to the concept of freedom is profound and multifaceted. At the core of his philosophical thought lies the idea of élan vital, or vital force, a concept that he believed to be the driving force behind all life and consciousness. For Bergson, élan vital is not merely a physiological force but a fundamental principle that permeates all aspects of existence, from the microscopic to the macroscopic. It is this dynamic, ever-changing force that differentiates living beings from mechanical objects and enables the creative evolution of life.

Bergson's élan vital has significant implications for the concept of freedom. He argued that freedom is an intrinsic aspect of this vital force, inherent in the very nature of life itself. In his seminal work, "L'Évolution créatrice" (Creative Evolution), Bergson posits that freedom is not a metaphysical attribute but a biological necessity. Life, he asserts, is characterized by a continuous process of creation and transformation, a dynamic interplay of forces that continually redefines itself. This process of self-creation is, in essence, an expression of freedom.

One of the key ideas that Bergson introduces is the notion of "élan psychique," or mental élan, which he sees as a manifestation of the vital force in the human mind. For Bergson, the mind is not a static entity but a dynamic force constantly in flux. This mental élan, he argues, is the source of human creativity and innovation. It is this creativity that allows humans to transcend the constraints of their environment and circumstances, to act in unforeseen and unpredictable ways.

In his philosophical system, Bergson distinguishes between two types of freedom: instinctual freedom and intellectual freedom. Instinctual freedom is the freedom of the body, the capacity of living organisms to move and adapt to their environment. Intellectual freedom, on the other hand, is the freedom of the mind, the capacity for self-determination and creative thought. For Bergson, both forms of freedom are essential for human flourishing. Instinctual freedom enables humans to survive and adapt, while intellectual freedom allows them to create and innovate, to shape their own destiny.

One of the most significant contributions of Bergson's thought to the concept of freedom is his challenge to traditional notions of determinism. He argued that while external factors such as physical laws and societal norms can constrain human behavior, they do not eliminate the possibility of freedom. For Bergson, freedom is not the absence of constraints but the ability to act creatively in the face of constraints. This view has profound implications for our understanding of human agency and the nature of reality.

In conclusion, Henri Bergson's concept of freedom is rooted in his theory of élan vital. He posits that freedom is an intrinsic aspect of life, a force that drives creativity and innovation. Through his ideas about mental élan and the distinction between instinctual and intellectual freedom, Bergson challenges traditional notions of determinism and offers a vision of human flourishing that is deeply grounded in the dynamic nature of life. His work continues to inspire philosophers, scientists, and thinkers who seek to understand the nature of freedom and its role in shaping human experience.

### 1.3 Ludwig Wittgenstein's Approach to Language Rules

Ludwig Wittgenstein's exploration of language rules is central to his broader philosophical project, which aimed to elucidate the nature of thought and reality. His work, particularly the "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus" and his later "Philosophical Investigations," provides a profound analysis of the relationship between language, meaning, and human experience. In these works, Wittgenstein examines how language functions as a system of rules that shapes our understanding of the world and our place within it.

In the early phase of his career, Wittgenstein's approach to language was heavily influenced by the logical positivism of the Vienna Circle. The "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus," published in 1921, presents a radical new way of thinking about language and reality. For Wittgenstein, language is a system of signs that corresponds to reality in a straightforward, mechanical way. In this view, words are tied to objects and propositions are either true or false. The world, according to Wittgenstein, is a collection of objects, and the function of language is to represent these objects.

Wittgenstein's concept of language rules is central to his theory of meaning. He argued that the meaning of a word is determined by its use in language, not by an underlying abstract concept or definition. This idea is encapsulated in his famous quote, "The meaning of a word is its use in the language." For Wittgenstein, language is not a mere representation of reality but a form of life. The rules that govern language are not arbitrary but are deeply intertwined with the social practices and cultural norms of a community.

In the "Tractatus," Wittgenstein introduces the concept of "picture theory," which suggests that language functions like a camera, capturing aspects of reality. He posited that a proposition is true if it corresponds to reality in the same way a picture corresponds to a scene. This theory emphasizes the role of logical structure in determining the truth of statements. However, Wittgenstein's later work, the "Philosophical Investigations," critically examines and expands upon these early ideas.

In the "Philosophical Investigations," published in 1953, Wittgenstein adopts a more pragmatic approach to language and meaning. He criticizes the idea that language can be analyzed in terms of a fixed set of rules. Instead, he emphasizes the variability and flexibility of language use. For Wittgenstein, language is not a static system but a dynamic one that evolves in response to changing circumstances and new discoveries. He introduces the concept of "language games" to illustrate this point. A language game is a specific way in which language is used within a particular context or community. Different language games serve different purposes and have different rules.

Wittgenstein's later work also focuses on the concept of rule-following. He argued that understanding a rule involves more than simply memorizing a set of instructions. Rule-following is a social practice that requires a shared understanding of the rules and the purposes they serve. Wittgenstein's famous example of the "doubting chess player" illustrates this point. The player doubts the rule that dictates whether a move is legal, leading to a philosophical inquiry into the nature of rules and their interpretation.

In conclusion, Ludwig Wittgenstein's approach to language rules provides a deep and nuanced understanding of the role of language in human thought and behavior. His early work, the "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus," introduces the concept of picture theory and emphasizes the role of logical structure in determining meaning. His later work, the "Philosophical Investigations," expands on these ideas, focusing on the variability and flexibility of language use and the social practices that govern it. Wittgenstein's insights into language and meaning continue to shape contemporary philosophy and our understanding of human communication.

### 2.2.1 The Early Wittgenstein and Language Rules

In the early part of his career, Ludwig Wittgenstein's focus on language rules was largely influenced by the logical positivism of the Vienna Circle. This period is characterized by his work in the "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus," which presents a foundational analysis of language and reality. Wittgenstein's early theory of language rules revolves around the idea that language is a system of symbols that mirrors reality in a direct, unambiguous manner.

The "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus," published in 1921, is a work that aims to clarify the relationship between language, thought, and reality. Wittgenstein introduces the concept of "picture theory," which posits that propositions in language function like photographs that represent aspects of the world. According to this theory, a proposition is true if it corresponds accurately to reality, much like a photograph that matches a scene. This theory emphasizes the role of logical structure in determining the truth of statements and underlines the importance of language as a means of representation.

At the heart of Wittgenstein's early theory of language rules is the notion that meaning is derived from the way words are used in language. He famously states, "The meaning of a word is its use in the language." This idea challenges traditional notions of meaning as being based on abstract definitions or underlying concepts. Instead, Wittgenstein argues that meaning is context-dependent and arises from the specific ways in which words are employed in communication.

Wittgenstein's "Tractatus" is also known for its emphasis on the distinction between the logical and the factual. He maintains that the logical structure of language is concerned with the relations between objects, while the factual content of language deals with the truth or falsehood of statements about the world. This separation is crucial for understanding the role of language in representing reality accurately.

The early Wittgenstein's theory of language rules is heavily influenced by his logical positivist colleagues, such as Moritz Schlick and Otto Neurath. He shares their belief in the possibility of a scientific worldview that is based on empirical observation and logical reasoning. This philosophical stance is reflected in his use of logical symbols and his focus on the clarity and precision of language.

In summary, the early Wittgenstein's approach to language rules is rooted in his "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus," where he introduces the concept of picture theory and emphasizes the importance of meaning derived from language use. His early work lays the foundation for his later philosophical investigations, which expand on these ideas and offer a more nuanced understanding of language and meaning.

### 2.2.2 The Later Wittgenstein and Language Rules

In his later works, Ludwig Wittgenstein takes a markedly different approach to the concept of language rules, moving away from the logical positivism of his early career. The "Philosophical Investigations," published posthumously in 1953, represents a significant shift in his philosophical perspective. In this later period, Wittgenstein focuses on the pragmatic and social aspects of language, emphasizing that language is not a static or fixed system but a dynamic and flexible set of practices.

One of the central ideas in the "Philosophical Investigations" is the concept of "language games." Wittgenstein uses this metaphor to illustrate the various ways in which language is used in different contexts. A language game is a specific activity or form of communication within which language rules are established and followed. These games can range from simple activities like playing chess to complex social practices like conducting scientific research. The key insight here is that the rules of language are not universal but are context-specific and vary according to the purposes and contexts in which they are used.

Wittgenstein's later work also emphasizes the importance of rule-following as a social practice. He argues that understanding a rule involves more than simply memorizing a set of instructions. Rule-following is a social activity that requires a shared understanding of the rules and the purposes they serve. Wittgenstein introduces the idea of "nonsense utterances," where someone might follow the literal words of a rule without understanding its intended purpose. This phenomenon highlights the need for a pragmatic understanding of rules, which takes into account the context and intentions behind their use.

In the "Philosophical Investigations," Wittgenstein also criticizes the notion of a hidden inner meaning or essence that underlies language. He argues that language does not have an abstract or universal meaning but is instead defined by its use in specific language games. This perspective is reflected in his famous quote, "The whole thing is so simple that the moment you try to explain it you don't understand it any longer." For Wittgenstein, understanding language is not about uncovering an underlying meaning but about recognizing the specific contexts and purposes in which words are used.

Another important aspect of Wittgenstein's later philosophy is his emphasis on the importance of language in shaping our perception of the world. He suggests that our concepts and ideas are formed through our interactions with language, and that these ideas can lead to philosophical confusion and misunderstanding. Wittgenstein's later work is particularly focused on resolving these philosophical confusions by highlighting the role of language in creating false appearances and illusions.

Wittgenstein's later theory of language rules also includes the idea of family resemblance. He argues that our concepts are not defined by a set of clear-cut criteria but by a series of overlapping and related features. This idea challenges the traditional notion of a clear-cut definition and suggests that language and meaning are more fluid and nuanced than previously thought.

In conclusion, Ludwig Wittgenstein's later work on language rules represents a significant departure from his early logical positivism. His focus on language games and rule-following highlights the social and contextual nature of language. He argues that understanding language involves recognizing the specific contexts and purposes in which it is used, rather than seeking a universal or abstract meaning. This later philosophy continues to influence contemporary discussions in philosophy, linguistics, and cognitive science, providing insights into the nature of language and meaning.

In summary, while Bergson and Wittgenstein share a common concern with the dynamic and creative nature of human thought, they offer distinct perspectives on the nature of freedom and the role of language. ### Comparative Analysis

In comparing Henri Bergson's and Ludwig Wittgenstein's theories of freedom and language, several key themes emerge that highlight their similarities and differences. Both philosophers grapple with the complexities of human thought and the nature of reality, offering distinct yet complementary perspectives on the concepts of freedom and language.

One of the central themes in both theories is the role of creativity and spontaneity. For Bergson, freedom is closely tied to the concept of élan vital, which he sees as the driving force of all life and consciousness. This dynamic force enables individuals to act in spontaneous and creative ways, transcending external constraints and shaping new possibilities. Similarly, Wittgenstein's later work emphasizes the dynamic and flexible nature of thought and language. He introduces the concept of language games to illustrate how language is not a fixed system but is instead shaped by specific social practices and contexts. This emphasis on creativity and flexibility underscores the dynamic and evolving nature of human thought, reflecting a common theme in both philosophers' works.

Another theme that unites Bergson and Wittgenstein is their focus on the relationship between individual agency and social context. For Bergson, freedom is not an isolated or individual attribute but is instead deeply embedded in the social fabric. His idea of élan vital implies that individual creativity and spontaneity are influenced by the collective forces of society. Similarly, Wittgenstein's concept of language games highlights the social nature of language and thought. Language is not a private or internal process but is instead shaped by social interactions and shared conventions. This shared emphasis on the interconnectedness of individual and social dimensions highlights a fundamental similarity between their perspectives.

Despite these commonalities, there are significant differences in their approaches to freedom and language. Bergson's focus on spontaneity and creativity contrasts with Wittgenstein's emphasis on the social and contextual aspects of language. For Bergson, freedom is a creative force that arises from within, driven by the dynamic force of élan vital. In contrast, Wittgenstein views freedom as a complex social practice, shaped by the rules and conventions of language games. This difference in perspective reflects their distinct philosophical frameworks and the ways in which they conceptualize human thought and behavior.

Another area of contrast is their understanding of the relationship between language and reality. For Bergson, consciousness is an active process of creation and interpretation, shaped by the dynamic force of élan vital. This view suggests that reality is not a fixed or objective entity but is instead continually constructed through the creative acts of consciousness. In contrast, Wittgenstein's early work, the "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus," presents a more rigid and formalistic view of language and reality. He argues that language corresponds to reality in a direct and unambiguous way, much like a photograph captures a scene. This view contrasts with Bergson's more nuanced understanding of the relationship between consciousness and reality.

These differences highlight the richness and complexity of their respective theories, offering valuable insights into the nature of freedom and language. While Bergson's emphasis on spontaneity and creativity provides a dynamic and open view of human potential, Wittgenstein's focus on the social and contextual aspects of language offers a grounded and pragmatic perspective on human thought and behavior. By comparing their theories, we can gain a deeper understanding of the complexities of freedom and language, recognizing the diverse ways in which these concepts can be conceptualized and applied.

In conclusion, the comparative analysis of Bergson's and Wittgenstein's theories of freedom and language reveals both common themes and significant differences. Their shared emphasis on creativity, spontaneity, and the social dimensions of human thought offers a rich foundation for understanding the nature of freedom and language. At the same time, their distinct perspectives highlight the complexity and richness of philosophical inquiry, encouraging us to explore the diverse ways in which these concepts can be understood and applied.
