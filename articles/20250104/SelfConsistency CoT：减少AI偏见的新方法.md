## 1.1. Background of AI Bias

### 1.1.1 AI Bias: Definition and Impact

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has become an integral part of modern technology, permeating various sectors such as healthcare, finance, and transportation. However, the increasing reliance on AI systems has unveiled a concerning issue—AI bias. AI bias refers to the tendency of AI models to exhibit unfair or discriminatory behaviors due to biased training data or flawed algorithms. This bias can lead to several adverse consequences, including unjust treatment of individuals or groups, false predictions, and reinforced stereotypes.

The impact of AI bias is multifaceted and profound. Firstly, it undermines the fairness and transparency of AI systems, which are crucial for their acceptance and trust by users. Biased AI models can perpetuate existing societal biases and discrimination, leading to increased inequality and social injustice. For example, in the case of hiring algorithms, biased models may systematically exclude certain demographic groups from job opportunities, thereby exacerbating existing disparities.

Secondly, AI bias can have significant economic implications. In industries such as finance and insurance, biased models can result in unfair pricing and credit decisions, leading to financial exclusion and economic disadvantage for certain groups. Additionally, in autonomous vehicles, AI bias can result in accidents and fatalities, as demonstrated by the tragic incidents involving self-driving cars.

Finally, AI bias poses a threat to the overall reliability and credibility of AI technologies. As more critical decisions, such as medical diagnosis and criminal justice, rely on AI systems, the presence of bias can lead to errors and misjudgments, compromising the trust in these technologies.

### 1.1.2 Current Solutions and Limitations

To address AI bias, researchers and practitioners have explored various approaches. One common method is the use of fairness metrics, which evaluate the distribution of outcomes across different demographic groups. Techniques such as re-sampling, re-weighting, and re-scoring have been applied to adjust the impact of bias in training data. Additionally, algorithmic transparency and explainability have gained prominence, aiming to uncover the underlying causes of bias and make AI systems more understandable to users.

However, these current solutions have their limitations. Fairness metrics often focus on statistical parity but may fail to capture the complexities of real-world biases. Moreover, adjusting training data using re-sampling or re-weighting can lead to data sparsity or information loss, affecting the performance of AI models. Algorithmic transparency and explainability, while valuable, may not provide sufficient insight into the root causes of bias, especially in deep learning models with complex architectures.

Furthermore, existing methods often rely on post-hoc adjustments, treating bias as an afterthought rather than an inherent issue in the design and development of AI systems. This reactive approach may not be sufficient to eliminate AI bias comprehensively and proactively.

### 2.2.2 Formulation of the Self-Consistency CoT Algorithm

The Self-Consistency CoT algorithm can be formulated as follows:

Let \( D \) be the dataset containing input-output pairs \((x_i, y_i)\), where \( x_i \) represents the input features and \( y_i \) represents the corresponding output labels. The objective is to train an AI model that generates predictions \( \hat{y} \) that are self-consistent and aligned with empirical evidence.

### 2.2.3 Key Mathematical Concepts

1. **Empirical Loss Function:**
   The empirical loss function measures the discrepancy between the model's predictions and the actual labels. A common choice for the empirical loss function is the mean squared error (MSE):

   $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{empirical}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 $$

   where \( n \) is the number of data points in the dataset.

2. **Consistency Measure:**
   The consistency measure evaluates how well the model's predictions align with the logical rules and empirical evidence. This can be formulated as:

   $$ \mathcal{C} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta(y_i, \hat{y}_i) $$

   where \( \delta(y_i, \hat{y}_i) \) is a binary indicator that equals 1 if the model's prediction \( \hat{y}_i \) is consistent with the logical rule and empirical evidence, and 0 otherwise.

3. **Total Loss Function:**
   The total loss function combines the empirical loss and the consistency measure to optimize the model's performance:

   $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{empirical}} + \lambda \mathcal{C} $$

   where \( \lambda \) is a hyperparameter that balances the trade-off between empirical accuracy and consistency.

### 2.2.4 Mathematical Formulation

The objective of the Self-Consistency CoT algorithm is to minimize the total loss function:

$$ \min_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \min_{\theta} \left( \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \lambda \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta(y_i, \hat{y}_i) \right) $$

where \( \theta \) represents the model's parameters.
