## 1. Background Introduction

### 1.1 Problem Background

The history of thermodynamics is deeply intertwined with the quest to understand the behavior of energy and matter in various systems. Classical thermodynamics, which emerged in the early 19th century, laid the foundation for our understanding of energy conservation and transformation. However, as our scientific knowledge advanced, it became apparent that classical thermodynamics had limitations, particularly when dealing with non-equilibrium states.

Non-equilibrium thermodynamics is concerned with the study of systems that are not in thermal or chemical equilibrium. These systems are subject to various types of external influences, such as temperature gradients, chemical gradients, and mechanical forces. The study of non-equilibrium thermodynamics has become increasingly important in the context of modern science and technology, as many real-world systems operate far from equilibrium.

### 1.2 Problem Definition

In classical thermodynamics, a system is considered to be in equilibrium when its properties, such as temperature, pressure, and chemical composition, do not change with time. Non-equilibrium thermodynamics, on the other hand, deals with systems that are not in this state. These systems exhibit dynamic behavior, with properties changing over time in response to external influences.

Dissipative structure theory is a branch of non-equilibrium thermodynamics that seeks to understand the emergence of complex, structured systems from simpler, disordered ones. Dissipative structures are coherent, stable entities that arise from the flow of energy and matter in non-equilibrium systems. They are characterized by a minimum of entropy production, a maximum of order, and a sensitivity to initial conditions and boundary conditions.

### 3. Fundamental Principles of Dissipative Structure Theory

#### 3.1 Principles and Theories

The development of dissipative structure theory is closely associated with the pioneering work of Ilya Prigogine, who received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1977 for his contributions to this field. Prigogine's research focused on understanding the emergence of order and structure in non-equilibrium systems. He introduced the concept of dissipative structures, which are coherent and stable entities that arise from the flow of energy and matter in non-equilibrium environments.

Dissipative structure theory is built upon several fundamental principles:

- **Non-Equilibrium Steady States**: Unlike systems in thermal or chemical equilibrium, non-equilibrium steady states (NESS) are characterized by a constant, time-invariant set of properties, despite being subject to external influences. These states are crucial for understanding the long-term behavior of complex systems.

- **Dissipation and Entropy Production**: Dissipation is the irreversible loss of energy in a system, often due to friction or other dissipative processes. This dissipation leads to an increase in entropy production, which is a measure of the disorder or randomness in the system. However, dissipative structures are characterized by a minimum entropy production, which allows them to maintain their integrity over time.

- **Self-Organization**: Self-organization refers to the process by which complex, structured systems emerge from simpler, disordered components without external control. Dissipative structures are a prime example of self-organized systems, as they form spontaneously in non-equilibrium environments.

- **Sensitivity to Initial and Boundary Conditions**: Dissipative structures exhibit a high degree of sensitivity to their initial conditions and the conditions of their surroundings. This sensitivity can lead to significant differences in the behavior and properties of systems, even when they are governed by the same fundamental laws of physics.

#### 3.2 Dissipation and Entropy

Dissipation and entropy production are central to dissipative structure theory. Dissipation refers to the irreversible loss of energy in a system, which often manifests as heat or other forms of energy that cannot be converted back into useful work. This loss of energy leads to an increase in entropy, which is a measure of the disorder or randomness in a system.

In classical thermodynamics, the second law states that the total entropy of an isolated system can never decrease over time. However, in non-equilibrium systems, entropy can be produced or consumed depending on the nature of the processes at play. Dissipative structures are characterized by a minimum entropy production rate, which means that they are able to maintain a steady state while minimizing the increase in disorder.

The relationship between dissipation and entropy production can be understood through the concept of the dissipation function, which is a measure of the energy lost per unit time due to dissipative processes. In a dissipative structure, the dissipation function is minimized, leading to a balance between energy input and output that allows the system to maintain its coherent and stable state.
