#### Traditional Theories of Interpersonal Interaction

**Social Exchange Theory**

Social Exchange Theory, proposed by Thibaut and Kelly in the 1950s, views interpersonal relationships as a form of exchange between individuals. According to this theory, people evaluate their relationships based on the costs and benefits they receive. The balance between these factors determines the quality and stability of the relationship. The mathematical model of social exchange theory can be represented using utility functions that quantify the benefits and costs of interactions. For example, a utility function can be defined as:

$$
U(x, y) = bx - cy
$$

where \( x \) represents the benefits received from the interaction, \( y \) represents the costs incurred, and \( b \) and \( c \) are constants representing the individual's valuations of benefits and costs.

**Social Penetration Theory**

Social Penetration Theory, developed by Altman and Taylor in the 1970s, emphasizes the process of gradual and systematic disclosure of personal information as a key factor in relationship development. This theory suggests that as individuals share more personal information, they develop deeper emotional connections. Mathematical models of social penetration can be constructed using curve functions that represent the depth of information shared over time. For instance, a sigmoid function can be used to model the gradual increase in disclosure:

$$

$$

where \( t \) represents time and \( k \) is a constant that controls the rate of information exchange.

**Interpersonal Attraction Theory**

Interpersonal Attraction Theory, formulated by Byram Karasu in the 1960s, focuses on the cognitive and emotional processes that underlie the development of attraction between individuals. According to this theory, attraction is influenced by factors such as physical attractiveness, similarity, and complementary traits. Mathematical models of interpersonal attraction can be based on optimization principles, where individuals choose partners that maximize their overall utility. For example, a utility function for selecting a partner can be defined as:

$$
U(A, B) = \alpha A + \beta B - \gamma |A - B|
$$

where \( A \) and \( B \) represent the traits of the individuals, and \( \alpha \), \( \beta \), and \( \gamma \) are constants representing the individual's preferences for specific traits.

#### Modern Theories of Interpersonal Interaction

**Social Cognitive Theory**

Social Cognitive Theory, developed by Albert Bandura in the 1970s, emphasizes the role of cognitive processes in the development and regulation of interpersonal behavior. This theory includes concepts such as self-efficacy, observational learning, and self-regulation. Mathematical models of social cognitive theory can incorporate variables representing cognitive processes and their interactions.
