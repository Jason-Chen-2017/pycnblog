### The Philosophical Background of Bergson and Wittgenstein

#### Henri Bergson: A Brief Biography

Henri Bergson (1859-1941) was a French philosopher, biologist, and social theorist whose work has had a significant impact on various fields, including the philosophy of science, psychology, and literary theory. Born in Paris, Bergson was educated at the Lycée Henri-IV and the École Normale Supérieure, where he pursued studies in both science and philosophy. His pioneering work in biology, particularly his studies on the physiology of the brain and the theory of evolution, laid the foundation for his later philosophical ideas.

Bergson's major philosophical work, "La Pensée et le Mouvement," published in 1889, introduced his theory of duration and the concept of the élan vital, or vital force. This theory posits that life is a continuous process of growth and change, characterized by an innate creative force that opposes the mechanistic view of nature espoused by physical science. Bergson's ideas were influential in the development of the philosophy of science, challenging the dominant paradigm of empiricism and advocating for a more dynamic understanding of reality.

#### Ludwig Wittgenstein: A Brief Biography

Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889-1951) was an Austrian-British philosopher who is widely regarded as one of the most influential thinkers of the 20th century. Born into a wealthy family in Vienna, Wittgenstein initially pursued a career in engineering and mathematics before turning to philosophy. His early work, "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus," published in 1921, presents a rigorous logical analysis of language and reality, asserting that all meaningful statements are tautologies.

Wittgenstein's later work, particularly his essays "Philosophical Investigations," published posthumously in 1953, represents a departure from the early logical positivism of the Tractatus. In "Philosophical Investigations," Wittgenstein introduces the concept of "language games" to describe the various ways in which language is used in different contexts. This perspective emphasizes the importance of understanding language in its social and practical dimensions, rather than attempting to reduce it to a set of formal rules.

#### Commonalities and Differences

While Bergson and Wittgenstein come from different intellectual backgrounds and address different aspects of science, their work shares several common themes. One key difference between their philosophies lies in their views on the nature of time. This emphasis on the fluidity and diversity of language highlights a fundamental contrast between their perspectives on the nature of reality and the role of science in understanding it.

One of the earliest philosophical inquiries into science can be traced back to the Ancient Greeks, particularly to figures like Aristotle, who sought to understand the natural world through empirical observation and logical reasoning. In the Renaissance, Francis Bacon and René Descartes further developed the scientific method, emphasizing the importance of empirical evidence and rational inquiry.

The 19th century saw the rise of positivism, a philosophical movement championed by figures like Auguste Comte, which asserted that only empirical data could provide reliable knowledge. This view dominated scientific thought for much of the 20th century, leading to the development of logical positivism and the Vienna Circle, which sought to apply rigorous logical analysis to scientific theories.

This view is encapsulated in his famous quote: "Time is nature's way of keeping everything from happening at once." By emphasizing the fluidity and creativity of nature, Bergson's philosophy offers a more dynamic and nuanced understanding of the natural world.

#### 1.3 The Works of Ludwig Wittgenstein

Ludwig Wittgenstein's philosophy of science is closely tied to his work on language and logic. In his later work, "Philosophical Investigations" (1953), Wittgenstein shifts his focus to the concept of "language games," which he uses to describe the various ways in which language is used in different contexts. Wittgenstein's later philosophy has had a significant impact on the philosophy of science, particularly in the areas of scientific methodology and the nature of scientific theories.

They also emphasize the limitations of language and the importance of understanding the context in which it is used.

One key difference between their perspectives is their views on the nature of time. Bergson sees time as a continuous flow, inherently linked to the creative force of life, while Wittgenstein's later philosophy focuses on the concept of "linguistic games" and the diverse ways in which language is employed in different contexts. ### Chapter 2: Henri Bergson's Scientific Philosophy

#### 2.1 The Biological Background and Philosophical Thoughts of Bergson

Henri Bergson's scientific philosophy is deeply rooted in his biological research and his observations of the natural world. Born in 1859, Bergson was educated at the Lycée Henri-IV and the École Normale Supérieure, where he pursued studies in both science and philosophy. His interest in biology led him to conduct groundbreaking research on the physiology of the brain and the theory of evolution.

Bergson's major philosophical work, "La Pensée et le Mouvement," published in 1889, is a cornerstone of his thought. In this work, he introduces the concept of the élan vital, or vital force, which he posits as the fundamental driving force of life. This idea is derived from his biological research and represents a fundamental challenge to the mechanistic view of nature that dominated scientific thought at the time.

Bergson's belief in the élan vital is based on the idea that life is a continuous process of growth and change, driven by an innate creative force. He argues that this force is not directly observable through empirical methods but can be inferred from the patterns of growth and development observed in living organisms. This perspective has had a profound impact on the philosophy of science, challenging the reductionist approach of physical science and advocating for a more dynamic understanding of reality.

#### 2.2 Critique of Empiricism

One of the key aspects of Bergson's scientific philosophy is his critique of empiricism, a philosophical movement that emphasizes the importance of empirical evidence in scientific inquiry. Empiricism holds that knowledge is derived from sensory experience and that all scientific theories must be testable through observation and experimentation.

Bergson argues that empiricism is too restrictive in its approach to science. He contends that the natural world is not solely governed by mechanical laws but is also shaped by a continuous, creative force that cannot be captured through empirical methods. This perspective is encapsulated in his famous quote: "Time is nature's way of keeping everything from happening at once." By emphasizing the fluidity and creativity of nature, Bergson's philosophy offers a more dynamic and nuanced understanding of the natural world.

Bergson's critique of empiricism is rooted in his belief that the natural world is too complex to be fully captured by empirical observations alone. He argues that in order to truly understand the natural world, we must move beyond the limitations of empirical methods and embrace a more holistic approach that takes into account the inherent creativity and complexity of life.

#### 2.3 Bergson's View of Time and Science

Bergson's philosophy is deeply intertwined with his views on the nature of time. He posits that time is not a linear sequence of events but rather a continuous, creative flow. This concept is known as "durée," which he contrasts with the abstract, measurable time of physical science.

Bergson argues that the linear, measurable time of physical science is inadequate for capturing the complexity and fluidity of life. He contends that life is a continuous process of growth and change, characterized by a dynamic, creative force that is not fully captured by the static, mechanical laws of physics.

This view has profound implications for the philosophy of science. By emphasizing the importance of duration over measurable time, Bergson challenges the reductionist approach of physical science and advocates for a more dynamic and holistic understanding of the natural world.

In conclusion, Henri Bergson's scientific philosophy is characterized by his belief in the élan vital, his critique of empiricism, and his views on the nature of time. These ideas have had a significant impact on the philosophy of science, offering a more dynamic and nuanced understanding of the natural world. ### Chapter 3: Ludwig Wittgenstein's Scientific Philosophy

#### 3.1 Ludwig Wittgenstein: Early Philosophy

Ludwig Wittgenstein's early philosophical work is marked by his rigorous logical analysis of language and reality. His first major work, "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus" (1921), presents a systematic and rigorous account of the relationship between language, thought, and reality. In this work, Wittgenstein asserts that all meaningful statements are tautologies, meaning that they are true by virtue of their logical structure rather than their empirical content.

The Tractatus is characterized by its logical positivism, a philosophical movement that emphasizes the importance of empirical evidence and logical analysis in scientific inquiry. Wittgenstein's early philosophy is heavily influenced by the ideas of the Vienna Circle, a group of philosophers and scientists who sought to apply logical analysis to all areas of human knowledge.

One of the key ideas in the Tractatus is the concept of "language games," which Wittgenstein uses to describe the various ways in which language is used in different contexts. He argues that the meaning of a word or phrase is determined by its use in a specific language game, which can be thought of as a rule-governed activity. This idea highlights the importance of understanding language in its social and practical dimensions, rather than attempting to reduce it to a set of formal rules.

#### 3.2 Ludwig Wittgenstein: Later Philosophy

Wittgenstein's later philosophical work, particularly his essays "Philosophical Investigations" (1953), represents a significant departure from the logical positivism of the Tractatus. In "Philosophical Investigations," Wittgenstein shifts his focus from the analysis of language to the examination of language use in everyday life. He introduces the concept of "language games" to describe the diverse ways in which language is employed in different contexts.

In the later philosophy, Wittgenstein emphasizes the importance of understanding language in its social and practical dimensions. He argues that the meaning of a word or phrase is not determined by a fixed set of rules but rather by its use in specific contexts. This perspective is known as "ordinary language philosophy" and has had a significant impact on the philosophy of language and linguistics.

One of the key ideas in the later philosophy is the concept of "family resemblance," which Wittgenstein uses to describe the relationship between words and concepts. He argues that the boundaries between concepts are not sharply defined but are rather fuzzy and overlapping. This idea challenges the idea of a fixed, objective reality and highlights the role of language in shaping our understanding of the world.

#### 3.3 Wittgenstein's Critique of Empirical Science

Wittgenstein's later philosophy is critical of the empirical methods of science, particularly the notion of objectivity and the idea that scientific theories can provide a complete and accurate description of reality. He argues that the empirical sciences are based on a series of idealizations and simplifications that make it impossible to achieve a complete and objective account of the world.

Wittgenstein contends that the empirical sciences are essentially languages games, much like the language games he describes in "Philosophical Investigations." He argues that the rules of these games are not objective but are rather subjective and context-dependent. This perspective challenges the idea of an objective, external reality and highlights the role of language and culture in shaping our understanding of the world.

In conclusion, Ludwig Wittgenstein's scientific philosophy is characterized by his early logical positivism and his later emphasis on ordinary language philosophy. His work offers a profound critique of empirical science and highlights the limitations of our ability to achieve a complete and accurate understanding of the world. By exploring his ideas, we can gain a deeper appreciation of the complexities and challenges inherent in scientific inquiry.
