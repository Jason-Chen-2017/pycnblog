### Ludwig Wittgenstein and His Language Innovations

#### 1.1. Wittgenstein's Life and Works

Ludwig Wittgenstein was born in Vienna in 1889 and died in Cambridge in 1951. He was a prominent philosopher who made significant contributions to the fields of mathematics, logic, and philosophy. His most famous works include "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus" and "Philosophical Investigations," which have had a lasting impact on the development of modern philosophy.

#### 1.2. The Early Stage of Wittgenstein's Language Theory

Wittgenstein's early work, "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus," presents a theory of language that emphasizes the relationship between language and reality. In this work, Wittgenstein argues that language is a system of symbols that represents the world, and that the meaning of a word is determined by its relationship to reality. This theory is known as logical atomism, and it has been influential in the development of analytic philosophy.

#### 1.3. The Later Stage of Wittgenstein's Language Theory

In his later work, "Philosophical Investigations," Wittgenstein criticizes the logical atomism of his early work and proposes a new theory of language that emphasizes the role of language in human activity. In this work, Wittgenstein argues that language is not a fixed system of symbols but a tool that we use to perform specific tasks. This theory is known as ordinary language philosophy, and it has had a significant impact on contemporary philosophy.

#### 1.4. Key Innovations in Wittgenstein's Language Theory

Wittgenstein's language theories have had a profound impact on the field of philosophy and linguistics. Some of the key innovations in his work include:

1. The idea that language is a tool for performing specific tasks, rather than a fixed system of symbols.
2. The concept of language games, which illustrates the different ways in which language can be used in various contexts.
3. The distinction between the language of ordinary language and the language of philosophy, emphasizing the importance of understanding the context in which language is used.

### Fundamental Concepts of Combinatory Logic

#### 2.1. Historical Background of Combinatory Logic

Combinatory logic was first introduced by Moses Schönfinkel in 1924, and later developed by Haskell Curry. Schönfinkel was interested in simplifying the foundations of mathematics, and he proposed using combinators as a way to eliminate the need for variables and quantifiers in logical expressions. Curry further developed this idea, leading to the creation of combinatory logic as a formal system of symbolic logic.

#### 2.2. Basic Notations and Principles

Combinatory logic is based on the use of combinators, which are functions that take one or more arguments and return a new function. The basic notation for combinators consists of capital letters, such as $S$, $K$, and $C$, which represent specific combinators. The principles of combinatory logic are defined by a set of axioms and rules of inference.

#### 2.3. Applications of Combinatory Logic in Computer Science

Combinatory logic has found applications in various areas of computer science, particularly in the realm of functional programming. In functional programming, combinators are used to implement functions in a more concise and efficient manner. They have been used to design efficient algorithms for various problems, such as sorting and searching, and have also been used to develop domain-specific languages for specific applications.

### The Combinator Patterns in Functional Programming (FP)

#### 3.1. Introduction to Functional Programming

Functional programming is a programming paradigm that treats computation as the evaluation of mathematical functions and avoids changing-state and mutable data. In functional programming, programs are composed of functions, which are pure and have no side effects. This paradigm has gained popularity in recent years due to its simplicity, efficiency, and scalability.

#### 3.2. The Role of Combinators in FP

In functional programming, combinators are used to implement functions in a more concise and efficient manner. They are essentially functions that take one or more functions as arguments and return a new function as a result. This allows for the composition of functions, making it easier to write complex programs by combining simple functions.

#### 3.3. Key Combinator Patterns in FP

There are several key combinators used in functional programming, including:

1. **Identity Combinator ($I$):** This combinator takes a function as an argument and returns the same function. It is often used as a placeholder in function compositions.
2. **K Combinator ($K$):** This combinator takes two functions as arguments and returns the first function, ignoring the second. It is often used to implement function application.
3. **S Combinator ($S$):** This combinator takes three functions as arguments and combines the first two functions in a specific way. It is the most commonly used combinator in functional programming and is used to implement function composition.
