### 2. Fundamental Concepts

#### 2.1 Basic principles of thermodynamics

Thermodynamics is a branch of physics that deals with the relationships between heat, work, temperature, and energy. It provides a framework for understanding and predicting the behavior of matter and energy in various physical systems. The basic principles of thermodynamics are derived from experimental observations and are expressed in the form of laws. These laws are fundamental and apply to a wide range of systems, from macroscopic objects to microscopic particles.

##### 2.1.1 Definition and history of thermodynamics

Thermodynamics can be defined as the study of the relationships between heat, work, and energy. The field has its roots in the early 19th century, with the development of the steam engine and the need to understand the efficiency of heat engines. The first significant contributions came from physicists such as Sadi Carnot, who introduced the concept of the heat engine, and Rudolf Clausius, who formulated the laws of thermodynamics. Later, James Clerk Maxwell and Ludwig Boltzmann made significant contributions to statistical thermodynamics, providing a microscopic basis for understanding macroscopic thermodynamic phenomena.

##### 2.1.2 Laws of thermodynamics

The laws of thermodynamics are fundamental principles that govern the behavior of energy in physical systems. There are four laws, but the most relevant to our discussion are the zeroth, first, and second laws.

**Zeroth law of thermodynamics**

The zeroth law states that if two systems are in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are also in thermal equilibrium with each other. This law defines the concept of temperature and establishes a basis for comparing the thermal states of different systems. It also implies that temperature is a quantitative measure of the thermal energy distribution within a system.

**First law of thermodynamics**

The first law, also known as the law of conservation of energy, states that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant. This law is a restatement of the principle of conservation of energy, which is a fundamental principle in physics. In the context of thermodynamics, it means that energy can be converted from one form to another, but the total amount of energy in a closed system remains constant.

Mathematically, the first law can be expressed as:

\[ \Delta U = Q - W \]

where \( \Delta U \) is the change in internal energy of the system, \( Q \) is the heat added to the system, and \( W \) is the work done by the system.

**Second law of thermodynamics**

The second law of thermodynamics is perhaps the most profound and challenging law in thermodynamics. It states that the entropy of an isolated system always increases over time. Entropy is a measure of the disorder or randomness of a system, and the second law implies that natural processes tend to move from a state of order to a state of disorder.

The second law can be stated in several ways, but the most common formulation is:

\[ \Delta S \geq \frac{Q}{T} \]

where \( \Delta S \) is the change in entropy, \( Q \) is the heat added to the system, and \( T \) is the temperature.

This equation indicates that the entropy of a system increases when heat is added, and the rate of increase is proportional to the temperature. In other words, the second law tells us that heat naturally flows from high-temperature regions to low-temperature regions, and this flow of heat is irreversible.

The third law of thermodynamics states that the entropy of a perfect crystal at absolute zero temperature is zero. #### 2.2 Open systems and their thermodynamics

In thermodynamics, a system can be classified as open, closed, or isolated based on its interaction with its surroundings. An open system is one that can exchange both matter and energy with its surroundings. In contrast, a closed system can only exchange energy but not matter, and an isolated system cannot exchange either matter or energy with its surroundings.

##### 2.2.1 Definition and characteristics

An open system is defined by its ability to exchange both matter and energy with its surroundings. This means that the matter and energy within an open system are not constant, and they can change over time. Open systems are common in nature and engineering, as many processes and systems interact with their environment in some way.

Some key characteristics of open systems include:

1. **Exchange of matter and energy**: Open systems can exchange both matter and energy with their surroundings. This exchange can occur through various mechanisms, such as conduction, convection, and radiation.
2. **Non-equilibrium states**: Open systems are often not in thermal or mechanical equilibrium with their surroundings. This means that their temperature, pressure, and other properties can vary spatially and temporally.
3. **Irreversible processes**: Open systems are subject to irreversible processes, such as heat transfer and friction, which lead to the generation of entropy.

Entropy production is a key concept in non-equilibrium thermodynamics and is particularly relevant to open systems. It measures the amount of entropy generated within a system due to irreversible processes, such as heat transfer and friction.

#### 2.3 Non-equilibrium thermodynamics

Non-equilibrium thermodynamics is a branch of thermodynamics that deals with the behavior of systems that are far from thermal, mechanical, or chemical equilibrium. Unlike classical thermodynamics, which is primarily concerned with equilibrium states, non-equilibrium thermodynamics provides a framework for understanding the dynamics and evolution of systems under conditions of ongoing change and dissipation.

The principles of non-equilibrium thermodynamics were first developed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, as scientists sought to explain the behavior of complex systems that could not be described by classical thermodynamics. The field has evolved significantly since then, with significant contributions from physicists, chemists, and engineers.

It provides a framework for understanding and analyzing complex systems that operate far from equilibrium, such as materials undergoing phase transitions, chemical reactions, and living organisms.

These properties include the positions and momenta of the particles, as well as their energies and interactions. By calculating the statistical distribution of these properties, statistical mechanics allows us to derive the macroscopic thermodynamic properties of a system, such as its temperature, pressure, and entropy.

Non-equilibrium thermodynamics extends these principles to systems that are far from equilibrium. In these systems, the interactions between particles are complex and dynamic, leading to the generation of entropy and the occurrence of irreversible processes. Non-equilibrium thermodynamics provides a framework for understanding and analyzing these processes, taking into account the fluctuations and dissipative effects that arise from the interactions between particles.

In summary, non-equilibrium thermodynamics is a complex and challenging field that provides a framework for understanding the behavior of systems that are far from equilibrium. #### 3.2 Non-equilibrium phase transitions

Non-equilibrium phase transitions are a fascinating and important aspect of non-equilibrium thermodynamics. Unlike equilibrium phase transitions, which occur when a system reaches a critical point and undergoes a dramatic change in its properties, non-equilibrium phase transitions occur when a system is driven out of equilibrium through external influences or internal processes. These transitions are often characterized by sudden and significant changes in the system's behavior, which can have profound implications for its stability, organization, and functionality.

##### 3.2.1 Critical phenomena

Critical phenomena are behaviors that occur near the critical point of a system undergoing a phase transition. These phenomena are characterized by scale invariance and universality, meaning that they exhibit similar behavior regardless of the details of the underlying microscopic interactions. Some key critical phenomena include:

**Scale invariance**: Scale invariance is the property of a system where its properties remain unchanged under scaling transformations, such as changes in length, time, or energy scales. This property is a hallmark of critical phenomena and is responsible for the emergence of fractal structures and long-range correlations in critical systems.

**Universality**: Universality refers to the fact that critical phenomena exhibit similar behavior across different systems, even though the underlying microscopic interactions may differ. This universality is a consequence of the fact that critical phenomena are governed by the same underlying physical principles, such as symmetry breaking and the renormalization group.

**Long-range correlations**: Long-range correlations are correlations between physical quantities that extend over large distances in a system. In critical systems, long-range correlations are a consequence of the scale invariance and universality of critical phenomena and are responsible for the emergence of complex structures and patterns.

**Critical exponents**: Critical exponents are numerical constants that describe the behavior of critical phenomena near the critical point. These exponents characterize the rate of approach to the critical point, the behavior of correlation lengths, and the scaling behavior of physical quantities near the critical point.

##### 3.2.1.1 Scale invariance

Scale invariance is a fundamental property of critical phenomena and is defined as the property of a system where its properties remain unchanged under scaling transformations. In other words, if a system exhibits scale invariance, its properties, such as density, correlation length, or response function, are independent of the scale or size of the system.

Scale invariance is a direct consequence of the fact that critical phenomena are governed by the same underlying physical principles, regardless of the details of the microscopic interactions. This universality is responsible for the emergence of fractal structures and long-range correlations in critical systems.

In critical phenomena, scale invariance leads to power-law behavior, where the properties of the system are described by functions that depend on a few critical exponents. These exponents characterize the behavior of the system near the critical point and are independent of the microscopic details of the interactions.

##### 3.2.1.2 Universal behavior

Universal behavior is another key property of critical phenomena and refers to the fact that critical phenomena exhibit similar behavior across different systems, even though the underlying microscopic interactions may differ. This universality is a consequence of the fact that critical phenomena are governed by the same underlying physical principles, such as symmetry breaking and the renormalization group.

The universality of critical phenomena allows us to identify and classify different types of critical phenomena based on their behavior near the critical point, without having to consider the details of the microscopic interactions. This makes it possible to develop general theories and models that can be applied to a wide range of systems, from fluids and magnets to networks and biological systems.

##### 3.2.2 Non-equilibrium phase transitions and complex dynamics

Non-equilibrium phase transitions and complex dynamics are closely related and play a crucial role in the behavior of open systems. In non-equilibrium phase transitions, the system is driven out of equilibrium through external influences or internal processes, leading to sudden and significant changes in its properties. These transitions can have profound implications for the stability, organization, and functionality of the system.

**Turbulence**: Turbulence is a classic example of a non-equilibrium phase transition. In turbulent flows, the fluid undergoes sudden and irregular changes in velocity and pressure, leading to the formation of complex structures and patterns. Turbulence is characterized by the presence of long-range correlations and scale invariance, which are hallmarks of critical phenomena.

**Self-organized criticality (SOC)**: Self-organized criticality is another example of a non-equilibrium phase transition. In SOC systems, the system evolves towards a critical state, where it is poised on the brink of a phase transition. These systems are characterized by the absence of external influences and the emergence of complex, fractal structures.

**Bifurcations and chaos**: Non-equilibrium phase transitions can also lead to the emergence of bifurcations and chaos. In a bifurcation, the system undergoes a qualitative change in its behavior as a parameter is varied. In chaos, the system exhibits unpredictable and irregular behavior, even though its underlying equations are deterministic.

**Crossover phenomena**: Crossover phenomena occur when a system undergoes a gradual transition between different regimes, rather than a sudden phase transition. These transitions are often characterized by a slow approach to the critical point and the emergence of scaling behaviors.

In summary, non-equilibrium phase transitions and complex dynamics are important topics in non-equilibrium thermodynamics that have profound implications for the behavior of open systems. By studying these phenomena, we can gain a deeper understanding of the dynamics of complex systems and develop new theories and methods to predict and control their behavior.

### 4.3 Linear Response Theory

Linear response theory is a powerful framework in non-equilibrium thermodynamics that allows us to understand how a system responds to small perturbations. The theory is based on the assumption that the response of the system is linear and can be described by a set of linear response functions. These functions relate the change in a system's properties, such as temperature or pressure, to the magnitude and direction of the perturbation.

#### 4.3.1 Basic Concepts

**Linear Response**

In linear response theory, the response of a system to a small perturbation is assumed to be linear. This means that the change in a system's property, \( \delta \Xi \), can be expressed as a linear function of the perturbation, \( \delta \Phi \):

\[ \delta \Xi = \chi(\Phi) \delta \Phi \]

where \( \chi(\Phi) \) is the linear response function, which characterizes the system's response to the perturbation \( \Phi \). The linear response function can be positive or negative, indicating a direct or inverse relationship between the perturbation and the response.

**Linear Response Functions**

Linear response functions describe how a system's properties change in response to a small perturbation. For example, the thermal expansion coefficient is a linear response function that describes how the volume of a material changes with temperature. Similarly, the electrical conductivity is a linear response function that describes how the current in a material changes with an applied electric field.

**Response Functions in Non-equilibrium Thermodynamics**

In non-equilibrium thermodynamics, linear response functions are used to relate the change in a system's properties to the perturbations applied to it. For example, the heat capacity at constant volume, \( C_V \), is a linear response function that relates the change in internal energy, \( \delta U \), to the change in temperature, \( \delta T \):

\[ \delta U = C_V \delta T \]

Similarly, the thermal conductivity, \( k \), is a linear response function that relates the heat flux, \( \dot{Q} \), to the temperature gradient, \( \Delta T \):

\[ \dot{Q} = -k \Delta T \]

These linear response functions are crucial for understanding and predicting the behavior of non-equilibrium systems.

#### 4.3.2 Fluctuation-Dissipation Theorem

The fluctuation-dissipation theorem is a fundamental result in linear response theory that relates the fluctuations in a system to its dissipation. The theorem states that the linear response function, \( \chi(\Phi) \), can be expressed as the inverse of the thermal conductivity matrix, \( \chi^{-1}(\Phi) \), evaluated at the equilibrium value of the perturbation:

\[ \chi(\Phi) = -\frac{1}{kT} \left< \delta \Phi \delta \Xi \right> \]

where \( \left< \cdot \right> \) denotes the thermal average and \( k \) is the Boltzmann constant. This equation shows that the linear response function is directly related to the fluctuations in the system, providing a quantitative connection between the system's response to perturbations and its intrinsic fluctuations.

The fluctuation-dissipation theorem has important implications for the behavior of non-equilibrium systems. It indicates that the response of a system to a perturbation is proportional to the fluctuations in the system, which are a measure of its disorder. This theorem also suggests that systems with high dissipation tend to have strong responses to perturbations, as their fluctuations are large.

#### 4.3.3 Applications

**Thermal Conductivity**

Linear response theory is widely used to calculate the thermal conductivity of materials. By measuring the change in temperature, \( \delta T \), due to a small heat flux, \( \delta Q \), and using the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, the thermal conductivity can be determined. The thermal conductivity is related to the linear response function as:

\[ k = -\frac{\left< \delta Q \delta T \right>}{\left< \delta T^2 \right>} \]

This equation shows that the thermal conductivity is proportional to the square of the response function and inversely proportional to the variance of the temperature fluctuations.

Linear response theory also provides insights into the fluctuations and dissipation in non-equilibrium systems.
