The development of quantum cryptography has been fueled by advancements in quantum mechanics and the growing interest in quantum computing. #### Basic Concepts and Mechanisms of Post-Quantum Symmetric Encryption

Post-quantum symmetric encryption relies on the principles of quantum mechanics to ensure the security of encrypted communications. The two most prominent post-quantum symmetric encryption techniques are quantum key distribution (QKD) and lattice-based cryptography.

1. **Quantum Key Distribution (QKD):**

Quantum key distribution is a method for securely distributing secret keys between two parties using quantum communication channels. The key distribution process involves the following steps:

   - **Encoding:** The sender encodes the key information into quantum states and sends them to the receiver through a quantum channel.

   - **Detection and Measurement:** The receiver measures the quantum states received from the sender and generates corresponding classical bits.

   - **Privacy Amplification:** The sender and receiver perform error correction and privacy amplification to ensure that any eavesdropping attempts can be detected and the remaining key can be discarded.

   - **Secret Key Generation:** The sender and receiver disclose the remaining key bits to each other, resulting in a shared secret key.

The security of QKD is based on the fundamental principles of quantum mechanics, such as the no-cloning theorem and the uncertainty principle. These principles ensure that any attempt by an eavesdropper to intercept and measure the quantum states will inevitably disrupt the communication and alert the legitimate parties to the eavesdropping attempt.

2. **Lattice-Based Cryptography:**

Lattice-based cryptography is another promising approach to post-quantum symmetric encryption. Lattice-based cryptosystems are designed to be hard to solve using quantum computers, making them suitable for post-quantum cryptography.

Lattice-based cryptography relies on the difficulty of solving certain problems related to lattices, which are geometric structures composed of points in multi-dimensional space. Some of the key lattice-based cryptosystems include:

   - **Learning With Errors (LWE):** The LWE problem involves finding a vector in a lattice that is close to a linear combination of other lattice vectors, subject to small errors. LWE-based cryptosystems have been shown to be secure against both classical and quantum attacks.

   - **Shortest Vector Problem (SVP):** The SVP problem involves finding the shortest non-zero vector in a lattice. Cryptosystems based on the SVP problem are considered to be highly secure against quantum attacks.

   - **NTRU Cryptosystem:** The NTRU cryptosystem is a popular lattice-based cryptosystem that utilizes polynomial lattices. It offers efficient key generation, encryption, and decryption operations.

#### Lattice-Based Cryptography

Lattice-based cryptography is one of the most widely studied and promising post-quantum cryptographic techniques. It relies on the difficulty of solving certain problems related to lattices, which are geometric structures composed of points in multi-dimensional space.

1. **Learning With Errors (LWE):**

The Learning With Errors (LWE) problem is a core component of lattice-based cryptography. It involves finding a vector in a lattice that is close to a linear combination of other lattice vectors, subject to small errors. The LWE problem is considered hard for both classical and quantum computers, making it a suitable candidate for post-quantum cryptography.

**Mathematical Model:**

Let $\mathcal{L}$ be a lattice in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with basis $\mathbf{B} = [\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2, ..., \mathbf{b}_n]$. The LWE problem can be formalized as follows:

Given a random vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, a random basis $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, and a random error vector $\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, find a vector $\mathbf{y}$ such that:

$$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{e}$$

**Algorithm:**

The LWE problem can be solved using various algorithms, such as the shortest vector algorithm (SVA) and the lattice reduction algorithm. These algorithms find the closest vector to the target vector $\mathbf{y}$ in the lattice $\mathcal{L}$.

2. **NTRU Cryptosystem:**

The NTRU cryptosystem is a popular lattice-based cryptosystem that utilizes polynomial lattices. It is known for its efficiency and resistance to both classical and quantum attacks.

**Mathematical Model:**

The NTRU cryptosystem operates in a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$ and uses polynomials over $\mathbb{F}_q$. The encryption and decryption processes involve modular polynomial arithmetic and lattice operations.

**Encryption:**

Given a public polynomial $\mathbf{p}(x)$ and a random polynomial $\mathbf{r}(x)$, the encryption algorithm computes the ciphertext as:

$$\mathbf{c}(x) = \mathbf{p}(x) \cdot \mathbf{r}(x) \mod q$$

**Decryption:**

Given the ciphertext $\mathbf{c}(x)$ and the private polynomial $\mathbf{q}(x)$, the decryption algorithm computes the decrypted message as:

$$\mathbf{m}(x) = \frac{\mathbf{c}(x)}{\mathbf{q}(x)} \mod q$$

**Security:**

The security of the NTRU cryptosystem relies on the hardness of the NTRU problem, which involves factoring a ring of polynomials. The NTRU cryptosystem has been shown to be secure against both classical and quantum attacks, making it a suitable candidate for post-quantum cryptography.

#### Hash-Based Cryptography

Hash-based cryptography is another important class of post-quantum symmetric encryption algorithms. These algorithms rely on the properties of cryptographic hash functions to ensure security.

1. **Hash-Based Cryptography with Encryption (HBCE):**

Hash-Based Cryptography with Encryption (HBCE) is a family of post-quantum symmetric encryption algorithms that combine hash functions and pseudo-random functions. The most prominent example of HBCE is the GHash algorithm.

**Mathematical Model:**

Given a message $m$ and a cryptographic hash function $H$, the HBCE algorithm works as follows:

**Encryption:**

1. Compute the hash value $h = H(m)$.
2. Use $h$ as a key for a pseudo-random function $F$ to generate a random mask $m'$: $m' = F(h)$.
3. Encrypt the message $m$ using the mask $m'$: $c = m \oplus m'$.

**Decryption:**

1. Compute the hash value $h = H(c)$.
2. Use $h$ as a key for the pseudo-random function $F$ to generate the mask $m'$: $m' = F(h)$.
3. Decrypt the ciphertext $c$ using the mask $m'$: $m = c \oplus m'$.

**Security:**

The security of HBCE relies on the collision resistance and pre-image resistance properties of the cryptographic hash function $H$. The hardness of inverting pseudo-random functions further enhances the security of the algorithm.

2. **Hash-Based Cryptography with Key Derivation (HBKDF):**

Hash-Based Cryptography with Key Derivation (HBKDF) is a key generation and derivation mechanism that uses a cryptographic hash function to derive one or more secret keys from a secret value and a public key.

**Mathematical Model:**

Given a secret value $s$, a public value $p$, and a cryptographic hash function $H$, the HBKDF algorithm works as follows:

**Key Generation:**

1. Compute the hash value $h = H(s \|\| p)$.
2. Use $h$ as a key for a pseudo-random function to generate the secret key $k$: $k = F(h)$.

**Key Derivation:**

1. Compute the hash value $h = H(s \|\| p)$.
2. Use $h$ as a key for a pseudo-random function to generate one or more keys from $k$: $k_1 = F(h), k_2 = F(k_1), ...$.

**Security:**

The security of HBKDF relies on the collision resistance and pre-image resistance properties of the cryptographic hash function $H$. The pseudo-random function used for key generation and derivation further enhances the security of the algorithm.

Multivariate polynomial cryptography (MPC) is a post-quantum cryptographic technique that utilizes multivariate polynomial equations to provide security. MPC relies on the difficulty of solving systems of multivariate polynomial equations over finite fields.

1. **Trace-Based MPC:**

Trace-Based MPC is a variant of MPC that uses the trace of a multivariate polynomial to simplify the decryption process.

**Mathematical Model:**

Let $f(x_1, x_2, ..., x_n)$ be a multivariate polynomial over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$. The trace of $f$ is defined as:

$$tr(f) = \sum_{i=1}^{q-1} f(i, i, ..., i)$$

**Encryption:**

1. Choose a random polynomial $g(x_1, x_2, ..., x_n)$ and compute $h(x) = f(x) + g(x)$.
2. Compute the ciphertext $c = tr(h)$.

**Decryption:**

1. Compute $h(x) = f(x) + g(x)$.
2. Compute the trace of $h$ and compare it to the ciphertext $c$.
3. If $tr(h) = c$, decrypt the message as $x = i$.

**Security:**

The security of Trace-Based MPC relies on the hardness of solving multivariate polynomial equations over finite fields. The trace property simplifies the decryption process while maintaining security.

#### Code-Based Cryptography

Code-based cryptography utilizes error-correcting codes to provide security. The most prominent example of code-based cryptography is McEliece cryptosystem.

**Cryptographic Uses:**

$$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{e}$$

**Cryptographic Uses:**
