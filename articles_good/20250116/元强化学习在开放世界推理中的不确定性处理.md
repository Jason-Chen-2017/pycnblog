#### Step 1: Reviewing Basic Reinforcement Learning Algorithms

Before diving into MRL, it is essential to revisit the foundational algorithms of reinforcement learning, such as Q-learning, Policy Gradient, and Deep Q-Networks (DQN).

**Q-Learning Algorithm**

Q-learning is an off-policy reinforcement learning algorithm that aims to learn the value function, \( Q(s, a) \), representing the quality of an action \( a \) in state \( s \). The algorithm uses an iterative process to update the Q-values based on the Bellman equation:

$$
Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha [r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') - Q(s, a)]
$$

**Policy Gradient Algorithm**

Policy Gradient is an on-policy reinforcement learning algorithm that directly optimizes the policy \( \pi(a|s; \theta) \). The goal is to maximize the expected return:

$$
J(\theta) = \sum_{s, a} \pi(a|s; \theta) \cdot r(s, a)
$$

The gradient descent is used to update the policy parameters:

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla_\theta J(\theta)
$$

**Deep Q-Networks (DQN)**

DQN is an extension of Q-learning that uses a deep neural network to approximate the Q-value function. It involves experience replay and fixed Q-targets to stabilize the training process:

$$
Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha [r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') - Q(s, a)]
$$

#### Step 2: Core Principles of Meta-Reinforcement Learning

**Meta-Learning in Reinforcement Learning**

Meta-learning enhances reinforcement learning by enabling models to quickly adapt to new tasks with minimal data. The core principle is to learn a set of transferable skills from diverse tasks, which can be applied to new tasks efficiently.

**Pseudo-Task Generation**

Pseudo-task generation is a key component of meta-reinforcement learning. It involves generating a variety of tasks that are similar in structure but different in specific details. The model learns to solve these tasks, developing a general policy that can transfer knowledge to new tasks.

**Algorithm Framework**

The general framework of MRL involves several steps:

1. **Task Selection**: Randomly select tasks from a pool of available tasks.
2. **Task Initialization**: Initialize the environment and task-specific parameters.
3. **Policy Training**: Train the policy in the selected task using reinforcement learning techniques.
4. **Experience Transfer**: Aggregate experiences across tasks to update the model.
5. **Policy Evaluation**: Evaluate the policy's performance on the current task.
6. **Model Update**: Update the model based on the aggregated experiences.

#### Step 3: Mathematical Models and Formulation

**Meta-Learning Loss Function**

The meta-learning loss function aims to optimize the model's ability to transfer knowledge across tasks. It involves minimizing the difference between the expected return of the learned policy and the optimal return:

$$
L(\theta) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{s \in S} \sum_{a \in A} \pi(a|s; \theta) [r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a'; \theta) - Q(s, a; \theta)]
$$

To handle uncertainty in open-world inference, MRL can use uncertainty quantification techniques, such as Bayesian neural networks or uncertainty-aware policy optimization. The goal is to estimate the uncertainty in state-action values:

$$

where \( \mu_Q \) and \( \sigma_Q^2 \) are the mean and variance of the Q-value distribution, and \( D \) is the experience dataset.
