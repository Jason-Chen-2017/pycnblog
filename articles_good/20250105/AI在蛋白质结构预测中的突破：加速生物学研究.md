### Theoretical foundations of AI in protein structure prediction

To fully grasp the transformative power of AI in protein structure prediction, it is essential to delve into the theoretical foundations that underpin this field. #### Key Mathematical Models

1. **Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)**
   CNNs are a class of deep learning models specifically designed for processing and analyzing visual data. In the context of protein structure prediction, CNNs are used to analyze protein sequences and identify patterns that correlate with specific structural features. The core concept behind CNNs is the use of convolutional layers, which apply filters to the input data to capture spatial features.

2. **Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)**
   RNNs are another class of deep learning models that are well-suited for handling sequential data. Unlike traditional neural networks, RNNs have loops that allow them to maintain information from previous steps in the sequence. This makes them particularly effective for modeling the temporal dependencies in protein sequences.

3. **Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)**
   GANs are a type of neural network architecture composed of two sub-networks: a generator and a discriminator. The generator creates synthetic protein sequences, while the discriminator evaluates whether these sequences are real or fake. By training these two networks together in a competitive manner, GANs can learn to generate high-quality protein sequences that are indistinguishable from real ones.

4. **Transformer Models**
   Transformer models, first introduced in the paper "Attention is All You Need," have revolutionized the field of natural language processing and have also shown promise in protein structure prediction. Transformers use self-attention mechanisms to weigh the importance of different parts of the input sequence, enabling them to capture long-range dependencies in protein sequences.

#### Core Algorithms

1. **Sequence Alignment Algorithms**
   Sequence alignment is a fundamental step in protein structure prediction, as it allows for the comparison of protein sequences to identify similarities and differences. Common sequence alignment algorithms include Smith-Waterman and Needleman-Wunsch algorithms. These algorithms use dynamic programming to find the optimal alignment between two sequences and score the alignment based on a scoring matrix.

2. **Phylogenetic Trees**
   Phylogenetic trees are used to represent the evolutionary relationships between proteins. By constructing phylogenetic trees, researchers can infer ancestral sequences and use them to guide protein structure prediction. Algorithms such as Maximum Likelihood and Bayesian Inference are commonly used for constructing phylogenetic trees.

3. **Machine Learning Algorithms**
   Machine learning algorithms play a crucial role in protein structure prediction by training models to predict structures from protein sequences. Common machine learning algorithms used in this field include Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests, and Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM). These algorithms are trained on labeled datasets containing protein sequences and their corresponding structures.

4. **Optimization Algorithms**
   Once a protein structure has been predicted, optimization algorithms are used to refine the structure and improve its energy stability. Common optimization algorithms include simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, and molecular dynamics simulations. These algorithms aim to minimize the energy of the protein structure and identify the most stable conformation.

#### Key Mathematical Models and Formulas

1. **Energy Function**

The energy function is a core component of protein structure prediction algorithms, representing the overall stability of a protein structure. It is composed of several components, including bond angles, bond lengths, and torsional angles. The energy function can be expressed as:

$$ E = \sum_{i<j}^n (E_{bond}(r_{ij}) + E_{angle}(\theta_{ij}) + E_{torsion}(\phi_{ij})) $$

Where:
- \( E \) is the total energy of the protein structure.
- \( n \) is the number of atoms in the protein.
- \( E_{bond}(r_{ij}) \) is the bond energy function, representing the energy due to bond lengths.
- \( E_{angle}(\theta_{ij}) \) is the angle energy function, representing the energy due to bond angles.
- \( E_{torsion}(\phi_{ij}) \) is the torsional angle energy function, representing the energy due to torsional angles.

2. **Scoring Function**

The scoring function is used to evaluate the quality of a predicted protein structure. It is derived from the energy function and provides a single scalar value representing the stability of the protein. A commonly used scoring function is the Gō score, which is defined as:

$$ S = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\alpha \cdot E}} $$

Where:
- \( S \) is the scoring function value.
- \( E \) is the energy of the protein structure.
- \( \alpha \) is a scaling factor that adjusts the range of the scoring function.

3. **Probability Distribution**

In probabilistic models, such as those based on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, the probability distribution of protein structures is used to sample the most likely conformations. The probability distribution can be represented as:

$$ P(s) = \frac{e^{-\beta \cdot E(s)}}{\sum_{s'} e^{-\beta \cdot E(s')}} $$

Where:
- \( P(s) \) is the probability of structure \( s \).
- \( E(s) \) is the energy of structure \( s \).
- \( \beta \) is the inverse temperature parameter.

4. **Distance Matrix**

The distance matrix is used to represent the spatial relationships between atoms in a protein structure. It is a square matrix with dimensions \( n \times n \), where \( n \) is the number of atoms. The element \( d_{ij} \) in the distance matrix represents the distance between atoms \( i \) and \( j \):

$$ d_{ij} = \sqrt{(x_i - x_j)^2 + (y_i - y_j)^2 + (z_i - z_j)^2} $$

Where:
- \( d_{ij} \) is the distance between atoms \( i \) and \( j \).
- \( (x_i, y_i, z_i) \) and \( (x_j, y_j, z_j) \) are the Cartesian coordinates of atoms \( i \) and \( j \), respectively.
