### Fundamental Concepts and Theoretical Framework

Keyword engineering is grounded in several fundamental concepts and theoretical frameworks that provide a robust foundation for its practical applications. #### Basic Concepts of Keyword Engineering

Keyword engineering is essentially the process of identifying, selecting, and utilizing terms that capture the essence of data and represent its important characteristics. These keywords serve as the building blocks for creating meaningful models and insights from raw data. The basic concepts of keyword engineering include:

1. **Keyword Extraction**: This is the process of identifying relevant terms from a given dataset. Various techniques such as Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF), Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), and Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) are used to extract meaningful keywords.

2. **Keyword Selection**: Once a set of potential keywords is extracted, the next step is to select the most relevant ones. This involves evaluating the importance and relevance of each keyword based on criteria such as term frequency, document frequency, and mutual information.

3. **Keyword Ranking**: After selection, keywords are ranked to prioritize their importance in the data analysis process. Common ranking methods include information gain, chi-square test, and support vector machines.

4. **Keyword Integration**: Finally, the selected keywords are integrated into the anomaly detection models. This could involve transforming the keywords into a suitable format for the model, such as embedding them into a high-dimensional space.

#### Comparison of Keyword Engineering Methods

Several methods are employed in keyword engineering, each with its advantages and disadvantages. Here, we compare some of the most common methods:

1. **TF-IDF**:
   - **Advantages**: Simple to implement, computationally efficient, captures the importance of keywords based on their frequency in the dataset and their uniqueness.
   - **Disadvantages**: Can prioritize common words over informative ones, does not capture semantic relationships between keywords.

2. **LDA**:
   - **Advantages**: Can capture latent topics in the data, provides a more nuanced understanding of the data distribution.
   - **Disadvantages**: Can be computationally intensive, requires prior knowledge of the number of topics, and may struggle with datasets of high dimensionality.

3. **NMF**:
   - **Advantages**: Unsupervised learning method that can identify hidden patterns in the data, preserves the non-negativity of the data.
   - **Disadvantages**: Can lead to overfitting, requires careful tuning of parameters, and may not be suitable for very high-dimensional data.

4. **Word Embeddings**:
   - **Advantages**: Capture semantic relationships between words, provide a high-dimensional representation of keywords that can be used in various machine learning models.
   - **Disadvantages**: Can be computationally expensive to train, may suffer from issues like semantic drift, and require large amounts of training data.

#### Mathematical Model and Formulas

To further delve into the theoretical aspects, we present the mathematical models and formulas used in keyword engineering. These include:

1. **TF-IDF**:
   - Formula: TF \* IDF
   - Where TF is Term Frequency (TF = count of term in a document / total number of terms in the document), and IDF is Inverse Document Frequency (IDF = log(1 + N / df))

2. **LDA**:
   - Formula: P(w|z) = (α + Φ\_w\_z) / Σ(α + Φ\_w\_j)
   - Where P(w|z) is the probability of a word given a topic, α is the prior distribution of topics, Φ\_w\_z is the word-topic probability matrix.

3. **NMF**:
   - Formula: X ≈ W \* H
   - Where X is the data matrix, W and H are the factor matrices representing the basis and coefficients, respectively.

4. **Mutual Information**:
   - Formula: MI(X, Y) = Σ p(x, y) log(p(x, y) / (p(x) \* p(y)))
   - Where X and Y are two variables, p(x, y) is the joint probability of X and Y, and p(x) and p(y) are the marginal probabilities of X and Y, respectively.

### Principles of Anomaly Detection Algorithms

Anomaly detection algorithms are designed to identify instances that significantly deviate from a predefined norm or baseline within a dataset. #### Basic Principles of Anomaly Detection

Anomaly detection algorithms can be broadly classified into two categories: statistical methods and machine learning-based methods.

1. **Statistical Methods**:
   - **Distance-Based Methods**: These methods identify anomalies based on the distance of a data point from the majority of the data points. Common techniques include Euclidean distance and Manhattan distance.
   - **Statistical Models**: These methods model the expected behavior of normal data using statistical distributions like Gaussian distribution or kernel density estimation. Any data point that does not fit the model is considered an anomaly.
   - **Density-Based Methods**: These methods identify anomalies based on the density of data points. Common techniques include Local Outlier Factor (LOF) and DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise).

2. **Machine Learning-Based Methods**:
   - **Supervised Learning**: These methods use labeled data to train a model that can classify new data points as normal or anomalous. Common techniques include decision trees, support vector machines, and neural networks.
   - **Unsupervised Learning**: These methods do not require labeled data and identify anomalies based on the inherent structure of the data. Common techniques include clustering algorithms like K-means, hierarchical clustering, and self-organizing maps (SOMs).

#### Mathematical Model and Formulas

The k-means algorithm, a popular unsupervised learning method, can be explained using the following mathematical model and formulas:

1. **Objective Function**:
   - Minimize the sum of squared distances between data points and their cluster centroids.
   - Formula: min Σ(d(x_i, \(\mu\)_j)^2), where x_i is a data point, \(\mu\)_j is the centroid of cluster j, and d(·, ·) is the distance metric (e.g., Euclidean distance).

2. **Cluster Centroids**:
   - Initial centroids can be chosen randomly or using more sophisticated methods like k-means++. The centroids are then updated iteratively as the mean of all data points assigned to the cluster.
   - Formula: \(\mu\)_j = (1/N_j) * Σ(x_i | x_i ∈ cluster j), where N_j is the number of data points in cluster j.

3. **Assignment of Data Points**:
   - Each data point is assigned to the nearest centroid based on the distance metric.
   - Formula: assign x_i to cluster j if d(x_i, \(\mu\)_j) ≤ d(x_i, \(\mu\)_k) for all k ≠ j.

4. **Convergence Criteria**:
   - The algorithm converges when the centroids no longer change significantly, or a maximum number of iterations is reached.
   - Formula: ||\(\mu\)_new - \(\mu\)_old|| ≤ threshold, where ||·|| is the Euclidean norm.

5. **Convergence**:
   - The centroids do not change significantly, indicating convergence.

6. **Anomaly Detection**:
   - The point [100, 100] is classified as an anomaly since it is significantly different from the rest of the data points.

#### Keyword Extraction

Keyword extraction is the process of identifying the most informative terms or keywords from the data. For this example, we will use the Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) method, which is a commonly used technique in natural language processing.

```python
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

# Convert the feature data to a string format suitable for TF-IDF
```

#### Keyword Integration

Once the keywords are selected, they need to be integrated into the anomaly detection model. For this example, we will use the k-means algorithm to cluster the data and identify anomalies.


#### Anomaly Detection

Anomalies are detected based on the cluster centroids and the distances between the data points and centroids. Data points that are far from their assigned centroids are considered anomalies.

```python
# Calculate the distance between each data point and its centroid
distances = kmeans.transform(tfidf_matrix)

# Assign anomalies based on distance
anomaly_threshold = 2 * silhouette_avg  # Set the threshold as a multiple of the silhouette score
anomalies = [i for i, x in enumerate(distances[:, 0]) if np.linalg.norm(x) > anomaly_threshold]

# Get the anomalies from the original dataset
anomalies_data = X_test[anomalies]
print("Anomalies Detected:", anomalies_data)
```

#### Mathematical Models and Formulas

The k-means algorithm is based on several mathematical models and formulas:

1. **Objective Function**:
   - Minimize the sum of squared distances between data points and their cluster centroids.
   - Formula: min Σ(d(x_i, \(\mu\)_j)^2), where x_i is a data point, \(\mu\)_j is the centroid of cluster j, and d(·, ·) is the distance metric (e.g., Euclidean distance).

2. **Cluster Centroids**:
   - Initial centroids are chosen randomly or using more sophisticated methods like k-means++.
   - Formula: \(\mu\)_j = (1/N_j) * Σ(x_i | x_i ∈ cluster j), where N_j is the number of data points in cluster j.

3. **Assignment of Data Points**:
   - Each data point is assigned to the nearest centroid based on the distance metric.
   - Formula: assign x_i to cluster j if d(x_i, \(\mu\)_j) ≤ d(x_i, \(\mu\)_k) for all k ≠ j.

4. **Convergence Criteria**:
   - The algorithm converges when the centroids no longer change significantly, or a maximum number of iterations is reached.
   - Formula: ||\(\mu\)_new - \(\mu\)_old|| ≤ threshold, where ||·|| is the Euclidean norm.
