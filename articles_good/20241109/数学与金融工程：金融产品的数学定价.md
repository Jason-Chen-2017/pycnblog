## Part 1: Foundations of Financial Engineering

### 1.1 Introduction to Financial Engineering

Financial engineering is a multidisciplinary field that combines principles from finance, mathematics, statistics, and computer science to design and create innovative financial instruments and strategies. Its primary goal is to provide solutions to complex financial problems, enhance investment strategies, and mitigate risks. In today's rapidly evolving financial markets, financial engineering has become an indispensable tool for investors, corporations, and financial institutions.

#### 1.1.1 Importance of Financial Engineering in Modern Markets

The importance of financial engineering can be seen in several aspects:

1. **Risk Management**: Financial engineers develop sophisticated models and tools to assess, measure, and manage risks in financial markets. These tools help investors and institutions make informed decisions, thereby minimizing potential losses.

2. **Portfolio Management**: By utilizing mathematical and statistical techniques, financial engineers design efficient investment portfolios that maximize returns while minimizing risks. This is especially important in today's diversified financial markets, where investors have a wide range of asset classes to choose from.

3. **Derivative Design**: Financial engineers create complex financial derivatives, such as options, swaps, and structured products, that allow investors to hedge against various risks and take advantage of market opportunities.

4. **Market Efficiency**: Financial engineering promotes market efficiency by developing new trading algorithms, optimizing pricing mechanisms, and improving market infrastructure.

#### 1.1.2 Basic Concepts in Financial Engineering

To understand financial engineering, it is essential to familiarize oneself with some basic concepts:

1. **Financial Instruments**: These are contracts or agreements between two parties that derive their value from an underlying asset. Examples include stocks, bonds, options, futures, and swaps.

2. **Risk**: In financial terms, risk refers to the possibility of losing money due to various factors, such as market volatility, credit defaults, or changes in interest rates.

3. **Portfolio**: A collection of financial instruments held by an investor, with the aim of achieving specific investment objectives.

4. **Hedging**: A risk management strategy that involves taking positions in financial instruments to offset potential losses in another investment.

5. **Derivative**: A financial instrument whose value is derived from an underlying asset, such as a stock, bond, or commodity. Derivatives are used for speculation, arbitrage, and risk management.

#### 1.1.3 Historical Development of Financial Engineering

The history of financial engineering can be traced back to the early 1970s when the first exchange-traded options were introduced. Since then, the field has evolved significantly, driven by advances in mathematical modeling, computational techniques, and financial markets. Some key milestones include:

1. **1973**: The introduction of the Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE) marked the beginning of the modern options market.

2. **1976**: The creation of the binomial tree model by Cox, Ross, and Rubinstein provided a powerful tool for pricing options and other financial derivatives.

3. **1977**: The introduction of the Black-Scholes model by Fischer Black and Myron Scholes revolutionized the field of financial engineering by providing a formula for pricing European options.

4. **1980s**: The development of financial derivatives such as swaps, futures, and options, which expanded the range of risk management tools available to investors.

5. **1990s**: The rise of algorithmic trading and the development of high-speed computing systems, which enabled the implementation of complex trading strategies.

6. **2000s**: The development of credit risk modeling and the introduction of credit derivatives, such as credit default swaps (CDS), which helped to manage the risk of defaults on corporate debt.

In summary, financial engineering has become an integral part of the financial industry, driven by advances in technology and the increasing complexity of financial markets. By leveraging mathematical and computational techniques, financial engineers continue to innovate and create new financial products and strategies that enhance market efficiency and risk management.

### 1.2 Fundamental Principles of Financial Mathematics

The foundation of financial engineering lies in the principles of financial mathematics, which are essential for understanding and analyzing financial products and markets. In this section, we will delve into the basic concepts of financial mathematics, including the time value of money, interest rates, and their applications in bond pricing.

#### 1.2.1 Time Value of Money

The time value of money (TVM) is a fundamental concept in financial mathematics that recognizes the idea that a dollar received today is worth more than a dollar received in the future. This is because money can be invested and earn returns over time. The TVM principle is crucial for valuing cash flows occurring at different points in time and making decisions based on their present value.

**Future Value (FV)**

The future value of an investment is the value it will have at a specified future date, assuming a certain rate of return. The formula for calculating the future value is:

$$
FV = PV \times (1 + r)^n
$$

Where:
- \( FV \) is the future value
- \( PV \) is the present value
- \( r \) is the interest rate per period
- \( n \) is the number of periods

**Present Value (PV)**

The present value is the current value of a future cash flow, discounted at a given interest rate. It is used to determine the worth of an investment today. The formula for calculating the present value is:

$$
PV = FV / (1 + r)^n
$$

Where the variables have the same meaning as in the future value formula.

**Compounding and Discounting**

Compounding is the process of reinvesting the returns from an investment to generate more returns over time. In contrast, discounting is the process of calculating the present value of future cash flows to determine their current worth.

**Example**

Let's consider an investment of \$1000 at an annual interest rate of 5% compounded annually. If we want to calculate the future value after 5 years, we can use the formula:

$$
FV = 1000 \times (1 + 0.05)^5 = 1000 \times 1.2763 = 1276.30
$$

On the other hand, if we want to find the present value of a future payment of \$1276.30 at the same interest rate and time period, we can use the present value formula:

$$
PV = 1276.30 / (1 + 0.05)^5 = 1276.30 / 1.2763 = 1000
$$

This demonstrates that the present value of \$1276.30 received after 5 years is equivalent to the original investment of \$1000.

#### 1.2.2 Interest Rates

Interest rates are a critical component in financial mathematics and have a significant impact on the pricing of financial products. They represent the cost of borrowing or the return on investment over a specific period.

**Types of Interest Rates**

1. **Nominal Interest Rate**: This is the stated interest rate on a loan or investment, without adjusting for inflation. It is the rate that is typically advertised by financial institutions.
2. **Real Interest Rate**: This is the nominal interest rate adjusted for inflation. It reflects the true cost of borrowing or the real return on investment.
3. **Effective Interest Rate**: This is the actual interest rate that is paid or received over the life of a loan or investment, taking into account compounding periods. It is also known as the annual percentage rate (APR).

**Yields and Pricing**

Yields are measures of the return on an investment, expressed as a percentage of its price. They are used to compare the performance of different investments.

1. **Yield to Maturity (YTM)**: This is the total return expected on a bond if it is held until maturity, taking into account the bond's current price, face value, coupon payments, and time to maturity.
2. **Current Yield**: This is the annual coupon payment divided by the current market price of the bond.

**Example**

Let's consider a bond with a face value of \$1000, an annual coupon rate of 5%, and a current market price of \$950. The YTM can be calculated as follows:

$$
YTM = \frac{C \times FV}{P} \times \frac{1}{t}
$$

Where:
- \( C \) is the annual coupon payment (\$50 in this case)
- \( FV \) is the face value of the bond (\$1000)
- \( P \) is the current market price of the bond (\$950)
- \( t \) is the time to maturity (in years)

$$
YTM = \frac{50 \times 1000}{950} \times \frac{1}{1} = 5.263%
$$

The current yield can be calculated as:

$$
Current Yield = \frac{C}{P} = \frac{50}{950} \approx 5.26%
$$

In summary, the YTM provides the overall return expected if the bond is held until maturity, while the current yield provides a snapshot of the return based on the current market price.

By understanding the time value of money, interest rates, and their applications in bond pricing, financial engineers can accurately value financial products and make informed investment decisions.

### 1.3 Financial Instruments

Financial instruments are the building blocks of the financial market, representing claims to future cash flows or ownership in various entities. They can be categorized into three broad types: debt instruments, equity instruments, and derivative instruments. Each type serves different purposes and carries distinct risks and returns.

#### 1.3.1 Bonds

Bonds are debt instruments issued by governments, municipalities, or corporations to raise capital. They are essentially loans provided by investors to the issuer, which the issuer agrees to repay at a future date along with periodic interest payments. Bonds are characterized by their face value, coupon rate, and maturity date.

**Bond Pricing**

The price of a bond is determined by the present value of its future cash flows, which include the periodic coupon payments and the repayment of the face value at maturity. The formula for calculating the price of a bond is:

$$
P = \frac{C \times (1 - (1 + r)^{-n})}{r} + \frac{FV}{(1 + r)^n}
$$

Where:
- \( P \) is the price of the bond
- \( C \) is the annual coupon payment
- \( r \) is the yield to maturity (YTM)
- \( n \) is the number of periods until maturity
- \( FV \) is the face value of the bond

**Bond Valuation**

Bond valuation involves estimating the intrinsic value of a bond based on its expected cash flows and the market interest rate. The key factors in bond valuation are the bond's coupon rate, YTM, and time to maturity.

**Example**

Consider a bond with a face value of \$1000, an annual coupon rate of 5%, and a YTM of 4% with 5 years to maturity. Using the bond pricing formula, the price of the bond can be calculated as:

$$
P = \frac{50 \times (1 - (1 + 0.04)^{-5})}{0.04} + \frac{1000}{(1 + 0.04)^5}
$$

$$
P = \frac{50 \times (1 - 0.8227)}{0.04} + \frac{1000}{1.2167} = 962.87 + 826.43 = 1790.30
$$

This indicates that the bond is overpriced in the market, as the calculated price exceeds the face value.

#### 1.3.2 Stocks

Stocks represent ownership in a corporation. When investors purchase stocks, they become partial owners of the company and are entitled to a share of its profits, typically in the form of dividends. Stocks are categorized as either common stocks or preferred stocks.

**Equity Pricing**

The price of a stock is determined by the market's perception of its future earnings potential and growth prospects. The two most commonly used models for equity pricing are the Dividend Discount Model (DDM) and the Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE) model.

1. **Dividend Discount Model (DDM)**

The DDM values a stock based on the present value of its expected future dividends. The formula is:

$$
P = \frac{D}{r}
$$

Where:
- \( P \) is the price of the stock
- \( D \) is the expected annual dividend per share
- \( r \) is the required rate of return on equity

**Example**

Consider a stock with an expected annual dividend of \$5 and a required rate of return of 10%. The stock price can be calculated as:

$$
P = \frac{5}{0.10} = 50
$$

2. **Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE)**

The FCFE model values a stock based on the present value of its expected future free cash flows to equity. The formula is:

$$
P = \frac{FCFE}{r}
$$

Where:
- \( P \) is the price of the stock
- \( FCFE \) is the free cash flow to equity
- \( r \) is the required rate of return on equity

**Example**

Consider a stock with an expected annual FCFE of \$10 million and a required rate of return of 15%. The stock price can be calculated as:

$$
P = \frac{10,000,000}{0.15} = 66,666,667
$$

In summary, bonds and stocks are fundamental financial instruments with distinct characteristics and pricing models. Understanding these instruments and their pricing mechanisms is crucial for effective financial engineering and investment decision-making.

## Part 2: Mathematical Pricing Models

### 2.1 Binomial Tree Model

The binomial tree model is a discrete-time model used in financial engineering to price financial instruments, particularly options. It provides a method for calculating the price of an option by simulating the possible paths that the underlying asset's price can take. The model is based on the assumption that the price movement of the underlying asset can be modeled as a series of binary choices, where the asset price moves up or down at each step.

#### 2.1.1 Basic Concepts

**Node and Time Steps**

In a binomial tree model, each point in the tree represents a node, and the horizontal axis represents time steps. The tree is built by starting at the current time (node 0) and moving forward in time through a series of time steps (nodes 1, 2, 3, ...). At each node, the price of the underlying asset can move either up or down based on the risk-neutral probability.

**Risk-Neutral Probability**

The risk-neutral probability is a key concept in the binomial tree model. It is the probability that the underlying asset price will move up at each step, assuming that investors are risk-neutral and do not require additional compensation for risk. The risk-neutral probability is calculated based on the asset's drift rate and the risk-free interest rate.

#### 2.1.2 Pricing Under Binomial Tree

**Option Pricing**

The binomial tree model can be used to price European and American options. Here, we will focus on the pricing of European options.

1. **European Call Option**

The price of a European call option can be calculated by simulating the possible paths of the underlying asset's price and calculating the payoff at each node. The option price at each node can be expressed as:

$$
C_t = \max(S_t - K, 0)
$$

Where:
- \( C_t \) is the option price at time \( t \)
- \( S_t \) is the price of the underlying asset at time \( t \)
- \( K \) is the strike price of the option

The option price at the final node (time \( n \)) can be calculated as:

$$
C_n = \max(S_n - K, 0)
$$

The option price at the previous node (time \( n-1 \)) can be calculated using the risk-neutral probability:

$$
C_{n-1} = e^{-r \Delta t} \times C_n + (1 - e^{-r \Delta t}) \times 0
$$

This process is repeated iteratively until the option price is calculated at the initial node (time \( 0 \)).

2. **European Put Option**

The price of a European put option can be calculated similarly to the call option. The payoff at each node is given by:

$$
P_t = \max(K - S_t, 0)
$$

The option price at the final node:

$$
P_n = \max(K - S_n, 0)
$$

And at the previous node:

$$
P_{n-1} = e^{-r \Delta t} \times P_n + (1 - e^{-r \Delta t}) \times K
$$

**Pricing of Fixed Income Securities**

The binomial tree model can also be used to price fixed income securities, such as bonds. The price of a bond is the present value of its future cash flows, which include periodic coupon payments and the repayment of the face value at maturity.

The binomial tree model can simulate the potential paths of interest rates and calculate the present value of the bond's cash flows at each node. The bond price at each node can be calculated as:

$$
P_t = \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i \times e^{-r \Delta t_i}
$$

Where:
- \( P_t \) is the price of the bond at time \( t \)
- \( C_i \) is the cash flow at time \( i \)
- \( r \) is the yield to maturity
- \( \Delta t_i \) is the time period between time \( i-1 \) and time \( i \)

**Example**

Consider a European call option on a stock with a current price of \$100, a strike price of \$100, a risk-free interest rate of 5%, and a time to expiration of 1 year. Using a binomial tree with two time steps, we can calculate the option price as follows:

1. At time 0:
   - \( S_0 = 100 \)
   - \( u = S_0 \times (1 + \Delta u) = 100 \times 1.1 = 110 \)
   - \( d = S_0 \times (1 - \Delta u) = 100 \times 0.9 = 90 \)
   - \( p = \frac{e^{r \Delta t} - d}{u - d} = \frac{e^{0.05 \times 1} - 0.9}{1.1 - 0.9} = 0.625 \)
   - \( (1 - p) = 0.375 \)

2. At time 1 (up state):
   - \( S_1 = 110 \)
   - \( C_1 = \max(110 - 100, 0) = 10 \)

3. At time 1 (down state):
   - \( S_1 = 90 \)
   - \( C_1 = \max(90 - 100, 0) = 0 \)

4. At time 0:
   - \( C_0 = e^{-r \Delta t} \times C_1 \times p + (1 - e^{-r \Delta t}) \times C_1 \times (1 - p) \)
   - \( C_0 = e^{-0.05 \times 1} \times 10 \times 0.625 + (1 - e^{-0.05 \times 1}) \times 10 \times 0.375 \)
   - \( C_0 = 0.9537 \times 6.25 + 0.4463 \times 3.75 \)
   - \( C_0 = 5.8656 + 1.6618 \)
   - \( C_0 = 7.5274 \)

The binomial tree model provides a straightforward method for pricing options and fixed income securities. By simulating the possible paths of the underlying asset's price and calculating the present value of cash flows, financial engineers can determine the fair value of these instruments and make informed investment decisions.

### 2.2 Black-Scholes Model

The Black-Scholes model, developed by Fischer Black and Myron Scholes in 1973, is a pivotal model in financial engineering that allows for the pricing of European options. It has become a cornerstone in the field of financial mathematics, providing a robust framework for option valuation that relies on continuous-time stochastic processes. The Black-Scholes model has had a profound impact on the financial industry, enabling the development of more sophisticated financial instruments and facilitating the management of market risks.

#### 2.2.1 Theoretical Background

**Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM)**

The Black-Scholes model is built upon the concept of geometric Brownian motion (GBM), which describes the random walk behavior of stock prices. GBM is a continuous-time stochastic process where the underlying asset price evolves according to the following stochastic differential equation (SDE):

$$
dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t
$$

Where:
- \( S_t \) is the price of the underlying asset at time \( t \)
- \( \mu \) is the drift coefficient, representing the expected return of the asset
- \( \sigma \) is the volatility coefficient, representing the variability of the asset's returns
- \( dW_t \) is the increment of a Wiener process (Brownian motion) over the interval \([t, t+dt]\)

The solution to the GBM SDE is given by the log-normal distribution:

$$
\ln(S_t) \sim N\left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}, \sigma^2 \right)
$$

**Risk-Neutral Measure**

A crucial component of the Black-Scholes model is the concept of the risk-neutral measure, which allows for the pricing of financial derivatives without directly considering the risks associated with the underlying asset. Under the risk-neutral measure, the expected return of the underlying asset is equal to the risk-free interest rate, assuming that all market participants are risk-neutral.

The risk-neutral probability, denoted by \( q \), can be derived from the following equation:

$$
q = \frac{e^{\rho - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}}}{1 + \frac{\sigma^2}{2} - \rho}
$$

Where:
- \( \rho \) is the risk-free interest rate

By using the risk-neutral measure, the expected return of the underlying asset becomes \( r \), and the GBM SDE can be rewritten as:

$$
dS_t = r S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t
$$

#### 2.2.2 The Black-Scholes Formula

The Black-Scholes formula provides a closed-form solution for the price of a European call option:

$$
C(S_t, t) = S_t N(d_1) - K e^{-r(t-t_0)} N(d_2)
$$

Where:
- \( C(S_t, t) \) is the price of a European call option at time \( t \) with underlying asset price \( S_t \)
- \( K \) is the strike price of the option
- \( r \) is the risk-free interest rate
- \( t \) is the time to expiration
- \( t_0 \) is the current time
- \( N(\cdot) \) is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution
- \( d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_t/K) + (r + \frac{\sigma^2}{2})(t-t_0)}{\sigma \sqrt{t-t_0}} \)
- \( d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{t-t_0} \)

The price of a European put option can be derived from the call option price using the put-call parity relationship:

$$
P(S_t, t) = C(S_t, t) - S_t + K e^{-r(t-t_0)}
$$

**Example**

Consider a European call option on a stock with a current price of \$100, a strike price of \$100, a risk-free interest rate of 5%, a volatility of 20%, and a time to expiration of 1 year. Using the Black-Scholes formula, we can calculate the option price as follows:

$$
d_1 = \frac{\ln(100/100) + (0.05 + 0.2^2/2) \times (1-0)}{0.2 \times \sqrt{1-0}} = 1.4495
$$

$$
d_2 = 1.4495 - 0.2 \times \sqrt{1-0} = 1.2495
$$

$$
C = 100 \times N(1.4495) - 100 \times e^{-0.05 \times 1} \times N(1.2495)
$$

$$
C = 100 \times 0.9332 - 100 \times 0.8860 = 7.32
$$

The Black-Scholes model provides a powerful tool for pricing European options by incorporating the underlying asset's price, strike price, risk-free interest rate, volatility, and time to expiration. By using the risk-neutral measure and the log-normal distribution, it enables financial engineers to derive the fair value of options and make informed investment decisions.

### 2.3 Stochastic Calculus in Financial Engineering

Stochastic calculus is a branch of mathematics that deals with stochastic processes, particularly those involving randomness and uncertainty. In financial engineering, stochastic calculus is fundamental for modeling and analyzing the behavior of financial markets, where the prices of assets are inherently random and influenced by numerous factors. This section will delve into the basics of stochastic calculus and its applications in financial engineering, focusing on Ito's lemma and stochastic differential equations (SDEs).

#### 2.3.1 Ito's Lemma

Ito's lemma is a key result in stochastic calculus that provides a formula for differentiating a function with respect to a stochastic process. It is particularly useful in financial engineering for pricing and risk management of financial derivatives. Ito's lemma relates the differential of a function \( f(S_t, t) \) with respect to time \( t \) and the stochastic process \( S_t \), which follows a geometric Brownian motion (GBM):

$$
dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t
$$

where \( W_t \) is a Wiener process (Brownian motion).

Ito's lemma states that if \( f(S_t, t) \) is a twice continuously differentiable function of both \( S_t \) and \( t \), then its differential is given by:

$$
df(S_t, t) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} dt + \frac{\partial f}{\partial S_t} dS_t + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S_t^2} (\sigma S_t)^2 dt
$$

Substituting the GBM SDE into Ito's lemma, we get:

$$
df(S_t, t) = \mu S_t \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} dt + \sigma S_t \frac{\partial f}{\partial S_t} dW_t + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S_t^2} dt
$$

This formula allows financial engineers to calculate the evolution of prices and other quantities under stochastic dynamics. For example, if \( f(S_t, t) = S_t \), we recover the GBM SDE itself.

#### 2.3.2 Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs)

Stochastic differential equations are equations that describe the evolution of a stochastic process in terms of its current state, a known function of that state, and a stochastic differential term. In financial engineering, SDEs are used to model the dynamics of asset prices and other financial variables. The general form of an SDE is:

$$
dX_t = a(X_t) dt + b(X_t) dW_t
$$

where \( X_t \) is the stochastic process, \( a(X_t) \) and \( b(X_t) \) are functions of \( X_t \), and \( W_t \) is a Wiener process.

Solving SDEs can be challenging, especially for complex models. However, there are several numerical methods for approximating solutions, such as the Euler-Maruyama method and the Milstein method. These methods are crucial for implementing financial models on computers and for practical applications in finance.

**Example: Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM)**

As mentioned earlier, GBM is a common model for asset prices. It can be represented by the SDE:

$$
dS_t = (\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2})S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t
$$

This SDE can be solved explicitly to obtain the solution for \( S_t \):

$$
S_t = S_0 e^{(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2})t + \sigma W_t}
$$

This solution shows that the logarithm of the asset price follows a normal distribution with mean \( (\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2})t \) and variance \( \sigma^2 t \).

**Example: Interest Rate Model**

Another example of an SDE in financial engineering is the Vasicek model for interest rates, which is given by:

$$
dR_t = (\theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2})(R_t - r)^+ dt + \sigma (R_t - r)^+ dW_t
$$

where \( R_t \) is the interest rate at time \( t \), \( \theta \) is the long-run mean reversion level, \( r \) is the current interest rate, and \( (\cdot)^+ \) is the positive part function.

This SDE can be solved to obtain an explicit solution for \( R_t \), which shows that the interest rate reverts to its mean value over time, with volatility given by \( \sigma \).

In conclusion, stochastic calculus and SDEs are essential tools in financial engineering for modeling and analyzing the dynamics of financial markets. Ito's lemma provides a powerful framework for differentiating functions with respect to stochastic processes, while SDEs describe the evolution of financial variables over time. These concepts enable financial engineers to develop robust models for pricing and risk management in complex market environments.

### 2.3.3 Solving Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs) in Financial Engineering

Solving stochastic differential equations (SDEs) is a crucial task in financial engineering, as it allows for the accurate modeling and prediction of asset prices and other financial variables. Due to the inherent randomness and uncertainty in financial markets, exact solutions to SDEs are often impractical or impossible to obtain. Therefore, numerical methods are employed to approximate the solutions. This section will explore several numerical methods for solving SDEs and their applications in financial engineering.

#### Numerical Methods for SDEs

**Euler-Maruyama Method**

The Euler-Maruyama method is one of the most widely used numerical methods for solving SDEs. It is a simple and efficient approach that approximates the solution by discretizing the time and applying finite differences to the SDE.

The basic form of the Euler-Maruyama method for an SDE of the type:

$$
dX_t = a(X_t) dt + b(X_t) dW_t
$$

is given by:

$$
X_{n+1} = X_n + a(X_n) \Delta t + b(X_n) \sqrt{\Delta t} Z_n
$$

where \( Z_n \) is a standard normal random variable, and \( \Delta t \) is the time step.

**Example**

Consider the GBM SDE:

$$
dS_t = (\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2})S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t
$$

Using the Euler-Maruyama method, we can approximate the stock price at a future time \( t_{n+1} \) given the initial stock price \( S_0 \):

$$
S_{n+1} = S_n + (\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2})S_n \Delta t + \sigma S_n \sqrt{\Delta t} Z_n
$$

**Milstein Method**

The Milstein method is an extension of the Euler-Maruyama method that improves the accuracy of the numerical solution by correcting the discretization error. It is particularly useful for SDEs with non-linear drift and diffusion terms.

The Milstein method updates the solution as follows:

$$
X_{n+1} = X_n + a(X_n) \Delta t + b(X_n) \Delta W_n + \frac{1}{2} \left[ b^2(X_n) (\Delta W_n)^2 - b(X_n) \Delta t \right]
$$

where \( \Delta W_n = W_{n+1} - W_n \) is the increment of the Wiener process.

**Example**

For the GBM SDE, the Milstein method can be applied to obtain a more accurate approximation of the stock price:

$$
S_{n+1} = S_n + (\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2})S_n \Delta t + \sigma S_n \Delta W_n + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S_n (\Delta W_n)^2
$$

**Higher-Order Methods**

Higher-order methods, such as the Euler-Maruyama method with an improved scheme, the Milstein method, and more sophisticated schemes like the explicit Euler scheme, the implicit Euler scheme, and the Runge-Kutta methods, provide better accuracy and stability for solving SDEs. These methods involve more complex calculations but are essential for capturing the true behavior of financial markets in realistic scenarios.

One of the primary applications of SDE-solving methods in financial engineering is option pricing. Options are financial derivatives whose value depends on the price of the underlying asset, which follows a stochastic process. **Interest Rate Models**

### 3.1 Credit Risk Modeling

Credit risk is a crucial aspect of financial engineering, referring to the potential for financial loss arising from the failure of a borrower to meet their debt obligations. Effective credit risk modeling is essential for banks, financial institutions, and investors to assess and manage the risk associated with lending and investing in debt instruments such as bonds, loans, and credit default swaps (CDS). This section will discuss credit risk modeling, focusing on credit default swaps and their pricing.

#### 3.1.1 Credit Default Swap (CDS)

A credit default swap (CDS) is a financial derivative that provides insurance against the risk of default on a loan or bond. It functions similarly to an insurance policy, where the buyer (protection buyer) pays a series of premiums to the seller (protection seller) in exchange for a payoff if the underlying reference entity (borrower) defaults. CDSs are widely used by investors and banks to hedge credit risk and to gain exposure to the credit performance of various entities.

**Structure of a CDS**

A CDS typically involves the following components:

1. **Reference Entity**: The entity whose credit risk is being insured, such as a corporate borrower or a sovereign entity.
2. **Notional Amount**: The principal amount of the debt that is insured, which represents the potential exposure in the event of a default.
3. **Protection Buyer**: The entity purchasing the CDS, which seeks to protect itself against the default risk of the reference entity.
4. **Protection Seller**: The entity selling the CDS, which assumes the credit risk of the reference entity in exchange for the premiums.
5. **Premiums**: The periodic payments made by the protection buyer to the protection seller.
6. **Credit Event**: An event that triggers the CDS, such as a default, bankruptcy, or failure to meet certain credit ratings criteria.
7. **Default Loss**: The amount the protection buyer receives from the protection seller upon the occurrence of a credit event.

**Pricing of CDS**

The pricing of a CDS involves calculating the present value of the expected future cash flows associated with the CDS contract. These cash flows include the periodic premiums paid by the protection buyer and the potential loss in the event of a credit event.

The pricing model for a CDS typically involves the following steps:

1. **Risk-Free Rate**: The risk-free rate is used to discount the future cash flows to their present value. It represents the return an investor can earn on a risk-free investment, such as a government bond.
2. **Credit Risk Premium**: The credit risk premium is the additional return required by investors to compensate for the risk of default. It is determined by factors such as the credit rating of the reference entity, market conditions, and economic factors.
3. **Expected Loss**: The expected loss is the expected amount of the default loss that the protection seller will pay to the protection buyer. It is calculated based on the probability of default and the potential loss given default.
4. **CDS Spread**: The CDS spread is the credit risk premium expressed as a percentage of the notional amount. It represents the cost of insurance against default risk.

The price of a CDS can be calculated using the following formula:

$$
Price_{CDS} = Notional \times (1 - e^{-\int_{t_0}^{t_f} \sigma \, ds}) \times e^{-rt_f}
$$

where:
- \( Price_{CDS} \) is the price of the CDS
- \( Notional \) is the notional amount of the CDS
- \( \sigma \) is the credit risk premium
- \( t_0 \) is the starting date of the CDS
- \( t_f \) is the expiration date of the CDS
- \( r \) is the risk-free interest rate

**Example**

Consider a CDS with a notional amount of \$10 million, a credit risk premium of 2% per annum, and a risk-free interest rate of 3% per annum. The CDS has a maturity of 5 years. Using the formula above, we can calculate the price of the CDS as follows:

$$
Price_{CDS} = 10,000,000 \times (1 - e^{-0.02 \times 5}) \times e^{-0.03 \times 5}
$$

$$
Price_{CDS} = 10,000,000 \times (1 - e^{-0.1}) \times e^{-0.15}
$$

$$
Price_{CDS} = 10,000,000 \times (1 - 0.9048) \times 0.8574
$$

$$
Price_{CDS} = 10,000,000 \times 0.0952 \times 0.8574
$$

$$
Price_{CDS} = \$843,760
$$

This means that the protection buyer would pay approximately \$843,760 in premiums over the 5-year life of the CDS to protect against the default risk of the reference entity.

In summary, credit risk modeling is essential for managing the potential financial losses associated with lending and investing in debt instruments. Credit default swaps are a powerful tool for managing credit risk, and their pricing relies on accurate modeling of the risk premium and the probability of default. By understanding the structure and pricing of CDSs, financial engineers can design effective strategies to manage credit risk in their portfolios.

```python
plt.hist(price_cds_sensitivity, bins=50, alpha=0.75)
plt.xlabel('CDS Price')
plt.ylabel('Frequency')
plt.title('CDS Price Distribution with Varying Credit Ratings')
plt.show()
```

The results reveal that as the credit rating decreases, the average CDS price generally increases, reflecting the increased credit risk associated with lower ratings. The distribution of CDS prices is skewed to the right, indicating that lower-rated issuers tend to have higher CDS prices.

By conducting sensitivity analysis, we gain insights into how changes in credit risk factors affect CDS pricing probabilistically. This analysis is invaluable for financial engineers and investors in making informed decisions about credit risk management and portfolio allocation.
