#### Chapter 1: Fundamentals of Mathematics

In this chapter, we will explore the foundational concepts of mathematics that serve as the building blocks for more advanced topics. These concepts include numbers, operations, set theory, logic, and combinatorics. Each of these topics is essential for understanding the mathematical principles that underpin musical structures and analysis.

**1.1 Basic Mathematical Concepts**

**Numbers and Operations**

The concept of numbers is central to mathematics, and it plays a crucial role in understanding musical notation. In music, numbers represent durations and intervals, and operations such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division are used to analyze and manipulate these values.

- **Natural Numbers**: The natural numbers are the counting numbers {1, 2, 3, ...}. They are used to denote the length of notes and rests in musical notation.
- **Integers**: Integers include all natural numbers, zero, and their negatives {..., -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ...}. In music, integers can represent note durations, such as triplets or dotted notes.
- **Rational Numbers**: Rational numbers are numbers that can be expressed as a fraction of two integers, such as 1/2 or 3/4. These numbers are often used to denote rhythmic values, such as quarter notes and eighth notes.
- **Real Numbers**: Real numbers include all rational and irrational numbers. In music, real numbers can represent tempo, duration, and dynamic levels.

**Operations**

Basic arithmetic operations form the foundation of mathematical analysis. These operations include:

- **Addition**: The sum of two numbers. In music, addition is used to combine durations, such as adding two quarter notes to create a half note.
- **Subtraction**: The difference between two numbers. In music, subtraction can be used to shorten a note's duration, such as subtracting an eighth note from a quarter note.
- **Multiplication**: The product of two numbers. In music, multiplication is used to extend note durations, such as multiplying a quarter note by two to create a half note.
- **Division**: The quotient of two numbers. In music, division can be used to divide a note's duration, such as dividing a half note by two to create two quarter notes.

**1.2 Functions and Relations**

**Functions**

A function is a relation that assigns to each element of a set a unique element of another set. In music, functions can represent relationships between different elements of a musical composition.

- **One-to-One Function**: A function where each element in the domain is mapped to a unique element in the codomain. In music, a one-to-one function can represent the relationship between a note and its duration.
- **Onto Function**: A function where every element in the codomain is mapped to by at least one element in the domain. In music, an onto function can represent the relationship between a tempo and the total duration of a piece.
- **Bijective Function**: A function that is both one-to-one and onto. In music, a bijective function can represent the relationship between a musical phrase and its structure.

**Relations**

A relation is a set of ordered pairs. In music, relations can represent the connections between different musical elements.

- **Symmetric Relation**: A relation where if (a, b) is in the relation, then (b, a) is also in the relation. In music, a symmetric relation can represent the equality of intervals, such as the relationship between a major third and a minor sixth.
- **Transitive Relation**: A relation where if (a, b) and (b, c) are in the relation, then (a, c) is also in the relation. In music, a transitive relation can represent the sequence of chords in a progression, where each chord follows a specific interval pattern.

**1.3 Combinatorics Basics**

**Combinatorics**

Combinatorics is the study of counting and arranging objects. In music, combinatorial principles are used to analyze and create musical structures.

- **Permutations**: A permutation is an arrangement of objects in a specific order. In music, permutations can represent the different ways a set of notes can be ordered, such as in a melody or an ostinato pattern.
- **Combinations**: A combination is a selection of objects from a set, without regard to order. In music, combinations can represent the different ways a set of notes can be selected, such as in a chord or an accompaniment figure.
- **Binomial Coefficients**: Binomial coefficients are used to calculate the number of ways to choose a subset of objects from a larger set. In music, binomial coefficients can be used to determine the number of possible inversions for a chord or the number of different rhythms that can be used to fill a given duration.

In summary, the basic concepts of mathematics, including numbers and operations, functions and relations, and combinatorics, provide a foundation for understanding and analyzing the elements of music. These mathematical principles help us to appreciate the complexity and beauty of musical structures, and they offer tools for composers and musicians to explore new forms of expression.

### Introduction to Music Theory

In this chapter, we delve into the fundamental concepts of music theory, which are essential for understanding the structure and organization of music. These concepts include notes, scales, keys, rhythm, meter, harmony, and melody. By exploring these elements, we can gain a deeper appreciation for the art of music and its rich historical context.

**2.1 Music Theory Essentials**

**Notes**

Notes are the basic units of music, representing specific frequencies that the human ear can hear. Each note is named according to its pitch, which is determined by its frequency. The notes are typically organized into a scale, which consists of a sequence of semitones and tones. The most common scale in Western music is the major scale, which is composed of seven distinct notes.

- **Semitones**: Semitones are half-step intervals between notes. In the major scale, there are five semitones.
- **Tones**: Tones are whole-step intervals between notes. In the major scale, there are four tones.
- **Whole Notes**: A whole note represents the longest duration in the standard notation system, equal to four beats.
- **Half Notes**: A half note represents half the duration of a whole note, equal to two beats.
- **Quarter Notes**: A quarter note represents one-fourth the duration of a whole note, equal to one beat.
- **Eighth Notes**: An eighth note represents one-eighth the duration of a whole note, equal to one-quarter of a beat.
- **Sixteenth Notes**: A sixteenth note represents one-sixteenth the duration of a whole note, equal to one-eighth of a beat.

**Scales**

Scales are sequences of notes that form the foundation of musical compositions. The most common scales include the major scale, the minor scale, and various modes derived from these scales.

- **Major Scale**: The major scale consists of seven notes, starting with the root note and following the pattern tone-tone-semitone-tone-tone-tone-tone.
- **Minor Scale**: The minor scale also consists of seven notes, but it follows the pattern tone-semitone-tone-tone-semitone-tone-tone-tone.
- **Modes**: Modes are variations of the major and minor scales, each with its own unique character and harmonic content. Examples include the Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, and Locrian modes.

**Keys**

A key is a set of notes that is used as the basis for a musical composition. It is determined by the root note of the scale being used. Each key has a specific set of sharps or flats that must be used when notating music in that key.

- **Major Keys**: Major keys are identified by their tonality, which is characterized by a bright and uplifting sound. The key signature of a major key indicates the number of sharps or flats needed to construct the major scale.
- **Minor Keys**: Minor keys are identified by their tonality, which is characterized by a darker and more melancholic sound. The key signature of a minor key indicates the number of sharps or flats needed to construct the natural minor scale.

**Rhythm and Meter**

Rhythm refers to the pattern of long and short notes that form the pulse of a piece of music. Meter, on the other hand, is the regular groupings of beats into measures. Understanding rhythm and meter is crucial for interpreting and performing music accurately.

- **Simple Meter**: Simple meter has a regular pattern of two or three beats per measure, indicated by a time signature such as 2/4 or 3/4.
- **Compound Meter**: Compound meter has a more complex pattern of beats, usually grouped in threes or sixes, indicated by a time signature such as 6/8 or 9/8.

**Harmony**

Harmony is the use of simultaneous notes to create a rich and complex sound. It is based on the relationships between the notes of a scale and the chords that can be derived from those notes.

- **Chords**: Chords are groups of three or more notes that are played together. The most common chords are the major chord, the minor chord, and the dominant seventh chord.
- **Inversions**: Chords can be played in different inversions, which are different arrangements of the same chord tones. For example, a C major chord can be played as a root position chord, a first inversion chord, or a second inversion chord.

**Melody**

Melody is the sequence of musical notes that forms the main musical idea of a piece. It is often the most memorable aspect of a composition and can evoke emotions and convey messages.

- **Contour**: The contour of a melody refers to the overall shape or pattern of its notes, including their highs and lows. A melody with a rising contour might sound uplifting, while a descending contour might sound melancholic.
- **Range**: The range of a melody is the difference between its highest and lowest notes. A wide range can create a dramatic effect, while a narrow range can sound more intimate.

**2.2 Historical Perspectives**

**The Evolution of Western Music Theory**

The development of Western music theory can be traced back to ancient civilizations, such as Greece and Egypt, where music was closely intertwined with religious and cultural practices. During the Middle Ages, music theory began to take shape as a distinct field of study, with the introduction of the concept of the musical mode. The Renaissance period saw further advancements, with the development of notation systems and the creation of complex polyphonic compositions.

In the Baroque period, music theory expanded to include the study of harmony and counterpoint, leading to the creation of complex compositions with intricate musical structures. The Classical period introduced more formal structures and balance in compositions, while the Romantic period emphasized emotional expression and individualism.

**The Influence of Mathematics on Music**

The relationship between mathematics and music has been recognized for centuries, with many scholars exploring the connections between geometric structures and musical forms. Pythagoras, a Greek philosopher and mathematician, was one of the first to propose that the harmony of the universe could be understood through mathematical principles.

During the Renaissance, mathematicians such as Johannes Kepler and Galileo Galilei explored the relationship between music and astronomy, attempting to uncover the "music of the spheres." In the 18th century, the German philosopher and composer Georg Friedrich Händel developed the theory of the "Great Eight Division," which attempted to explain the underlying mathematical principles of musical harmony.

In the 19th and 20th centuries, the study of mathematics and music continued to evolve, with mathematicians such as Henri Poincaré and David Hilbert contributing to the development of set theory and group theory, which have been applied to music theory and analysis.

**Non-Western Musical Systems**

Music theory is not limited to Western traditions; non-Western musical systems have their own unique structures and concepts. In Indian classical music, for example, music is organized into rhythmic cycles called talas and melodic modes called ragas. In African music, rhythms are often based on complex polyrhythms, and melodic structures are derived from call-and-response patterns.

In the Middle East, music theory is based on the maqam system, which consists of various modes and scales used to create intricate melodic and harmonic structures. In Japan, music theory is influenced by the concept of on (interval) and oku (rhythm), which are used to create beautiful and expressive melodies.

In summary, the study of music theory provides a framework for understanding the elements of music and their relationships. From the basic concepts of notes and scales to the complex structures of harmony and melody, music theory offers a window into the creative processes of composers and musicians. By exploring the historical and cultural contexts of music theory, we can gain a deeper appreciation for the diversity and richness of human musical expression.

#### 3.1 Geometric Representation of Music

Geometry has long been recognized as a fundamental tool for visualizing musical concepts. One of the most apparent geometric representations in music is the circle, which is often used to represent rhythmic patterns.

**Circular Geometry and Rhythm**

Rhythm, the organization of long and short sounds in music, can be visualized using a circle. In circular geometry, a circle represents a continuous, unending cycle, which is analogous to the repetitive nature of many rhythmic patterns.

- **Rhythmic Cycles**: A rhythmic cycle is a repetitive pattern of notes that forms the basis of much music. For example, a common rhythmic cycle is the 4/4 meter, where a quarter note receives one beat, and each measure contains four beats. This can be visualized on a circle, with each section of the circle representing a beat.
- **Rotations and Reflections**: In music, rhythmic patterns can be manipulated through rotations and reflections. A rotation shifts the pattern around the circle, while a reflection reverses the pattern. These operations can create complex and intricate rhythms.

**Linear Geometry and Melody**

While circles are useful for visualizing rhythm, linear geometry is often used to represent melody. A linear representation of music can help to illustrate the relationships between different notes and their intervals.

- **Interval Representation**: Intervals, which represent the distance between two notes, can be visualized on a linear staff. For example, a major third can be represented as three whole steps on a staff.
- **Melodic Shape**: The shape of a melody, including its contours and inflections, can be mapped onto a linear geometric structure. This can help composers and musicians to analyze and understand the structure of a melody.

**Group Theory and Musical Forms**

Group theory, a branch of mathematics that studies the properties of symmetry and transformation, provides a powerful tool for analyzing musical forms and structures.

- **Symmetry Groups**: In music, symmetry groups can be used to analyze and classify musical forms. For example, the symmetry group D4 can be used to analyze a musical form that has four identical sections, each followed by a section that is a mirror image of the first.
- **Transformation Groups**: Transformation groups are used to study how musical structures can be transformed while maintaining their essential characteristics. For example, a musical phrase can be transposed, inverted, or retrograded while still retaining its meaning and function.

**3.2 Fractals and Aesthetics**

Fractals, geometric shapes that exhibit self-similarity at different scales, have gained popularity in music theory for their ability to describe the complex and often irregular patterns found in musical compositions.

- **Self-Similarity**: Fractals are characterized by self-similarity, meaning that parts of the shape resemble the whole. In music, this can be seen in the repetition of rhythmic or melodic motifs at different levels of complexity.
- **Aesthetic Appeal**: Fractals have a unique aesthetic quality that can evoke a sense of beauty and wonder. This quality can be applied to music to create compositions that are both structurally complex and aesthetically pleasing.

**3.3 Applications in Music Composition**

The application of geometric and group theory in music composition can lead to innovative and intricate compositions.

- **Geometric Structures**: Composers can use geometric structures to create complex rhythms and melodies. For example, a melody can be constructed using the notes that lie on the edges of a fractal shape.
- **Symmetry and Invariance**: By analyzing symmetry groups, composers can create compositions that maintain their structure under various transformations. This can lead to music that is both versatile and captivating.

#### Number Theory and Musical Scales

In the realm of music, the relationship between mathematics and musical scales is particularly intriguing. This chapter will delve into the fundamental concepts of number theory and explore how these concepts underpin the development of different musical scales, from the just interval to the tempered scale.

**4.1 Number Theory Fundamentals**

**Prime Numbers and Divisibility**

Prime numbers are a cornerstone of number theory, as they are the building blocks of all other integers. A prime number is a natural number greater than 1 that is divisible only by 1 and itself. For example, 2, 3, 5, and 7 are prime numbers.

In music, prime numbers are significant because they represent the simplest intervals. The most common prime interval in Western music is the octave, which is defined as the interval between two notes that are exactly twice the frequency of each other. This corresponds to a ratio of 2:1 in terms of frequency.

Divisibility, another fundamental concept in number theory, is also relevant to music. When a note is played, its frequency can be divided by various prime numbers to produce harmonious intervals. For example, the frequency of a note can be divided by 3 to produce a perfect fifth (3:2 ratio) and by 5 to produce a perfect major third (5:4 ratio).

**Modular Arithmetic**

Modular arithmetic is a system of arithmetic for integers, where numbers "wrap around" after reaching a certain value. This system is often represented using a modulus, which is a positive integer. For example, in modulo 12 arithmetic, the numbers wrap around after reaching 12.

In music, modular arithmetic can be used to analyze and construct scales. For example, the chromatic scale consists of 12 notes, and each note can be represented as an integer from 0 to 11 in modulo 12 arithmetic. This system allows for the analysis of patterns and relationships between notes, such as the cyclical nature of harmonics.

**Number Theory Applications in Music**

Number theory is not only fundamental to the construction of musical scales but also plays a role in the analysis of musical harmonics and tuning systems.

- **Harmonics**: The harmonic series is a sequence of frequencies that arise when a musical instrument is played. The first harmonic is the fundamental frequency, and subsequent harmonics are whole number multiples of the fundamental. In music, harmonics produce harmonious sounds when played together, as their frequencies are related by simple integer ratios.
- **Tuning Systems**: Different tuning systems use various mathematical principles to determine the relationship between notes. For example, the just intonation system, which was used historically, is based on the harmonic series and uses simple integer ratios to define intervals. In contrast, the tempered scale, which is the most commonly used tuning system today, uses a slightly more complex system to allow for more flexible composition and performance.

**4.2 Musical Scales and Their Mathematical Underpinnings**

**Just Intervals and Tuning Systems**

Just intonation is a tuning system that uses simple integer ratios to define intervals. The most important just intervals are the unison (1:1), the perfect fifth (3:2), and the perfect fourth (4:3). These intervals form the basis of many just tunings, where each interval is tuned to its just ratio.

- **Pythagorean Tuning**: The Pythagorean tuning system is one of the earliest forms of just intonation. It uses the just ratios for the major third (5:4) and the perfect fifth (3:2). While Pythagorean tuning produces beautiful harmonies, it is not practical for all musical compositions due to the resulting dissonance in some intervals.
- **Mean Tone Tuning**: Mean tone tuning is a compromise between Pythagorean tuning and equal temperament. It uses the mean of the two ratios for the major third and the perfect fifth, resulting in a more harmonious sound but still slightly different from equal temperament.

**Tempered Scales**

The tempered scale is a tuning system that divides the octave into a fixed number of equal intervals, called semitones. The most common tempered scale is the twelve-tone equal temperament (ET), which divides the octave into twelve semitones.

- **Equal Temperament**: Equal temperament allows for consistent tuning across all keys, making it practical for both composition and performance. Despite the fact that equal temperament does not produce just intonation, it provides a stable and predictable tuning system that has become the standard for Western music.
- **Theory of Equal Temperament**: The theory of equal temperament is based on the idea that the frequency ratio of a semitone is the twelfth root of two (approximately 1.059463). This ratio allows for the consistent division of the octave into twelve equal parts, resulting in a system where all twelve major thirds are slightly flatter than the just major third (5:4).

### Harmonic Analysis and Composition

Harmonic analysis and composition are vital components of music theory that allow musicians and composers to analyze and create complex musical structures. In this chapter, we will delve into the principles of harmonic analysis and explore various techniques and models used in composition. By understanding these concepts, we can gain a deeper appreciation for the intricate relationships between harmony and melody.

#### 5.1 Harmonic Analysis

**Harmonic Progression**

Harmonic progression refers to the sequential arrangement of chords in a piece of music. The progression of chords establishes the tonality and harmony of a composition. There are several common harmonic progressions, each with its own unique characteristics and functions.

- **Ionian Progression**: The Ionian progression is the most common type and is characterized by the I-IV-V-I chord progression in the key of C major. This progression is often used in popular music and is considered diatonic because it uses only the notes of the major scale.
- **Dorian Progression**: The Dorian progression uses the Dorian mode, starting with the tonic and following the pattern tone-semitone-tone-tone-semitone-tone-tone-tone. A typical Dorian progression is ii-V-I.
- **Phrygian Progression**: The Phrygian progression starts with the flat second note of the major scale, creating a distinctively dark and melodic progression.
- **Lydian Progression**: The Lydian progression has a bright and open sound, characterized by the use of the sharp fourth note in the major scale.
- **Mixolydian Progression**: The Mixolydian progression is similar to the Ionian progression but has a slightly different final chord, often resulting in a more open and less conclusive sound.

**Voice Leading**

Voice leading is the process of moving between chords in a way that maintains a clear and pleasing melody. Effective voice leading involves ensuring that the intervals between notes in the melody and the chords they represent are harmonious and smooth.

- **Parallel Motion**: Parallel motion occurs when two or more voices move in the same direction, either up or down, in a chord progression. This can create a sense of stability and continuity in the music.
- **Oblique Motion**: Oblique motion occurs when two or more voices move in different directions, creating a dynamic and flexible sound. This can add interest and complexity to a piece of music.
- **Contrary Motion**: Contrary motion occurs when two or more voices move in opposite directions. This can create a strong sense of direction and momentum in the music.

**Analysis of Complex Harmonic Structures**

Complex harmonic structures involve the use of advanced harmonic devices and advanced chord progressions. These structures can include modes other than the major and minor scales, non-diatonic chords, and extended chord functions.

- ** modes**: Modes are variations of the major and minor scales, each with its own unique character and harmonic content. Examples include the Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, and Locrian modes. These modes can be used to create diverse harmonic textures and melodies.
- **Non-Diatonic Chords**: Non-diatonic chords are chords that do not belong to the diatonic scale of the current key. They are often used to introduce dissonance and create tension, which is resolved by returning to diatonic chords.
- **Extended Chord Functions**: Extended chord functions include seventh chords, ninth chords, eleventh chords, and thirteenth chords. These chords add additional intervals to the basic triad, creating richer harmonic textures and more complex progressions.

#### 5.2 Composition Techniques

**Formal Structures in Music**

Formal structures in music refer to the organization of a piece into distinct sections or movements. Common formal structures include binary form, ternary form, sonata form, and rondo form.

- **Binary Form**: Binary form consists of two main sections, typically A and B. The A section is often a melody or theme, while the B section offers a contrasting melody or harmony. This form is simple and can be easily understood by listeners.
- **Ternary Form**: Ternary form consists of three main sections, typically A-B-A. The A section introduces a melody or theme, the B section offers a contrasting melody or harmony, and the A section returns to conclude the piece.
- **Sonata Form**: Sonata form is a complex structure typically used in instrumental music, particularly in sonatas, symphonies, and concertos. It consists of three main sections: an exposition, a development, and a recapitulation. The exposition introduces the main themes and harmonic progressions, the development explores these elements in a more complex and intricate way, and the recapitulation returns to the original themes but in a transformed manner.
- **Rondo Form**: Rondo form consists of a main theme (A) that is repeated several times, with contrasting sections (B, C, D, etc.) inserted between repetitions of the theme. This form is known for its lively and varied character.

**Aesthetic Principles in Composition**

Aesthetic principles in composition refer to the principles that guide the creation of aesthetically pleasing and emotionally impactful music. These principles include balance, contrast, unity, and variety.

- **Balance**: Balance refers to the equal distribution of musical elements, such as melody, harmony, and rhythm, to create a cohesive and harmonious sound.
- **Contrast**: Contrast involves the use of opposing elements to create interest and depth. For example, a soft melody can be contrasted with a loud and aggressive melody to create a dynamic and engaging musical experience.
- **Unity**: Unity refers to the coherence and integration of different elements within a composition. A well-integrated composition has a sense of harmony and coherence, even when using diverse musical elements.
- **Variety**: Variety involves the use of different musical elements and techniques to create a rich and diverse musical texture. This can include varying the melody, harmony, rhythm, and form to keep the listener engaged and interested.

**Mathematical Models**

Mathematical models can be used to analyze and create complex musical compositions. These models use mathematical principles to describe the relationships between different musical elements and their organization.

- **Fractal Models**: Fractal models are used to describe the self-similar and complex structures found in music. These models can be used to analyze and create melodies and rhythms that exhibit fractal patterns.
- **Waveform Models**: Waveform models use mathematical equations to describe the shape of sound waves, which can be used to analyze and manipulate musical sounds.
- **Set Theory Models**: Set theory models use mathematical set theory to analyze the relationships between different musical elements, such as notes, chords, and scales.

In conclusion, harmonic analysis and composition are essential aspects of music theory that allow musicians and composers to create complex and emotionally impactful music. By understanding the principles of harmonic progression, voice leading, and complex harmonic structures, as well as the techniques and aesthetic principles of composition, musicians can develop a deeper appreciation for the art of music and create compositions that resonate with listeners.

### Mathematical Modeling of Musical Structures

#### 6.1 Difference Equations in Music

Difference equations are mathematical equations that describe the differences between consecutive values in a sequence. These equations can be used to model various aspects of music, such as rhythms and melodies.

**6.1.1 Simple Difference Equation Model**

A simple difference equation can be used to model the rhythm of a drumbeat. Consider the following difference equation:

$$ R_{n+1} = R_n + d $$

where \( R_n \) represents the rhythm at time step \( n \), and \( d \) is a constant representing the duration of each beat.

For example, if \( d = 1 \), the rhythm at each time step would increase by one, representing a steady beat. This model can be extended to more complex rhythms by incorporating additional parameters or by using more sophisticated difference equations.

**6.1.2 Applications in Music**

One application of difference equations in music is in modeling polyrhythms. Polyrhythms are rhythms where different groups of notes or beats are played simultaneously at different speeds. For example, a common polyrhythm is the 3-4 polyrhythm, where three notes are played for every four beats.

To model a 3-4 polyrhythm, we can use two difference equations, one for each group of notes:

$$ R_{3n+1} = R_{3n} + 3d $$
$$ R_{4n+1} = R_{4n} + 4d $$

where \( d \) is the duration of each beat.

#### 6.2 Fourier Analysis and Harmonic Series

Fourier analysis is a mathematical tool used to decompose periodic signals into their constituent frequencies, which can be applied to analyze musical sounds. The Fourier series represents a periodic signal as a sum of sine and cosine waves with different frequencies and amplitudes.

**6.2.1 Basic Principles of Fourier Analysis**

A Fourier series for a periodic signal \( x(t) \) with period \( T \) can be expressed as:

$$ x(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( a_n \cos(2\pi n f_0 t) + b_n \sin(2\pi n f_0 t) \right) $$

where \( a_0, a_n, \) and \( b_n \) are the Fourier coefficients, and \( f_0 = \frac{1}{T} \) is the fundamental frequency.

The Fourier coefficients can be calculated using the following integrals:

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^{T} x(t) dt $$
$$ a_n = \frac{2}{T} \int_{0}^{T} x(t) \cos(2\pi n f_0 t) dt $$
$$ b_n = \frac{2}{T} \int_{0}^{T} x(t) \sin(2\pi n f_0 t) dt $$

**6.2.2 Harmonic Series and Music**

The harmonic series, which is the sequence of frequencies of the harmonics of a fundamental frequency, is closely related to Fourier analysis. Each harmonic is a multiple of the fundamental frequency, and the amplitudes of the harmonics determine the timbre or quality of the sound.

For a pure tone, the harmonic series consists of harmonics with integer multiples of the fundamental frequency. The first harmonic (fundamental) has an amplitude of 1, the second harmonic has an amplitude of 0.7071, the third harmonic has an amplitude of 0.5000, and so on.

**Example: Harmonic Series of a Sine Wave**

Consider a sine wave with a fundamental frequency of \( f_0 \). The Fourier series for this wave would consist only of the fundamental frequency with an amplitude of 1:

$$ x(t) = \sin(2\pi f_0 t) $$

The harmonic series for this wave would include only the first harmonic:

$$ x(t) = \sin(2\pi f_0 t) + 0 \cdot \sin(4\pi f_0 t) + 0 \cdot \sin(6\pi f_0 t) + \ldots $$

#### 6.3 Graph Theory in Music

Graph theory, a branch of mathematics that deals with the study of graphs, can be applied to analyze the structure of musical compositions. Graphs can represent the relationships between different elements of a piece, such as notes, chords, or sections.

**6.3.1 Network Representation of Music**

A musical composition can be represented as a network, where nodes represent musical elements (such as notes, chords, or sections) and edges represent relationships between these elements. For example, a network can represent the progression of chords in a piece, with nodes representing the chords and edges representing the transitions between chords.

**6.3.2 Applications of Graph Theory**

Graph theory can be used to analyze various aspects of music, such as:

- **Complexity Analysis**: By analyzing the connectivity of a musical network, we can determine the complexity of a piece. For example, a piece with a highly interconnected network may be considered more complex and intricate.
- **Community Detection**: Graph theory can be used to identify groups of related elements within a musical network. This can help in understanding the organization and structure of a piece.
- **Path Analysis**: By analyzing paths in a musical network, we can study the transitions between different parts of a piece and identify patterns in these transitions.

**Example: Graph Representation of a Musical Form**

```python
from music21 import harmony

def harmonic_analysis(notes, chords):
    analysis = []
    for note in notes:
        chord = note.chord
        if chord is not None:
            chord_analysis = harmony.Analysis(chord)
            analysis.append(chord_analysis)
    return analysis
```

**7.2.3 Rhythm Analysis**

To analyze the rhythm of the music, we will use a difference equation model to calculate the rhythm at each time step. We will also extract the duration of each note and chord.

```python
import numpy as np

def rhythm_analysis(notes, chords):
    rhythms = []
    for note in notes:
        rhythms.append(note.duration.quarterLength)
    for chord in chords:
        rhythms.append(chord.duration.quarterLength)
    return np.array(rhythms)
```
