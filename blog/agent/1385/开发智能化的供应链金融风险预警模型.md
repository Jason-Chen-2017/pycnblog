#### 1.1 Background of Supply Chain Finance

The concept of supply chain finance has evolved significantly over the past few decades. It encompasses a broad range of financial instruments and processes designed to enhance the liquidity and efficiency of the supply chain. Initially, it was primarily about managing the payment and receivable cycles within the supply chain, but as the complexity of global supply chains has increased, it has expanded to include risk management, inventory financing, and trade finance.

The evolution of supply chain finance can be traced back to the early 2000s when technology advancements and the rise of e-commerce made supply chains more intricate. Managing a supply chain involves dealing with various challenges such as demand volatility, supply disruptions, inventory management, and payment delays. #### 3.3 Mathematical Models and Formulations

**Basic Mathematical Models**

The development of intelligent supply chain finance risk预警 models involves the use of various mathematical models. Basic mathematical models used in risk analysis include:

1. **Probability Distributions**: Probability distributions are used to model the likelihood of different outcomes in a risk scenario. Common distributions include normal distribution, Poisson distribution, and binomial distribution.

2. **Expected Value and Variance**: Expected value and variance are used to quantify the central tendency and dispersion of a probability distribution. They provide insights into the average risk and the degree of uncertainty associated with it.

3. **Risk Measures**: Risk measures such as Value at Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) are used to quantify the potential financial loss associated with a risk scenario. VaR provides an estimate of the maximum loss that can occur within a given confidence level, while CVaR measures the average loss beyond the VaR threshold.

**Formulations for Supply Chain Finance Risk**

In supply chain finance, the mathematical models are used to assess the risks associated with various aspects of the supply chain, including:

1. **Credit Risk**: Credit risk models are used to assess the likelihood of a supplier or customer defaulting on their financial obligations. The models typically involve calculating the probability of default (PD) and the exposure at default (EAD).

2. **Market Risk**: Market risk models assess the impact of market fluctuations on the value of investments or assets in the supply chain. The models often use concepts such as beta, volatility, and the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) to quantify market risk.

3. **Operational Risk**: Operational risk models assess the risk of financial loss due to internal processes, people, systems, or external events. These models may involve event tree analysis, fault tree analysis, and other probabilistic methods to identify and quantify operational risks.

**Mathematical Formulation Examples**

Here are some examples of mathematical formulations used in supply chain finance risk analysis:

1. **Credit Risk Formulation**:
   - Probability of Default (PD):
     $$ PD = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} p_i $$
     where \( N \) is the number of loan defaults observed, and \( p_i \) is the probability of default for the \( i \)-th loan.

   - Exposure at Default (EAD):
     $$ EAD = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \max(0, x_i - L_i) $$
     where \( x_i \) is the outstanding loan balance for the \( i \)-th loan, and \( L_i \) is the loan amount.

2. **Market Risk Formulation**:
   - Value at Risk (VaR):
     $$ VaR = -\alpha \times \sigma \times \sqrt{T} $$
     where \( \alpha \) is the confidence level, \( \sigma \) is the standard deviation of returns, and \( T \) is the time horizon.

3. **Operational Risk Formulation**:
   - Loss Given Default (LGD):
     $$ LGD = \frac{\text{Total Loss}}{\text{Exposure}} $$
     where the total loss is the amount of financial loss incurred due to a default event.

These mathematical models and formulations provide a framework for analyzing and quantifying risks in supply chain finance. By understanding these concepts and applying them to real-world scenarios, supply chain finance professionals can develop more effective strategies for risk management and decision-making.
