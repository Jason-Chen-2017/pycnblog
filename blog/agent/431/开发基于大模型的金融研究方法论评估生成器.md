### 2.1 Mathematical Models for Financial Evaluation

**2.1.1 Risk Assessment Models**

Risk assessment is a fundamental component of financial research, aiming to evaluate the potential for loss in an investment or portfolio. Several mathematical models are employed to assess and quantify risk in financial markets. One of the most widely used models is the Value at Risk (VaR), which estimates the maximum potential loss over a specified time horizon at a given confidence level.

**VaR Model**

The Value at Risk (VaR) model is defined as:

$$
VaR = p \times \sigma \times \sqrt{n}
$$

where:
- \( p \) is the probability of a loss occurring within the specified time period.
- \( \sigma \) is the standard deviation of the returns over the historical period.
- \( n \) is the number of observations in the historical period.

For example, if we want to calculate the 95% VaR for a stock over a one-day period and the historical return data shows a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1%, the VaR would be:

$$
VaR = 0.05 \times 0.01 \times \sqrt{252} \approx 0.0143
$$

This means there is a 5% chance that the stock will lose more than 1.43% over a single trading day.

**Semi-Variance Model**

The semi-variance model extends the concept of VaR by considering only the negative deviations from the mean return. It is defined as:

$$
SemiVaR = p \times (\mu - \sigma^2)
$$

where:
- \( \mu \) is the mean return.
- \( \sigma^2 \) is the variance.

For instance, if the mean return is 5% and the variance is 0.01, the 5% semi-VaR would be:

$$
SemiVaR = 0.05 - 0.01 = 0.04
$$

This indicates that there is a 5% chance of a loss greater than 4%.

**2.1.2 Performance Evaluation Models**

Evaluating the performance of financial investments is crucial for assessing their historical or expected returns. Several performance evaluation models are utilized, including the Sharpe ratio, alpha, and beta.

**Sharpe Ratio**

The Sharpe ratio is a widely used performance metric that measures the risk-adjusted return of an investment. It is calculated as:

$$
Sharpe\ Ratio = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_p}
$$

where:
- \( R_p \) is the portfolio return.
- \( R_f \) is the risk-free rate.
- \( \sigma_p \) is the standard deviation of the portfolio returns.

For example, if a portfolio has a return of 10% and a standard deviation of 5% per year, and the risk-free rate is 2%, the Sharpe ratio would be:

$$
Sharpe\ Ratio = \frac{0.10 - 0.02}{0.05} = 1.6
$$

A higher Sharpe ratio indicates better risk-adjusted performance.

**Alpha**

Alpha measures an investment's risk-adjusted performance relative to a benchmark index. It is calculated as:

$$
\alpha = R_i - R_f - \beta_i \times R_m
$$

where:
- \( R_i \) is the investment return.
- \( R_f \) is the risk-free rate.
- \( \beta_i \) is the beta of the investment.
- \( R_m \) is the benchmark index return.

For instance, if an investment has a return of 12%, a risk-free rate of 2%, and a beta of 1.2, and the benchmark index has a return of 10%, the alpha would be:

$$
\alpha = 0.12 - 0.02 - 1.2 \times 0.1 = 0.02
$$

A positive alpha indicates outperformance, while a negative alpha suggests underperformance.

**Beta**

Beta measures the volatility of an investment relative to the overall market. It is calculated as:

$$
\beta_i = \frac{Cov(R_i, R_m)}{Var(R_m)}
$$

where:
- \( Cov(R_i, R_m) \) is the covariance between the investment return and the market return.
- \( Var(R_m) \) is the variance of the market return.

For example, if the covariance between the investment return and the market return is 0.12 and the variance of the market return is 0.09, the beta would be:

$$
\beta_i = \frac{0.12}{0.09} = 1.33
$$

A beta greater than 1 indicates higher volatility than the market, while a beta less than 1 indicates lower volatility.

**2.1.3 Comparative Analysis of Models**

Comparing different risk assessment and performance evaluation models is essential for selecting the most appropriate methodology for a specific financial research task. Several factors can influence the choice of model, including the nature of the data, the objectives of the research, and the desired level of risk exposure.

- **Data Characteristics:** Models like VaR and semi-variance are more suitable for analyzing large datasets with historical return data, while models like alpha and beta are better suited for analyzing the relative performance of investments against a benchmark.
- **Research Objectives:** The choice of model should align with the research objectives. For instance, if the goal is to evaluate the performance of a portfolio, the Sharpe ratio would be a more appropriate measure than VaR, which focuses on risk assessment.
- **Risk Tolerance:** Investors with higher risk tolerance may prefer models that emphasize higher returns and higher risk, such as the Sharpe ratio and alpha. Conversely, those with lower risk tolerance may prefer models that emphasize risk mitigation, such as VaR and beta.

By understanding the strengths and limitations of different mathematical models, researchers can make informed decisions about which methodologies to use in their financial research. ### 2.2 Algorithm Design and Implementation

**2.2.1 Overview of Common Algorithms**

In the field of financial research, several common algorithms are utilized to process, analyze, and evaluate financial data. These algorithms include various types of machine learning models, each with its unique characteristics and applications. The choice of algorithm depends on the specific requirements of the financial research task, such as the nature of the data, the complexity of the problem, and the desired level of accuracy.

**Support Vector Machines (SVM)**

Support Vector Machines (SVM) is a powerful supervised learning algorithm used for classification and regression tasks. SVMs identify the optimal hyperplane that separates the data into different classes, maximizing the margin between the hyperplane and the nearest data points (support vectors). This algorithm is particularly effective in high-dimensional spaces and is commonly used in financial market analysis for tasks such as stock price prediction and credit risk assessment.

**Random Forest**

Random Forest is an ensemble learning method that combines multiple decision trees to enhance prediction accuracy and reduce overfitting. Each decision tree is trained on a random subset of the data and features, and the final prediction is based on the majority vote of all the trees. Random Forests are highly versatile and can handle large volumes of data with high dimensionality, making them suitable for various financial research tasks, including portfolio optimization and risk management.

**Neural Networks**

Neural networks, particularly deep learning models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), are increasingly being used in financial research due to their ability to capture complex patterns and relationships in financial data. CNNs are particularly effective in processing grid-like data structures, such as stock price charts, while RNNs excel at capturing temporal dependencies in time series data. Neural networks have been applied to tasks such as stock price forecasting, sentiment analysis, and option pricing.

```python
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error
```

### 2.3 Mathematical Formulas and Detailed Explanation

**2.3.1 Basic Formulas in Financial Analysis**

Financial analysis involves the use of various mathematical formulas to evaluate the performance, risk, and potential of investments. Here, we discuss some fundamental formulas used in financial research, including the formulas for expected return, standard deviation, covariance, and correlation.

**Expected Return**

The expected return of an investment is a measure of the average return an investor can expect to earn over a given time period. It is calculated using the following formula:

$$
Expected\ Return = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (R_i \times p_i)}{n}
$$

where:
- \( R_i \) is the possible return for the ith scenario.
- \( p_i \) is the probability of the ith scenario occurring.
- \( n \) is the total number of scenarios.

For example, consider an investment with three possible scenarios and their corresponding returns and probabilities:

| Scenario | Return (\( R_i \)) | Probability (\( p_i \)) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 10% | 0.3 |
| 2 | 5% | 0.5 |
| 3 | -2% | 0.2 |

The expected return would be:

$$
Expected\ Return = \frac{(0.1 \times 0.3) + (0.05 \times 0.5) + (-0.02 \times 0.2)}{1} = 0.042
$$

**Standard Deviation**

Standard deviation is a measure of the dispersion of returns around the expected return. It quantifies the risk associated with an investment. The formula for calculating the standard deviation is:

$$
Standard\ Deviation = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (R_i - \bar{R})^2 \times p_i}{n}}
$$

where:
- \( R_i \) is the possible return for the ith scenario.
- \( \bar{R} \) is the expected return.
- \( p_i \) is the probability of the ith scenario occurring.
- \( n \) is the total number of scenarios.

Using the same investment scenarios as above, the standard deviation would be:

$$
Standard\ Deviation = \sqrt{\frac{(0.1 - 0.042)^2 \times 0.3 + (0.05 - 0.042)^2 \times 0.5 + (-0.02 - 0.042)^2 \times 0.2}{1}} \approx 0.078
$$

**Covariance**

Covariance measures the degree to which two variables move together. It is used to assess the relationship between the returns of two investments. The formula for calculating covariance is:

$$
Covariance(R_x, R_y) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (R_xi - \bar{R}_x) (R_yi - \bar{R}_y)}{n - 1}
$$

where:
- \( R_x \) and \( R_y \) are the returns of two investments.
- \( \bar{R}_x \) and \( \bar{R}_y \) are the expected returns of the two investments.
- \( n \) is the total number of scenarios.

For example, if we have two investments with the following return data:

| Investment 1 | Investment 2 |
| --- | --- |
| 10% | 15% |
| 5% | 10% |
| -2% | -5% |

The covariance would be:

$$
Covariance(R_x, R_y) = \frac{(0.1 - 0.078)(0.15 - 0.078) + (0.05 - 0.078)(0.1 - 0.078) + (-0.02 - 0.078)(-0.05 - 0.078)}{2} \approx 0.005
$$

**Correlation**

Correlation is a standardized measure of the relationship between two variables. It ranges from -1 to 1, where -1 indicates a perfect negative relationship, 1 indicates a perfect positive relationship, and 0 indicates no relationship. The formula for calculating correlation is:

$$
Correlation(R_x, R_y) = \frac{Covariance(R_x, R_y)}{\sigma_x \sigma_y}
$$

where:
- \( \sigma_x \) and \( \sigma_y \) are the standard deviations of the returns of the two investments.

Using the same investment data as above, the correlation would be:

$$
Correlation(R_x, R_y) = \frac{0.005}{0.078 \times 0.078} \approx 0.8
$$

The portfolio's standard deviation can be calculated using the formula for portfolio variance:

$$
Variance = w_x^2 \sigma_x^2 + w_y^2 \sigma_y^2 + 2w_x w_y \sigma_x \sigma_y \rho_{xy}
$$

where:
- \( w_x \) and \( w_y \) are the weights of the two stocks.
- \( \sigma_x \) and \( \sigma_y \) are the standard deviations of the two stocks.
- \( \rho_{xy} \) is the correlation between the two stocks.

Substituting the values, we get:

$$
Variance = (0.5^2 \times 0.15^2) + (0.5^2 \times 0.20^2) + 2(0.5)(0.5)(0.15)(0.20)(0.8) \approx 0.045
$$

The portfolio's standard deviation is then:

$$
Standard\ Deviation = \sqrt{Variance} \approx 0.212

```python
import pandas as pd

def preprocess_data(data):

    df["date"] = pd.to_datetime(df["date"])

    # Set date as index

    df.fillna(method="ffill", inplace=True)
```
