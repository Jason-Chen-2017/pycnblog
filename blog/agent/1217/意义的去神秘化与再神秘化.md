#### 1.1 Background and Definition of the Concepts

##### 1.1.1 The Origins of the Debate

The discourse on the de-mythification and re-mythification of meaning has its roots deeply embedded in the human quest for understanding and significance. Historically, humans have often turned to myths and rituals to make sense of their world. These stories served as a means of coping with uncertainties and providing explanations for natural phenomena. The process of de-mythification began with the rise of scientific reasoning during the Enlightenment, which challenged the dominance of traditional beliefs and supernatural explanations. Key figures like Galileo Galilei, Isaac Newton, and Charles Darwin contributed to this movement by offering empirical and rational explanations for the natural world.

##### 1.1.2 Definition of De-mythification

De-mythification refers to the process of removing or dissolving myths and supernatural beliefs through critical inquiry and empirical evidence. It involves questioning established traditions and challenging irrational beliefs with logical reasoning and scientific principles. De-mythification is not solely about debunking myths but also about constructing a more accurate and rational understanding of reality. This process has led to significant advancements in various fields, including science, philosophy, and technology.

##### 1.1.3 Definition of Re-mythification

On the other hand, re-mythification is the process of re-introducing or reinterpreting myths and symbols in a contemporary context, often driven by psychological and cultural needs. Re-mythification can serve to restore a sense of meaning and purpose in an increasingly secular and materialistic world. It involves updating old myths to resonate with the current societal values and addressing the void left by the de-mythification process. Re-mythification seeks to create new narratives that reflect the complexities of modern life while providing spiritual and emotional sustenance.

##### 1.1.4 The Significance in Modern Society

In modern society, the concepts of de-mythification and re-mythification are more relevant than ever. As we navigate through rapid technological advancements and face existential questions, the need for meaning and purpose becomes even more pronounced. De-mythification continues to shape our understanding of reality, challenging dogmas and fostering critical thinking. At the same time, re-mythification plays a crucial role in helping individuals find meaning in their lives, providing a sense of continuity and belonging.

#### 1.2 Core Theoretical Frameworks

##### 1.2.1 Historical Perspectives on Meaning

The concept of meaning has been a subject of inquiry across various disciplines throughout history. In philosophy, thinkers like Plato, Aristotle, and Nietzsche explored the nature of meaning and its relationship to reality. Plato's Theory of Forms proposed that meaning exists independently of the physical world, while Aristotle emphasized the role of reason in discovering meaning. Nietzsche, on the other hand, critiqued the traditional notions of meaning and proposed the idea of "will to power" as a driving force behind human actions.

In religion, meaning has been central to the spiritual and moral teachings of major faiths. From the sacred texts of Hinduism, Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam to the rituals and practices that accompany these beliefs, the pursuit of meaning is a universal theme. The de-mythification of religious beliefs often leads to a reevaluation of spiritual practices and a search for new sources of meaning.

##### 1.2.2 Key Theoretical Approaches

Several key theoretical approaches have shaped our understanding of de-mythification and re-mythification. One such approach is critical theory, which originated in the field of sociology and focuses on analyzing the power dynamics and social structures that shape our beliefs and values. Critical theory emphasizes the importance of questioning dominant narratives and challenging the status quo. Another important approach is constructivism, which posits that meaning is not inherent in objects or experiences but is constructed through social interactions and cultural contexts. This perspective highlights the role of individuals in shaping their understanding of the world.

##### 1.2.3 Comparative Analysis

A comparative analysis of de-mythification and re-mythification across different cultures and historical periods reveals both commonalities and differences. In Western societies, de-mythification has often been associated with the scientific revolution and the rise of secularism. In contrast, re-mythification has been more prevalent in Eastern cultures, where the fusion of science and spirituality has led to a unique approach to understanding the world. Understanding these differences can provide valuable insights into the ways in which de-mythification and re-mythification shape our understanding of meaning and purpose.

### Chapter 2: The Process of De-mythification

#### 2.1 Steps and Methods

##### 2.1.1 Unveiling Hidden Assumptions

The first step in the process of de-mythification is to identify and challenge hidden assumptions. These assumptions are often deeply ingrained in our cultural and social fabric and go unexamined. They serve as the foundations upon which myths and traditional beliefs are built. Unveiling these hidden assumptions involves engaging in critical thinking and actively seeking out alternative perspectives.

To begin with, one must identify the assumptions that underpin the accepted narratives and beliefs of a given society. These assumptions can be related to various aspects of life, such as religion, politics, science, and social norms. For example, a hidden assumption in many societies might be the belief in the inherent superiority of certain racial or ethnic groups. Identifying such assumptions requires a keen sense of awareness and a willingness to question established beliefs.

Once the hidden assumptions are identified, the next step is to challenge them. This involves questioning the origins and justifications for these assumptions and examining the evidence that supports or refutes them. For instance, one might question the scientific validity of a belief in supernatural phenomena or the ethical implications of certain social norms. Challenging hidden assumptions often requires a deep understanding of the subject matter and the ability to think critically.

##### 2.1.2 Critical Examination of Traditions

The second step in the process of de-mythification is to critically examine established traditions and rituals. Traditions are the cultural practices and customs that have been passed down from generation to generation. They often serve as a means of preserving cultural heritage and providing a sense of identity and continuity. However, traditions can also be rooted in myths and irrational beliefs.

To critically examine traditions, one must first understand their historical context and origins. This involves researching the history of the tradition and examining the reasons why it was established. For example, one might study the origins of religious rituals or social customs to understand how they were influenced by myths and cultural beliefs.

Once the historical context is understood, the next step is to evaluate the relevance of the tradition in the contemporary world. This involves asking questions such as: Does the tradition still serve a meaningful purpose? Is it aligned with contemporary values and ethical standards? For instance, one might question the relevance of certain religious practices that are based on ancient myths and supernatural beliefs in a society that has become more secular and scientific.

##### 2.1.3 Rational Analysis

Rational analysis is a key component of de-mythification. This involves using empirical evidence and logical reasoning to evaluate the claims and assertions made in myths and traditions. By employing scientific methods and critical thinking, one can separate fact from fiction and arrive at a more accurate understanding of reality.

To begin with, one must gather empirical evidence that supports or refutes the claims made in myths and traditions. This can involve conducting scientific experiments, collecting data, and analyzing statistics. For example, one might use scientific methods to examine the claims of a supernatural being or a divine intervention.

Once the empirical evidence is gathered, the next step is to analyze it using logical reasoning. This involves evaluating the consistency and coherence of the evidence and drawing conclusions based on the evidence. For instance, one might analyze the logical inconsistencies in a myth that claims a supernatural being can control natural phenomena.

Additionally, rational analysis involves comparing different theories and explanations to determine which one is more plausible. This can be done by evaluating the strengths and weaknesses of each theory and considering the evidence that supports or refutes them. For example, one might compare the scientific explanation for a natural phenomenon with a supernatural explanation and evaluate which one provides a better fit for the available evidence.

For example, Galileo's observations with the telescope challenged the geocentric model of the universe and supported the heliocentric model proposed by Copernicus.    The rise of skepticism in the modern era represents another example of de-mythification. Skepticism involves a critical examination of claims and beliefs, demanding evidence and rational justification. Skeptical movements have challenged various pseudoscientific and paranormal claims, promoting critical thinking and scientific skepticism. For example, the skeptical movement has debunked many claims of paranormal phenomena and supernatural interventions, promoting a more evidence-based understanding of the world.

### Chapter 4: De-mythification and Re-mythification in Different Fields

#### 4.1 Philosophy

De-mythification and re-mythification have had a profound impact on the field of philosophy. Throughout history, philosophers have continually challenged traditional beliefs and sought to uncover the underlying assumptions that shape human understanding. This process of de-mythification has led to significant developments in philosophical thought, particularly in areas such as ethics, metaphysics, and epistemology.

In the ancient world, philosophers like Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle sought to de-mythify the world by promoting critical thinking and rational inquiry. Socrates, for instance, emphasized the importance of questioning established beliefs and seeking self-knowledge. Plato's dialogues often pitted reason against myth and superstition, advocating for a higher form of knowledge that transcended the material world. Aristotle, meanwhile, developed a systematic approach to philosophy that emphasized observation and empirical evidence.

The process of de-mythification continued in the modern era with thinkers like Immanuel Kant and Friedrich Nietzsche. Kant's Critique of Pure Reason sought to separate the realm of phenomena from the realm of things-in-themselves, challenging the notion that our knowledge of the world is purely passive. Nietzsche, on the other hand, critiqued traditional morality and proposed the concept of the "will to power" as a driving force behind human actions.

In contemporary philosophy, de-mythification is still a central concern. Philosophers like Michel Foucault and Jean-François Lyotard have examined the role of power and knowledge in shaping societal norms and beliefs. Foucault's concept of "power-knowledge" highlights the interdependence of these two elements and emphasizes the importance of questioning dominant narratives. Lyotard's notion of "postmodernity" challenges the idea of universal truths and emphasizes the importance of fragmented, localized knowledge.

Re-mythification in philosophy often involves reinterpreting ancient ideas in light of contemporary concerns. For instance, contemporary philosophers like Alain Badiou and Giorgio Agamben have reinterpreted Platonic and Aristotelian ideas to address issues such as the nature of truth, the role of the subject, and the relationship between politics and philosophy. Badiou's concept of " evento" seeks to revive the idea of the subject as a radical agent of change, while Agamben's work on "bare life" explores the relationship between politics, ethics, and the human condition.
