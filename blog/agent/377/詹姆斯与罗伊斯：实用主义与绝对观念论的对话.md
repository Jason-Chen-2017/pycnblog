#### 1.1 Background of Pragmatism and Absolute Idealism

The philosophical landscapes of pragmatism and absolute idealism have significantly shaped modern intellectual discourse. At their core, pragmatism and absolute idealism represent two distinct yet interrelated philosophical paradigms that offer profound insights into the nature of reality, knowledge, and ethics.

**Pragmatism** was formulated by American philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce and later developed by William James and John Dewey. It emphasizes the practical aspects of ideas and the role of experience in shaping understanding. At its heart, pragmatism asserts that the meaning of concepts and propositions is determined by their practical effects and consequences. This philosophy underscores the importance of action, experience, and utility in evaluating theories and beliefs.

**Absolute Idealism**, on the other hand, was advanced by German philosopher Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel and later popularized by English philosopher F.H. Bradley. It posits that reality is an expression of an all-encompassing, absolute mind or spirit. Absolute Idealism contends that the ultimate reality is a rational, unified whole, where all phenomena are manifestations of this all-encompassing spirit.

#### 1.2 The Emergence of Pragmatism

Pragmatism emerged as a philosophical movement during the late 19th and early 20th centuries in the United States, primarily as a reaction to the idealism and rationalism prevalent in European philosophy. Influenced by the intellectual currents of the time, pragmatism sought to bridge the gap between abstract philosophical theories and concrete human experiences.

Charles Sanders Peirce is often considered the father of pragmatism. In his work, he introduced the pragmatic maxim, which states that the meaning of a concept or proposition is to be determined by its practical consequences. This maxim became a cornerstone of pragmatism, emphasizing the importance of action and experience in understanding and validating ideas.

William James, Peirce's cousin, further developed pragmatism into a comprehensive philosophical system. His works, particularly "Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking," laid the groundwork for the practical and applied aspects of pragmatism. James emphasized the role of individual experiences and the collective wisdom derived from these experiences in shaping human understanding and behavior.

#### 1.3 The Core Principles of Pragmatism

The core principles of pragmatism can be summarized as follows:

1. **Practicality**: The meaning of concepts and propositions is determined by their practical effects and consequences.
2. **Experience**: Experience is the primary source of understanding and knowledge.
3. **Empirical Verification**: The validity of theories and beliefs is established through empirical observation and experimentation.
4. **Flexibility**: Pragmatism is adaptable and open to change, continually refining and adjusting theories based on new experiences and evidence.
5. **Utilitarianism**: The utility or usefulness of ideas and actions is a key criterion in evaluating their value.

#### 1.4 The Development of Absolute Idealism

Absolute Idealism originated with the works of Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel, a German philosopher who lived in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. Hegel's philosophy is characterized by its dialectical method, which posits that reality unfolds through a process of conflict, transformation, and synthesis.

Hegel's central idea is that reality is an all-encompassing, rational unity that manifests itself in the historical and intellectual processes of human civilization. His philosophy emphasizes the importance of reason and the objective spirit in the development of society and culture.

F.H. Bradley, an English philosopher, further developed and critiqued Hegel's ideas in the mid-19th century. Bradley's absolute idealism asserts that the ultimate reality is an all-encompassing, rational spirit that transcends individual consciousness and existence.

#### 1.2.1 James' Pragmatic Philosophy

William James, a pivotal figure in the development of pragmatism, proposed a philosophy that emphasized the practical aspects of human experience over abstract reasoning. Central to James' pragmatic philosophy are several core principles that have shaped the field of philosophy and influenced subsequent thinkers.

**Pragmatism as a Tool for Understanding**

At its core, pragmatism is a methodological tool used to determine the meaning and truth of concepts and propositions. The pragmatic maxim, articulated by Charles Sanders Peirce, encapsulates this principle: "Consider what effects, which might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. This maxim underscores the importance of empirical evidence and practical outcomes in evaluating theories and beliefs. By focusing on the consequences of ideas, pragmatism provides a tangible, actionable framework for understanding the world.

**Experience as the Source of Knowledge**

Experience is a cornerstone of James' pragmatic philosophy. He argued that knowledge is derived from personal experiences and the interactions between individuals and their environment. This emphasis on experience contrasts with more abstract and idealist perspectives that prioritize rational推理 and metaphysical speculation.

For James, the mind is not a passive recipient of sensory information but an active constructor of meaning. This process of constructing meaning through experience is continuous and dynamic, constantly reshaping our understanding of the world. This perspective is captured in his famous quote: "The truth is what works."

**Practical Outcomes as the Measure of Truth**

In line with the pragmatic maxim, James posited that the truth of a proposition is determined by its practical success. If an idea leads to beneficial outcomes and solves real-world problems, it is considered true in a practical sense. This practical criterion of truth is in stark contrast to the more abstract and theoretical notions of truth that prevailed in traditional philosophy.

**The Role of Individualism**

James' pragmatic philosophy also emphasized the role of individualism. He argued that individual experiences and perspectives are crucial in shaping collective knowledge and understanding. This perspective contrasts with more collectivist or socialist views that prioritize societal and communal values over individual experiences.

**Pragmatism and Ethics**

James' pragmatic approach extends into the realm of ethics, where he advocated for a practical and utilitarian approach to moral decision-making. He believed that ethical actions should be based on their ability to promote the greatest good for the greatest number. This utilitarian perspective aligns with the pragmatic emphasis on practical consequences and outcomes.

In summary, William James' pragmatic philosophy represents a shift towards a more practical, experience-based approach to philosophy. By focusing on the practical consequences of ideas and the continuous shaping of meaning through experience, James' pragmatism offers a dynamic and adaptable framework for understanding the world and our place within it.
