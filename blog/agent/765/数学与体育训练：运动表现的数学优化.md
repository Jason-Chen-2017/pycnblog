**2.1 Mathematical Optimization Basics**

**2.1.1 Principles and Approaches**

Mathematical optimization is the process of finding the maximum or minimum value of a function, subject to certain constraints. The core principles of optimization include:

- **Objective Function:** A function that represents the quantity to be optimized (e.g., performance, efficiency, or cost).
- **Constraints:** Restrictions that define the feasible region of the optimization problem.
- **Optimization Algorithms:** Methods used to find the optimal solution within the feasible region.

There are two main types of optimization problems:

- **Linear Optimization:** Problems where the objective function and constraints are linear.
- **Nonlinear Optimization:** Problems where the objective function and constraints are nonlinear.

**2.1.2 Types of Optimization Problems**

1. **Maximization Problems:** Finding the maximum value of the objective function within the feasible region.
2. **Minimization Problems:** Finding the minimum value of the objective function within the feasible region.
3. **Equilibrium Problems:** Finding a solution where the objective function is neither maximized nor minimized but reaches an equilibrium point.
4. **Multi-Objective Optimization:** Solving problems with multiple conflicting objectives simultaneously.

**3.1 Models of Physical Performance**

**3.1.1 Force-Velocity Profile**

One of the most critical models in sports performance is the force-velocity profile. This model describes the relationship between force production and the velocity at which an athlete can generate that force. In sports that involve sprinting, jumping, or throwing, understanding this relationship is crucial for optimizing performance.

The force-velocity profile typically exhibits an inverted U-shaped curve, where maximum force is produced at low velocities and maximum velocity is achieved at zero force. For example, in sprinting, an athlete needs to generate high force to push off the ground quickly, but as the velocity increases, the force requirement decreases. Therefore, optimizing the force-velocity profile involves finding the right balance between force and velocity for a specific sport.

**3.1.2 Power Output Models**

Power output is another essential aspect of sports performance. Power is defined as the rate at which work is done, and it is a crucial factor in many sports activities, such as sprinting, weightlifting, and jumping. The mathematical model for power output is given by:

\[ P = F \times v \]

Where \( P \) is power, \( F \) is force, and \( v \) is velocity. Power output models can be used to predict and optimize the performance of athletes in various sports by adjusting the force and velocity components.

**3.1.3 Muscle Fatigue Models**

Muscle fatigue is a significant factor that affects sports performance. Mathematical models of muscle fatigue can help predict how fatigue develops during prolonged physical activities and how it can be mitigated through appropriate training strategies. One common model is the force-velocity relationship that incorporates fatigue as a factor that alters the force-velocity profile.

**3.2 Energy Systems and Their Optimization**

Energy systems are the biological systems that generate the energy required for athletic performance. There are three primary energy systems:

- **Phosphagen System:** Provides rapid energy for short-duration high-intensity activities (e.g., sprinting, weightlifting).
- **Glycolytic System:** Generates energy through the breakdown of carbohydrates (e.g., endurance running, soccer).
- **Aerobic System:** Uses oxygen to break down carbohydrates and fats for prolonged activities (e.g., long-distance running, cycling).

Optimizing energy systems involves training specific components to enhance their efficiency and capacity. For example, interval training can improve the phosphagen system's capacity, while long-distance running can enhance the aerobic system's efficiency.

**3.2.1 Mathematical Models of Energy Systems**

Mathematical models of energy systems can be used to predict energy expenditure and optimize training strategies. These models typically involve equations that describe the rate of energy production and consumption based on factors such as exercise intensity, duration, and individual physiological characteristics.

**3.3 Statistical Models in Performance Analysis**

- **Machine Learning Algorithms:** Applied to large datasets to identify complex relationships and make predictions (e.g., player performance forecasting).

We can model this scenario using the binomial distribution:

\[ P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k} \]

Where \( X \) is the number of successes (in this case, scoring 2 points), \( n \) is the number of trials (10 free throws), \( p \) is the probability of success (0.4), and \( \binom{n}{k} \) is the binomial coefficient.

Plugging in the values, we get:

\[ P(X = 2) = \binom{10}{2} (0.4)^2 (0.6)^8 \approx 0.236 \]

Therefore, the probability of the player scoring 2 points in a game is approximately 23.6%.
