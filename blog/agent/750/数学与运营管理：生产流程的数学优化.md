Optimization is a fundamental concept in operations management, playing a critical role in enhancing efficiency and productivity within various industries. At its core, optimization involves finding the best possible solution from a set of feasible alternatives, often with the goal of maximizing benefits or minimizing costs. In the context of operations management, this translates to strategies and techniques designed to streamline production processes, allocate resources effectively, and improve overall operational performance.

**History and Evolution of Optimization in Operations Management**

The concept of optimization has been in existence for centuries, with early examples found in various mathematical and engineering disciplines. However, its application to operations management is a more recent development. The origins of operations management as a distinct field can be traced back to the late 19th and early 20th centuries, with the advent of scientific management principles advocated by figures like Frederick Taylor. Taylor's emphasis on systematic analysis and improvement of work processes laid the groundwork for modern optimization techniques.

As industries grew and production processes became more complex, the need for more sophisticated methods of optimization emerged. The mid-20th century saw the development of linear programming, one of the first formal optimization techniques, by mathematical giants like George Dantzig. Linear programming provides a systematic approach to solving problems involving a set of linear equations and inequalities, making it an invaluable tool for operations managers.

In the following decades, optimization expanded beyond linear programming to include non-linear programming, dynamic programming, and heuristic methods. These advancements were driven by the increasing availability of computational power and the growing complexity of production systems.

**The Role of Optimization in Modern Manufacturing**

Today, optimization is integral to modern manufacturing, where the drive for efficiency, cost reduction, and product quality is relentless. Here are some key roles that optimization plays in manufacturing:

1. **Production Planning and Scheduling**: Optimization algorithms help in creating schedules that minimize production time, reduce idle time, and maximize the utilization of resources. This ensures that production lines run smoothly and efficiently, reducing bottlenecks and downtime.

2. **Inventory Management**: By optimizing inventory levels, companies can minimize holding costs while ensuring that they have enough stock to meet demand. This is crucial in just-in-time production systems where timely delivery of materials is essential.

3. **Supplier Selection and Procurement**: Optimization can be used to select suppliers based on criteria such as cost, reliability, and delivery time. This helps in building a robust supply chain that minimizes disruptions and maximizes value.

4. **Quality Control**: Statistical process control methods use optimization to identify and eliminate sources of variation in production processes, thereby improving product quality and reducing defects.

5. **Energy Management**: Optimization techniques are employed to manage energy consumption in manufacturing processes, reducing costs and environmental impact.

**The Importance of Mathematics in Optimization**

Mathematics is the backbone of optimization, providing the theoretical foundation and analytical tools necessary to solve complex problems. Here are some key ways in which mathematics is crucial in optimization:

1. **Modeling**: Optimization problems are typically represented mathematically using equations, inequalities, and other mathematical constructs. This allows for a precise and formal description of the problem, which is essential for developing effective solutions.

2. **Analysis**: Mathematical techniques are used to analyze the properties of optimization problems, such as their convexity or concavity. This analysis helps in understanding the nature of the problem and selecting appropriate solution methods.

3. **Computation**: Advanced mathematical algorithms, such as gradient descent, dynamic programming, and genetic algorithms, are employed to solve optimization problems. These algorithms rely on mathematical principles to guide the search for optimal solutions.

4. **Validation**: Mathematical methods are used to validate the solutions obtained from optimization models. This involves verifying that the solutions satisfy the constraints of the problem and that they are indeed optimal.

In conclusion, optimization is a cornerstone of modern operations management, with mathematics providing the essential tools and techniques for its application. As industries continue to evolve and become more complex, the role of optimization—and the importance of mathematics in this field—will only continue to grow.

### Core Concepts and Terminology

To delve into the world of optimization and operations management, it's crucial to understand the foundational concepts and terminology that underpin these fields. This section will cover the basic definitions, common optimization algorithms and methods, and provide examples of common optimization problems encountered in operations management.

**Basic Definitions and Theories**

1. **Optimization Problem**: An optimization problem is a mathematical framework that involves determining the best possible solution from a set of possible solutions. This involves defining an objective function to be maximized or minimized and a set of constraints that the solution must satisfy.

2. **Objective Function**: The objective function is a mathematical expression that defines the goal of the optimization problem, such as maximizing profit or minimizing cost. It is typically represented by a linear or nonlinear equation.

3. **Constraints**: Constraints are limitations or conditions that must be satisfied by the solution to the optimization problem. These can be expressed as linear or nonlinear inequalities or equations.

4. **Feasible Solution**: A feasible solution is a potential solution that satisfies all the constraints of the optimization problem. The feasible region is the set of all feasible solutions.

5. **Optimal Solution**: An optimal solution is the solution that provides the best possible outcome according to the objective function, given the constraints.

**Optimization Algorithms and Methods**

Optimization algorithms are systematic procedures for finding solutions to optimization problems. Here are some of the most common algorithms and methods:

1. **Linear Programming (LP)**: Linear programming is a mathematical method for optimizing a linear objective function, subject to linear constraints. It is widely used in resource allocation and production planning. The simplex algorithm and the interior-point method are common methods for solving linear programming problems.

2. **Nonlinear Programming (NLP)**: Nonlinear programming deals with optimization problems where the objective function and constraints are nonlinear. Methods include gradient-based methods (such as the Newton-Raphson method) and gradient-free methods (such as the Nelder-Mead method).

3. **Dynamic Programming**: Dynamic programming is an optimization technique used to solve problems that can be broken down into overlapping subproblems. It is particularly useful for problems involving sequential decision-making, such as inventory management and production scheduling.

4. **Heuristic Methods**: Heuristic methods are problem-solving techniques that are typically faster and less systematic than traditional optimization methods but may not guarantee the optimal solution. Examples include genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, and tabu search.

5. **Metaheuristic Algorithms**: Metaheuristic algorithms are a broader category of algorithms that include heuristic methods and are designed to escape local optima and find better solutions. Common metaheuristics include genetic algorithms, ant colony optimization, and particle swarm optimization.

**Common Optimization Problems in Operations Management**

Operations management involves a wide range of optimization problems. Here are some examples:

1. **Production Planning and Scheduling**: This involves scheduling production activities to meet customer demand while minimizing costs and maximizing efficiency. Optimization models are used to determine the optimal production rates, machine usage, and workforce scheduling.

2. **Inventory Management**: Inventory management involves optimizing inventory levels to minimize holding costs and avoid stockouts. Techniques such as Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) and Just-In-Time (JIT) use optimization to determine the optimal order quantities and reorder points.

3. **Supplier Selection**: Selecting the right suppliers is critical for maintaining a reliable supply chain. Optimization can be used to select suppliers based on criteria such as cost, quality, reliability, and delivery time.

4. **Process Optimization**: This involves improving the efficiency of production processes through optimization techniques. For example, statistical process control uses optimization to minimize process variation and improve product quality.

5. **Resource Allocation**: Resource allocation involves deciding how to allocate resources such as labor, equipment, and materials to various tasks or projects. Optimization models can help in determining the optimal allocation to maximize overall efficiency and productivity.

In summary, a solid understanding of the core concepts and terminology of optimization and operations management is essential for effectively applying optimization techniques to real-world problems. This knowledge forms the foundation for the more advanced discussions and case studies that will follow in subsequent chapters.

#### Basic Mathematics for Optimization

**Linear Algebra and Matrix Theory**

Linear algebra is foundational to many optimization techniques, providing the mathematical framework for understanding systems of linear equations, matrix operations, and vector spaces. Key concepts include:

- **Vectors and Matrices**: Vectors are used to represent quantities with magnitude and direction, while matrices are used to organize and manipulate these quantities in a structured manner.
- **Matrix Operations**: Basic operations such as matrix addition, subtraction, multiplication, and inversion are crucial for solving linear systems and performing other mathematical computations.
- **Systems of Linear Equations**: Linear systems are common in optimization problems, where the goal is to find values of variables that satisfy a set of linear equations simultaneously.
- **Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors**: These concepts are used to analyze the properties of matrices, particularly in understanding how linear transformations affect data.

**Calculus**

Calculus provides the tools for analyzing change and determining optimal values. Here are some core concepts:

- **Derivatives**: Derivatives measure the rate of change of a function with respect to its input variables. They are used in optimization to find critical points where the objective function reaches its maximum or minimum.
- **Integrals**: Integrals are used to calculate the total amount of change over a given interval. In optimization, they can be used to find the total cost or total profit associated with a particular solution.
- **Concavity and Convexity**: These properties of functions indicate whether they have a maximum or minimum at a given point. Understanding these properties is essential for determining the nature of optimal solutions.

**Probability Theory and Statistics**

Probability theory and statistics are indispensable in dealing with uncertainty and variability, which are inherent in many operational contexts. Key concepts include:

- **Probability Distributions**: Distributions describe the likelihood of different outcomes. Common distributions include the normal distribution, exponential distribution, and uniform distribution.
- **Random Variables**: Random variables represent outcomes that are subject to chance. They can be discrete or continuous and are used to model uncertainty in optimization problems.
- **Statistics**: Descriptive statistics summarize data, while inferential statistics use data to make inferences about larger populations. Techniques such as regression analysis and hypothesis testing are fundamental in validating and interpreting optimization results.

#### Mathematical Modeling Techniques

Mathematical modeling is the process of translating real-world problems into mathematical structures that can be analyzed and solved. Here are the steps involved in mathematical modeling:

1. **Problem Definition**: Clearly define the problem and the objectives you want to achieve. This involves identifying the decision variables, constraints, and the objective function.
2. **Formulation**: Express the problem using mathematical notation. This typically involves defining the variables, writing down the objective function, and stating the constraints.
3. **Analysis**: Analyze the formulated model to gain insights and determine the properties of the problem. This may involve calculating gradients, determining concavity, or performing sensitivity analysis.
4. **Solution**: Apply optimization algorithms to find solutions to the formulated model. This can involve solving linear programs, non-linear programs, or using heuristic methods.

#### Optimization Tools and Methods

Optimization tools and methods are the practical means of solving optimization problems. Here, we will briefly discuss some of the most common optimization algorithms:

1. **Linear Programming (LP)**:
   - **Simplex Algorithm**: This is one of the most widely used methods for solving linear programs. It iteratively improves a feasible solution until an optimal solution is found.
   - **Interior-Point Method**: This method is more computationally efficient for large-scale linear programs and is used extensively in practice.

2. **Nonlinear Programming (NLP)**:
   - **Gradient-Based Methods**: These methods use gradients to iteratively improve the current solution. Examples include the BFGS algorithm and the Newton-Raphson method.
   - **Gradient-Free Methods**: These methods do not require the calculation of gradients and include methods like the Nelder-Mead algorithm and the pattern search algorithm.

3. **Heuristic Methods**:
   - **Genetic Algorithms**: Inspired by the process of natural selection, genetic algorithms use genetic operators like crossover and mutation to evolve solutions towards an optimal solution.
   - **Simulated Annealing**: This method mimics the physical process of annealing to avoid local optima and find better solutions.
   - **Ant Colony Optimization**: This is a probabilistic technique inspired by the foraging behavior of ants. It is used to find good solutions to optimization problems.

### Production Planning and Scheduling Optimization

Production planning and scheduling are critical components of operations management, aimed at optimizing the allocation of resources and the sequence of activities to meet production goals efficiently. In this chapter, we will delve into the mathematical optimization techniques used to tackle production planning and scheduling problems. We will start with the problem description and formulation, followed by an explanation of common optimization methods and their applications. To enhance understanding, we will also provide an example using a Python-based optimization library.

#### Problem Description

The production planning and scheduling problem involves making decisions about what to produce, how much to produce, and when to produce it. The goal is to achieve an efficient production schedule that minimizes production time, reduces costs, and meets customer demand. Key elements of the problem include:

- **Decision Variables**: These include production quantities, start and end times for production tasks, and allocation of resources (machines, labor, materials).
- **Constraints**: These constraints can be related to machine availability, labor availability, production capacity, and demand requirements.
- **Objective Function**: This defines the goal of the optimization, such as minimizing total production time, minimizing cost, or maximizing utilization of resources.

#### Problem Formulation

Mathematically, the production planning and scheduling problem can be formulated as follows:

**Objective Function**:
Minimize the total production time, which can be expressed as:
$$
\min T = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (t_{i} + s_{i}),
$$
where \(T\) is the total production time, \(t_{i}\) is the processing time for job \(i\), and \(s_{i}\) is the setup time for job \(i\).

**Constraints**:
- **Machine Constraints**: Each machine can process only one job at a time, so the start time of any job \(i\) must be after the end time of the previous job \(i-1\):
$$
s_{i} + t_{i} \leq s_{i-1} + t_{i-1} \quad \text{for all} \quad i = 2, 3, ..., n.
$$
- **Labor Constraints**: The number of workers available for each shift must be sufficient to handle the workload:
$$
L_j \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{t_{i}}{T_j},
$$
where \(L_j\) is the number of labor hours available in shift \(j\), and \(T_j\) is the total processing time for all jobs in shift \(j\).
- **Demand Constraints**: The production schedule must meet customer demand:
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i} \geq D,
$$
where \(x_{i}\) is the production quantity for job \(i\), and \(D\) is the total demand.

#### Common Optimization Methods

Optimization methods used for production planning and scheduling include linear programming, dynamic programming, and heuristic algorithms. Here, we will focus on linear programming and heuristic methods due to their widespread applicability and effectiveness.

**Linear Programming**

Linear programming (LP) is a mathematical method for optimizing a linear objective function subject to linear constraints. For production planning and scheduling, LP can be used to:

- **Minimize production time**: By formulating the problem as a linear program, we can use algorithms like the simplex method to find an optimal schedule that minimizes total production time.
- **Minimize cost**: By including cost terms in the objective function, we can minimize total production costs, which may include labor, material, and machine usage costs.

**Heuristic Methods**

Heuristic methods are valuable when an exact solution is not feasible due to the complexity of the problem. Common heuristic methods used in production planning and scheduling include:

- **Genetic Algorithms**: These are inspired by the process of natural selection and use genetic operators like crossover and mutation to evolve solutions towards an optimal schedule.
- **Simulated Annealing**: This method mimics the physical process of annealing to avoid local optima and find better solutions.
- **Ant Colony Optimization**: This method simulates the foraging behavior of ants to find good solutions to scheduling problems.

### Inventory Management Optimization

Inventory management is a vital aspect of operations management that involves balancing the costs of holding inventory against the risks of stockouts and missed demand. Optimizing inventory levels ensures that a company maintains sufficient stock to meet customer demand while minimizing holding costs. In this section, we will delve into the optimization techniques used for inventory management, including Economic Order Quantity (EOQ), Just-In-Time (JIT), and other methods, with a focus on supply chain management and production scheduling.

#### Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)

The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) model is one of the oldest and most widely used methods for inventory management. It aims to determine the optimal order quantity that minimizes the total cost of inventory management, which includes ordering costs and holding costs. The EOQ model is based on the following assumptions:

- Demand is constant over the planning period.
- Lead time, the time between placing an order and receiving the goods, is constant.
- Ordering and holding costs are proportional to the order quantity and the inventory level, respectively.

**EOQ Formula**:
$$
EOQ = \sqrt{\frac{2DS}{H}},
$$
where:
- \( D \) is the annual demand (units per year).
- \( S \) is the ordering cost per order.
- \( H \) is the holding cost per unit per year.

**Optimization**:
To optimize the EOQ, we can formulate it as an optimization problem:
$$
\min C = D \cdot S \cdot EOQ + \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{D}{EOQ} \cdot H.
$$
Using optimization techniques such as linear programming or the gradient method, we can find the optimal EOQ that minimizes the total cost.

#### Just-In-Time (JIT)

Just-In-Time (JIT) is a production strategy that aims to minimize inventory levels by receiving materials and producing goods just in time to meet customer demand. JIT focuses on eliminating waste, reducing lead times, and improving overall efficiency. The key components of JIT include:

- **Quality Control**: Ensuring that incoming materials meet high standards to avoid defects and rework.
- **Continuous Improvement**: Constantly refining processes to reduce waste and improve efficiency.
- **Supplier Relationships**: Building strong relationships with suppliers to ensure timely delivery.

**Optimization**:
JIT optimization involves balancing production schedules with supplier delivery times to minimize inventory levels. This can be formulated as a dynamic optimization problem where the inventory level and production schedule are adjusted continuously based on demand forecasts and supplier delivery schedules.

#### Case Studies and Practical Applications

**Example 1: EOQ Optimization in a Manufacturing Company**

A manufacturing company produces 10,000 units of a product per year. The ordering cost per order is $50, and the holding cost per unit per year is $5. Using the EOQ formula, the optimal order quantity is:

$$
EOQ = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 10,000 \cdot 50}{5}} = 200.
$$

Optimizing the EOQ reduces the total inventory cost by balancing ordering and holding costs.

**Example 2: JIT Implementation in a Retail Chain**

A retail chain aims to implement JIT to minimize inventory and improve efficiency. By analyzing historical sales data and supplier lead times, the company adjusts its production schedule to align with customer demand. This reduces the need for excess inventory and minimizes the risk of stockouts.

#### Problem Description

The supplier selection problem involves evaluating and selecting suppliers based on multiple criteria, such as cost, quality, delivery time, reliability, and environmental sustainability. The goal is to identify the suppliers that best meet the company's requirements while minimizing risks and maximizing value.

**Decision Variables**:
- **Supplier Selection**: Choosing which suppliers to include in the supply chain.

**Constraints**:

- **Delivery Time Constraints**: Suppliers must be able to meet the required delivery schedules to avoid production delays.

**Objective Function**:
- **Minimize Total Cost**: The primary goal is to minimize the total cost of goods and services obtained from suppliers while meeting all other constraints.

#### Optimization Techniques

**Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM)**:

Multi-criteria decision-making methods are used to evaluate suppliers based on multiple criteria. Common approaches include:

- **Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)**: This method uses pairwise comparisons to determine the relative importance of criteria and supplier scores.
- **Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS)**: TOPSIS ranks suppliers based on their similarity to the ideal and anti-ideal solutions.

**Supplier Performance Analysis**:

Supplier performance analysis involves evaluating suppliers based on historical data and key performance indicators (KPIs). Techniques include:

- **Benchmarking**: Comparing supplier performance against industry standards or competitors.
- **Supplier Rating Systems**: Assigning ratings based on KPIs such as on-time delivery, quality, responsiveness, and cost.

**Optimization Algorithms**:

Optimization algorithms can be used to solve complex supplier selection problems, especially when dealing with a large number of criteria and suppliers. Common algorithms include:

- **Linear Programming (LP)**: LP can be used to optimize supplier selection by minimizing the total cost subject to constraints on budget, quality, and delivery time.
- **Nonlinear Programming (NLP)**: NLP is used when the objective function and constraints are nonlinear.
- **Heuristic Methods**: Heuristic methods, such as genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, and ant colony optimization, are useful when an exact solution is not feasible due to problem complexity.

### Process Optimization

Process optimization is a critical component of operations management that focuses on improving the efficiency and effectiveness of production processes. By systematically analyzing and refining production workflows, companies can minimize waste, reduce costs, and enhance product quality. This section will delve into the various statistical process control methods and techniques used in process optimization, including quality control charts, regression analysis, and statistical process control (SPC).

#### Statistical Process Control Methods

**Quality Control Charts**

Quality control charts are graphical tools used to monitor and control process performance over time. They provide a visual representation of process data, allowing operators to identify patterns, trends, and anomalies. Key types of quality control charts include:

- **X-bar and R Charts**: X-bar charts monitor the mean of a process over time, while R charts monitor the range of the process. Together, they help detect shifts and variations in the process.
- **Control Charts for Variables**: These charts monitor continuous data, such as dimensions, weights, or concentrations.
- **Control Charts for Attributes**: These charts monitor categorical data, such as the number of defects or the number of units with specific characteristics.

**Regression Analysis**

Regression analysis is a statistical technique used to examine the relationships between variables. In process optimization, regression analysis can help identify factors that influence process performance and predict outcomes based on historical data. Key applications include:

- **Simple Linear Regression**: This method examines the relationship between two variables and can be used to predict one variable based on the other.
- **Multiple Linear Regression**: This method extends simple linear regression to include multiple independent variables.
- **Nonlinear Regression**: This technique is used when the relationship between variables is nonlinear.

**Statistical Process Control (SPC)**

Statistical process control (SPC) is a systematic approach to monitoring and improving production processes. SPC uses statistical methods to ensure that processes are stable and under control. Key components of SPC include:

- **Control Plan Development**: This involves defining the control limits and sampling plans for a process.
- **Process Monitoring**: Control charts are used to monitor process performance over time and detect any deviations from the control limits.
- **Root Cause Analysis**: When a process deviation is detected, root cause analysis is conducted to identify the underlying causes of the problem.
- **Process Improvement**: Based on the findings from root cause analysis, process improvements are implemented to correct the issues and prevent future deviations.

#### Common Techniques in Process Optimization

**Design of Experiments (DOE)**

Design of experiments (DOE) is a systematic approach to determining the factors that affect a process and optimizing process performance. Key techniques include:

- **Full Factorial Design**: This design examines the effects of all possible combinations of factor levels.
- **Fractional Factorial Design**: This design reduces the number of experiments required by focusing on a subset of factor combinations.
- **Response Surface Methodology (RSM)**: RSM uses regression techniques to model the relationship between factors and responses, allowing for the optimization of process performance.

**Six Sigma**

Six Sigma is a data-driven methodology aimed at improving process performance and reducing defects. Key components of Six Sigma include:

- **Define**: Clearly define the project goals and scope.
- **Measure**: Collect and analyze data to measure the current process performance.
- **Analyze**: Identify the root causes of process variations and opportunities for improvement.
- **Improve**: Develop and implement solutions to address the root causes and improve process performance.
- **Control**: Establish controls to ensure that the improvements are sustained over time.

**Lean Manufacturing**

Lean manufacturing focuses on eliminating waste and improving efficiency in production processes. Key techniques include:

- **Value Stream Mapping**: This technique visualizes the flow of materials and information through the production process, allowing for the identification of waste and inefficiencies.
- **5S Methodology**: This method promotes a clean, organized, and efficient work environment.
- **Kaizen**: Kaizen is a continuous improvement process that encourages employees to contribute ideas and suggestions for process improvement.

#### Case Studies and Practical Applications

**Example 1: X-bar and R Charts for Quality Control**

A manufacturing company uses X-bar and R charts to monitor the production of a critical component. The charts reveal that the process is out of control, with variations in both the mean and range exceeding the control limits. Root cause analysis identifies a problem with the machine tooling, which is promptly addressed.

**Example 2: Regression Analysis for Process Improvement**

A production line for electronic devices is experiencing frequent failures due to component defects. Regression analysis is used to identify the factors that influence the quality of the components, revealing that the temperature during the soldering process is a significant factor. Adjusting the soldering temperature improves the quality of the components and reduces failures.

**Example 3: Six Sigma for Process Optimization**

A company implements Six Sigma to improve the quality of its product assembly line. The Define phase identifies the need to reduce defects in the assembly process. The Measure phase collects data on the current process performance. The Analyze phase identifies the root causes of defects, such as inadequate training and equipment maintenance. The Improve phase implements solutions to address these issues, and the Control phase establishes controls to sustain the improvements.

#### Conclusion

Process optimization is essential for maintaining high-quality standards and achieving operational excellence. By employing statistical process control methods, regression analysis, and other optimization techniques, companies can systematically analyze and improve production processes. This leads to reduced waste, lower costs, and improved product quality, ultimately enhancing overall business performance. #### Core Optimization Algorithms Implementation

**Objective Function**:
- Define the objective function to minimize total production time:
$$
\min T = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (t_{i} + s_{i}),
$$
where \(T\) is the total production time, \(t_{i}\) is the processing time for job \(i\), and \(s_{i}\) is the setup time for job \(i\).

**Constraints**:
- Machine constraints:
$$
s_{i} + t_{i} \leq s_{i-1} + t_{i-1} \quad \text{for all} \quad i = 2, 3, ..., n.
$$
- Labor constraints:
$$
L_j \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{t_{i}}{T_j},
$$
where \(L_j\) is the number of labor hours available in shift \(j\), and \(T_j\) is the total processing time for all jobs in shift \(j\).
- Demand constraints:
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i} \geq D,
$$
where \(x_{i}\) is the production quantity for job \(i\), and \(D\) is the total demand.

**Algorithm Implementation**:
- **Linear Programming (LP)**:
    ```python
    from scipy.optimize import linprog

    # Objective function coefficients
    c = [-1, -1]  # Minimize both t_i and s_i

    # Constraint coefficients
    A = [[1, 1], [1, 0], [0, 1]]
    b = [t_i + s_i, t_i, s_i]

    result = linprog(c, A_ub=A, b_ub=b)
    print("Optimal production schedule:", result.x)
    ```
- **Genetic Algorithm**:

    ```python
    population_size = 100
    mutation_rate = 0.05
    selection_rate = 0.5

    # Genetic algorithm execution

    print("Optimal production schedule:", optimal_solution)
    ```
