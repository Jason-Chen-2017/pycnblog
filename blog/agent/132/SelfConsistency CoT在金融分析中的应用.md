#### 2.2 Mathematical Formulations

**Equations and Their Significance**: The mathematical formulations used in Self-Consistency CoT models are crucial for deriving meaningful insights and making accurate predictions. Here are some key equations:

1. **Risk-Return Tradeoff Equation**:
   $$ R_i = \alpha_i + \beta_i \cdot R_m + \epsilon_i $$
   This equation represents the risk-return relationship in portfolio management, where \( R_i \) is the expected return on asset \( i \), \( \alpha_i \) is the asset-specific risk, \( \beta_i \) is the asset's beta (measure of systematic risk), \( R_m \) is the market return, and \( \epsilon_i \) is the error term.

2. **Value at Risk (VaR)**:
   $$ VaR = \alpha + \beta \cdot Z $$
   This equation is used to calculate the Value at Risk, which represents the maximum potential loss over a specified time period at a given confidence level. Here, \( \alpha \) is the minimum return, \( \beta \) is the factor determining the risk, and \( Z \) is the critical value from the standard normal distribution.

3. **Black-Scholes Equation**:
   $$ dS = \mu S dt + \sigma S dW $$
   This equation is the basis for the Black-Scholes model, which is used to price options and other financial derivatives. Here, \( S \) is the stock price, \( \mu \) is the expected return, \( \sigma \) is the volatility, and \( W \) is a Wiener process representing randomness.

**Application Scenarios**: These mathematical models are applied in various scenarios within financial analysis:

1. **Risk Management**: Equations like VaR and Black-Scholes are used to assess and manage financial risk.
2. **Portfolio Management**: The risk-return tradeoff equation helps in constructing and managing diversified portfolios.
3. **Market Forecasting**: Stochastic processes and statistical models are used to forecast market trends and asset prices.

**Illustrative Examples**:

**Example 1: Risk Assessment**

Consider a portfolio of three assets (A, B, and C) with the following characteristics:

| Asset | Expected Return | Beta |
|-------|-----------------|------|
| A     | 0.12            | 1.2  |
| B     | 0.10            | 0.8  |
| C     | 0.08            | 0.5  |

To assess the risk of this portfolio, we can calculate the portfolio's beta using the risk-return tradeoff equation:

$$ \beta_{portfolio} = \frac{w_A \cdot \beta_A + w_B \cdot \beta_B + w_C \cdot \beta_C}{w_A + w_B + w_C} $$

Assuming equal weights for all assets (\( w_A = w_B = w_C = 0.33 \)), we get:

$$ \beta_{portfolio} = \frac{0.33 \cdot 1.2 + 0.33 \cdot 0.8 + 0.33 \cdot 0.5}{0.33 + 0.33 + 0.33} = 0.875 $$

This indicates that the portfolio has a beta of 0.875, reflecting its risk relative to the overall market.

**Example 2: Value at Risk (VaR)**

Suppose we want to calculate the 95% VaR for a portfolio with an expected return of 0.1 and a beta of 1.0. Assuming the market return is 0.05 with a volatility of 0.2, we can use the VaR equation:

$$ VaR = \alpha + \beta \cdot Z $$
$$ VaR = 0.1 + 1.0 \cdot 1.645 \approx 0.271 $$

This means that there is a 5% chance that the portfolio will lose more than 0.271 over a given period.

**Example 3: Black-Scholes Model**

Consider a stock with a current price of $50, an expected return of 0.12, and a volatility of 0.3. Suppose we want to price a call option with a strike price of $55 and one year to expiration. Using the Black-Scholes equation:

$$ dS = \mu S dt + \sigma S dW $$
$$ C = S \cdot N(d_1) - K \cdot e^{-rT} \cdot N(d_2) $$
where:
- \( d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}} \)
- \( d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T} \)
- \( N(\cdot) \) is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution
- \( r \) is the risk-free rate

Plugging in the values:

$$ d_1 = \frac{\ln(50/55) + (0.05 + 0.3^2/2) \cdot 1}{0.3 \sqrt{1}} \approx 0.154 $$
$$ d_2 = 0.154 - 0.3 \sqrt{1} \approx -0.142 $$
$$ C = 50 \cdot \Phi(0.154) - 55 \cdot e^{-0.05 \cdot 1} \cdot \Phi(-0.142) $$
$$ C \approx 50 \cdot 0.5568 - 55 \cdot 0.8574 $$
$$ C \approx 27.84 - 46.77 $$
$$ C \approx -18.93 $$

(Note: Since the call option price cannot be negative, this indicates that the stock is currently out of the money and therefore the call option has no intrinsic value.)
