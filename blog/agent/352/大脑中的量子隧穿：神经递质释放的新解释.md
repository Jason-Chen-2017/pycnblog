#### Chapter 1: What is Quantum Tunnelling?

**Introduction:**
Quantum tunnelling is a phenomenon rooted in quantum mechanics that describes the ability of a particle, such as an electron or a neutron, to pass through a potential energy barrier even if its energy is less than the barrier's height. This seemingly paradoxical process challenges classical physics and has far-reaching implications for various fields, including quantum chemistry, solid-state physics, and recently, biology.

**Core Concepts and Principles:**

1. **Wave-Particle Duality:**
   Quantum tunnelling arises from the wave-particle duality of quantum systems. In the quantum realm, particles exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties. This duality is described by the wavefunction, which provides the probability distribution of finding a particle at a particular location. When a particle encounters a potential energy barrier, its wavefunction can extend into the classically forbidden region beyond the barrier, allowing for a finite probability of tunnelling through the barrier.

2. **Potential Energy Barriers:**
   A potential energy barrier is a region in space where the potential energy is higher than the surrounding regions. In classical mechanics, a particle with insufficient energy to overcome such a barrier cannot cross it. However, in quantum mechanics, the wave-like nature of particles enables them to tunnel through the barrier, albeit with a probability that decreases exponentially with the barrier's height and width.

3. **Tunnelling Probability:**
   The probability of a particle tunnelling through a potential energy barrier is governed by its wavefunction and the properties of the barrier. The more confined the wavefunction within the barrier, the higher the tunnelling probability. The tunnelling probability is typically quantified by the transmission coefficient, which represents the probability of a particle passing through the barrier without being reflected.

4. **Tunnelling Time:**
   While the probability of tunnelling is well-defined, the time it takes for a particle to tunnel through a barrier is more challenging to determine. In classical physics, the time for a particle to cross a barrier is determined by its energy and the barrier's width. In quantum mechanics, the concept of tunnelling time is less clear-cut due to the probabilistic nature of the process.

**Core Concepts and Principles:**

1. **Synaptic Vesicles:**
   - Synaptic vesicles are small, membrane-bound sacs that store neurotransmitters within the presynaptic terminal.
   - These vesicles are formed through the Golgi apparatus and transported to the presynaptic terminal via the endosomal-lysosomal system.

2. **Release Mechanisms:**
   - The release of neurotransmitters from synaptic vesicles is triggered by an action potential arriving at the presynaptic terminal.
   - The influx of calcium ions (\(Ca^{2+}\)) into the presynaptic terminal is the primary trigger for neurotransmitter release.
   - The increase in \(Ca^{2+}\) concentration causes the synaptic vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane, leading to the exocytosis of neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft.

3. **Calcium-Induced Fusion:**
   - The process of synaptic vesicle fusion with the presynaptic membrane is tightly regulated by \(Ca^{2+}\).
   - Calcium channels in the presynaptic membrane open in response to an action potential, allowing \(Ca^{2+}\) to enter the terminal.
   - The binding of \(Ca^{2+}\) to specific proteins (such as synaptotagmin) initiates the fusion of synaptic vesicles with the presynaptic membrane.

4. **SNARE Complex Formation:**
   - The fusion of synaptic vesicles with the presynaptic membrane involves the formation of a complex known as the SNARE complex.
   - The v-SNAREs (vesicle-specific SNAREs) on the synaptic vesicle membrane interact with t-SNAREs (target-specific SNAREs) on the presynaptic membrane.
   - This interaction brings the vesicle and membrane into close proximity, facilitating fusion and neurotransmitter release.
