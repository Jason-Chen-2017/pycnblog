#### 1.1.1 Background

Emotional economics, as a field, has its roots in the growing recognition of the role emotions play in economic decision-making. The origin of this discipline can be traced back to the early 2000s when researchers began to question the traditional assumptions of economic theory that individuals make rational decisions based solely on self-interest and utility maximization. Instead, they observed that emotions significantly influence economic behavior, from consumer choices to investment decisions and even economic policy formulation. This shift in perspective prompted the development of emotional economics, which aims to integrate emotional factors into economic models to provide a more comprehensive understanding of human behavior in economic contexts.

The importance of emotional economics lies in its ability to address the limitations of traditional economic theories. By acknowledging the role of emotions, emotional economics can better explain phenomena that are often unaccounted for in classical economic models. For instance, it can help explain the high demand for luxury goods, the emotional attachment to brands, and the influence of social factors on economic decisions. Furthermore, emotional economics has practical implications for various fields, including marketing, psychology, and public policy, where understanding emotional drivers can lead to more effective strategies and policies.

#### 1.1.2 Definition and Description

Emotional economics is a multidisciplinary field that bridges psychology, economics, sociology, and neuroscience to explore the impact of emotions on economic behavior. It is concerned with understanding how emotions influence decision-making processes, individual preferences, and social interactions within economic systems. The core concept of emotional economics is that emotions, similar to rational considerations, shape economic decisions and behaviors, often in ways that traditional economic theories fail to capture.

To define emotional economics, we can consider its key components:

- **Emotions**: These are the psychological and physiological responses to events or stimuli that arise from the brain's processing of information. Emotions can range from basic feelings like happiness, sadness, anger, and fear to more complex emotions like love, pride, and envy.

- **Economic Behavior**: This encompasses all actions and decisions made by individuals, organizations, or societies that involve the exchange of goods, services, or resources. Economic behavior is influenced by various factors, including rational considerations, social norms, and emotional responses.

- **Economic Systems**: These are the structures and institutions through which economic activities are organized and resources are allocated. Emotional economics examines how emotions affect the functioning and outcomes of these systems.

In summary, emotional economics is defined as the study of how emotions influence economic decisions and behaviors, and how these emotional factors can be integrated into economic models and theories to provide a more nuanced understanding of economic phenomena.

### Chapter 2: The Definition and Characteristics of Emotional Value

#### 2.1.1 Meaning of Emotional Value

Emotional value is a concept that seeks to quantify the impact of emotions on economic decision-making and behavior. It represents the perceived worth or significance of emotional experiences in relation to economic activities. Unlike traditional economic values, which are typically measured in terms of utility or monetary gain, emotional value captures the non-monetary benefits and drawbacks derived from emotional experiences. These experiences can range from the joy of purchasing a luxury item to the distress caused by a financial loss.

Defining emotional value requires an understanding of how emotions influence economic decisions. Emotions can affect these decisions in several ways:

1. **Preference Formation**: Emotions shape individual preferences by influencing the perceived desirability of different options. For example, a positive emotional response to a product can increase its perceived value, leading to a higher likelihood of purchase.

2. **Risk Assessment**: Emotions can alter individuals' perceptions of risk and uncertainty. A high level of fear or anxiety may lead to conservative decision-making, while excitement and optimism may encourage riskier choices.

3. **Decision Speed**: Emotional factors can influence the speed at which decisions are made. Emotional responses often lead to quicker, more intuitive decisions, while rational considerations may require more time and analysis.

4. **Memory and Recall**: Emotions can enhance memory and recall, making emotionally significant events more likely to be remembered and influence future decisions. Positive emotional experiences may lead to brand loyalty, while negative experiences can result in reputational damage.

By quantifying emotional value, we can better understand how emotional experiences impact economic behavior and inform strategies in various fields such as marketing, finance, and public policy. Emotional value measurement can help businesses design more effective marketing campaigns, financial institutions assess the emotional risks of investments, and policymakers develop policies that consider the emotional well-being of citizens.

#### 2.1.2 Characteristics of Emotional Value

Emotional value possesses several unique characteristics that differentiate it from traditional economic values. Understanding these characteristics is essential for accurately measuring and interpreting emotional value in economic contexts.

1. **Subjectivity**: Emotional value is highly subjective, as it is influenced by individual perceptions and emotional experiences. What one person finds emotionally valuable may not be the same for another. This subjectivity means that emotional value cannot be objectively measured in the same way monetary value can.

2. **Contextual Dependence**: Emotional value is context-dependent, meaning that the emotional significance of an event or product can vary depending on the circumstances. For example, a luxury car may have high emotional value for someone who has just received a promotion, but low value for someone who is facing financial difficulties.

3. **Dynamic Nature**: Emotional value is not static; it can change over time and in response to different experiences and contexts. Emotions can fluctuate, and so can the value attached to them. For instance, the emotional value of a personal relationship can change as the relationship evolves.

4. **Impact on Decision-Making**: Emotional value can have a significant impact on decision-making processes. Emotions can override rational considerations, leading to decisions that are driven more by feelings than by logical analysis. This emotional influence can be seen in the high demand for luxury goods, where the emotional value of the product often outweighs its utilitarian value.

5. **Interconnectedness**: Emotional value is interconnected with other economic and social factors. For example, social norms and cultural contexts can shape emotional responses and, consequently, the emotional value of goods and services. Similarly, emotional value can influence social interactions and relationships, which in turn affect economic behaviors and outcomes.

#### 3.1.3 Overview of Quantification Methods

   - **Strengths**: Behavioral experiments provide a controlled environment for observing how emotional factors influence decision-making and behavior.    - **Limitations**: Behavioral experiments may lack external validity, as the controlled environment may not fully represent real-world situations. Additionally, experimental design can be complex and time-consuming, requiring careful planning and execution.
   - **Applications**: Behavioral experiments are widely used in psychological research, behavioral economics, and marketing studies. They can help validate theoretical models of emotional value and provide insights into the mechanisms through which emotions influence economic behaviors.

3. **Physiological Measures**
   - **Strengths**: Physiological measures, such as heart rate variability, skin conductance, and facial expressions, provide objective indicators of emotional responses. These measures are less susceptible to biases and subjective interpretation, offering a more accurate and reliable assessment of emotional value.
   - **Limitations**: Physiological measures can be challenging to collect, as they often require specialized equipment and technical expertise. They may also be influenced by factors other than emotions, such as physical activity and environmental conditions.
   - **Applications**: Physiological measures are used in psychological research, medical studies, and human-computer interaction studies. They can provide insights into the emotional experiences of individuals and how these experiences are reflected in their physiological responses.

4. **Sentiment Analysis**
   - **Strengths**: Sentiment analysis leverages natural language processing techniques to analyze textual data for emotional content. It allows for the large-scale extraction of emotional information from social media, customer reviews, and other textual sources.    - **Limitations**: Sentiment analysis can be error-prone, particularly when dealing with complex emotions or ambiguous language. It also relies on the accuracy of the underlying language models, which can vary in performance.
   - **Applications**: Sentiment analysis is widely used in market research, brand monitoring, and social media analytics. It can help businesses understand consumer sentiment and identify trends and patterns in emotional responses to products and campaigns.

   - **Applications**: Neuroimaging is used in psychological research and neuroscience studies to understand the relationship between emotions and economic behaviors. It can provide insights into the neural pathways and brain regions involved in emotional decision-making.

In summary, the choice of quantification method for emotional value depends on the research objectives, available resources, and the nature of the data. Each method has its own strengths and limitations, and a combination of methods may be necessary to obtain a comprehensive understanding of emotional value. By leveraging these methods, researchers and practitioners can develop accurate and reliable measures of emotional value, enhancing our understanding of the role of emotions in economic decision-making and behavior.

### Chapter 4: Applications of Emotional Economics

#### 4.1.1 Consumer Behavior Analysis

**Key Concepts and Theories**

1. **Hedonic Pricing**: Hedonic pricing is a concept that takes into account the emotional value associated with a product or service. It involves pricing products based on the combined utility of both the functional and emotional benefits they provide. For example, a luxury car may be priced higher not only because of its superior performance and features but also due to the emotional satisfaction it provides through status and identity.

2. **Emotion-Specific Preferences**: Emotional economics recognizes that individuals have different preferences based on their emotional states. These preferences can vary widely, influencing purchasing decisions. For instance, a consumer may be more inclined to buy a specific brand of chocolate when in a positive mood compared to a negative mood.

3. **Emotional Contagion**: Emotional contagion refers to the spread of emotions from one person to another, either directly or through social media and other communication channels. This concept is important in understanding how emotional experiences can influence consumer behavior on a larger scale.

**Methodologies and Tools**

1. **Sentiment Analysis**: Sentiment analysis is a powerful tool for analyzing consumer behavior through emotional economics.
