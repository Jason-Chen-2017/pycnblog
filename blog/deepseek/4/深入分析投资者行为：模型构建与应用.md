### Key Models in Investor Behavior Analysis

#### 2.1 Behavioral Finance Concepts

Behavioral finance is a field that seeks to understand the psychological and emotional factors that influence investor behavior and decision-making. It is grounded in the premise that traditional financial models, which assume rational behavior, do not fully capture the complexities of human decision-making. Key concepts in behavioral finance include cognitive biases, heuristics, and emotional influences.

**Cognitive Biases**: These are systematic errors in thinking that lead to irrational decision-making. Examples include confirmation bias, where individuals favor information that confirms their preexisting beliefs, and overconfidence bias, where individuals overestimate their ability to predict future outcomes.

**Heuristics**: These are mental shortcuts or rules of thumb that individuals use to make decisions quickly. While they can be efficient, they can also lead to errors, such as the availability heuristic, where individuals overestimate the likelihood of events based on how easily examples come to mind.

**Emotional Influences**: Emotions such as fear, greed, and hope can significantly impact investor behavior. For example, fear of missing out (FOMO) can lead to overinvestment in certain assets, while fear of losing money can result in panic selling.

#### 2.2 Model Characteristics and Comparisons

Several models have been proposed to analyze investor behavior, each with its own strengths and limitations. Below is a comparison of three prominent models: the Prospect Theory, the Cumulative Prospect Theory, and the Behavioral Portfolio Theory.

**Prospect Theory**

Developed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, Prospect Theory is a behavioral model that attempts to explain how individuals make decisions under uncertainty. It posits that people are more sensitive to potential losses than gains, a concept known as loss aversion. The model is characterized by two parameters: the subjective value of gains and losses, and the reference point from which gains and losses are measured.

*Advantages*:
- Accurately captures loss aversion and the non-linear shape of utility functions.
- Accounts for the psychological impact of uncertainty.

*Disadvantages*:
- Can be difficult to apply in practical settings due to its complexity.
- Assumes a fixed reference point, which may not always hold true.

**Cumulative Prospect Theory**

The Cumulative Prospect Theory (CPT) is an extension of the Prospect Theory that addresses some of its limitations. It allows for a dynamic reference point and introduces a time discount factor, which adjusts the value of future gains and losses based on how soon they are expected to occur.

*Advantages*:
- More flexible than Prospect Theory, accommodating dynamic decision-making.
- Accounts for time preference and temporal discounting.

*Disadvantages*:
- Still complex and challenging to implement in practical applications.
- Requires accurate estimation of the time discount factor.

**Behavioral Portfolio Theory**

Behavioral Portfolio Theory (BPT) is based on the idea that investors' portfolio choices are influenced by their psychological biases and heuristics. It aims to create a portfolio that minimizes the impact of these biases on investment decisions.

*Advantages*:
- Provides a practical framework for managing investment portfolios.
- Accounts for the role of psychological factors in portfolio construction.

*Disadvantages*:
- Lacks formal mathematical foundation.
- Assumes that all cognitive biases can be systematically addressed through portfolio construction.

#### 2.4 Mathematical Models and Formulas

To provide a more detailed understanding of these models, we will present the mathematical formulas that underpin them. These formulas are crucial for implementing and analyzing the models in practice.

**Prospect Theory**

The utility function in Prospect Theory is given by:

$$
u(pX + (1-p)Y) = \sqrt{p} \cdot \sqrt{X - R} + (1-\sqrt{p}) \cdot \sqrt{Y - R}
$$

where \( p \) is the probability of a gain, \( X \) and \( Y \) are potential gains and losses, and \( R \) is the reference point.

**Cumulative Prospect Theory**

The cumulative prospect theory utility function is more complex and is defined as:

$$
u(C) = \frac{1}{1 + \delta} \left( \sqrt{p} \cdot \sqrt{C_X} + (1-\sqrt{p}) \cdot \sqrt{C_Y} \right)
$$

where \( C_X \) and \( C_Y \) are the cumulative gains and losses, and \( \delta \) is the time discount factor.

**Behavioral Portfolio Theory**

Behavioral Portfolio Theory does not have a single mathematical formula but is based on a set of principles that guide the construction of portfolios. Key principles include:

- Minimizing the impact of cognitive biases on portfolio choices.
- Incorporating emotional factors into the portfolio optimization process.
- Using heuristics to simplify investment decisions.

#### 3.3 Mathematical Model and Formulas

To provide a deeper understanding of the algorithm's underlying principles, we will present the mathematical models and formulas used in the process. These models are essential for understanding how the algorithm makes predictions and evaluates performance.

**Probability Model for Prediction**

The probability model used in the algorithm can be represented as:

$$
P(Y|X) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp \left( \sum_{j=1}^{J} w_j \phi_j(X) \right)
$$

where \( P(Y|X) \) is the probability of the target variable \( Y \) given the feature vector \( X \), \( Z \) is the normalization constant, \( w_j \) are the model weights, and \( \phi_j(X) \) are the feature mappings.

**Cost Function for Model Evaluation**

The cost function used to evaluate the model's performance is typically the cross-entropy loss, defined as:

$$
J(\Theta) = -\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{k=1}^{K} y_k^{(i)} \log (\hat{h}_k^{(i)})
$$

where \( J(\Theta) \) is the cost function, \( m \) is the number of training examples, \( y_k^{(i)} \) is the true label, \( \hat{h}_k^{(i)} \) is the predicted probability for class \( k \), and \( K \) is the number of classes.

**Gradient Descent Optimization**

The optimization process involves minimizing the cost function using gradient descent. The update rule for the model weights is given by:

$$
\theta_j := \theta_j - \alpha \frac{\partial J(\Theta)}{\partial \theta_j}
$$

where \( \theta_j \) is the weight to be updated, \( \alpha \) is the learning rate, and \( \frac{\partial J(\Theta)}{\partial \theta_j} \) is the gradient of the cost function with respect to \( \theta_j \).
