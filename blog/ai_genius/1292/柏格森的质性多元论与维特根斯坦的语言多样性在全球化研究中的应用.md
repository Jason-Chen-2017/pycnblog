#### 1.1.1 Definition and Historical Background

Globalization, in its broadest sense, refers to the process of increased interconnectedness and interdependence among countries through the exchange of goods, services, information, and ideas. It is a multifaceted phenomenon that encompasses economic, political, social, and cultural dimensions. The term "globalization" gained prominence in the latter half of the 20th century, although the underlying processes have been occurring throughout human history.

Historically, globalization can be traced back to ancient trade routes such as the Silk Road, which facilitated the exchange of goods, ideas, and culture between different civilizations. The Age of Exploration in the 15th and 16th centuries marked a significant milestone in globalization as European powers established colonies around the world, leading to the exchange of goods, technologies, and cultures. The Industrial Revolution further accelerated globalization by promoting the mass production of goods and the spread of new technologies.

In the 20th century, globalization was significantly influenced by the establishment of international organizations such as the United Nations and the World Bank, which aimed to promote global cooperation and economic development. The end of the Cold War in the early 1990s removed many barriers to global trade and investment, leading to a new era of economic liberalization and integration.

#### 1.1.2 Impact of Globalization on Society

Globalization has had a profound impact on society, altering the way individuals live, work, and interact with one another. Some of the key impacts include:

1. **Economic Integration**: Globalization has led to the establishment of a global economy, with countries increasingly relying on international trade and investment. This has created new opportunities for businesses but has also led to job displacement in certain industries.

2. **Cultural Exchange**: The ease of travel and communication has facilitated the exchange of cultural practices, ideas, and values across borders. This has enriched societies but has also led to cultural homogenization and the erosion of traditional cultures.

3. **Technological Advancement**: Globalization has been a driving force behind technological innovation, as companies and researchers collaborate across borders to develop new products and services.

4. **Environmental Impact**: Globalization has led to increased resource consumption and environmental degradation. However, it has also facilitated the development of global environmental policies and agreements.

5. **Political Change**: Globalization has influenced political systems by promoting democracy and human rights. At the same time, it has also led to the rise of new forms of global governance, such as international organizations and transnational corporations.

#### 1.2.1 Bergson's Philosophy

Henri Bergson (1859-1941) was a French philosopher and prominent thinker in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. His work significantly influenced various fields, including philosophy, psychology, and literature. Bergson's philosophy is centered around the concept of "élan vital" or "vital force," which he considered to be the driving force behind all life processes. This vital force is an intrinsic part of living organisms and is responsible for their evolution and adaptation to changing environments.

One of Bergson's key ideas is the concept of "qualitative multiplicity," which he contrasted with quantitative multiplicity. While quantitative multiplicity focuses on the number of things, qualitative multiplicity emphasizes the differences in quality and individuality among these things. Bergson argued that the essence of life lies in these qualitative differences, which are constantly evolving and changing. He believed that these changes are driven by the vital force and are essential for the development and progress of individuals and societies.

Bergson's philosophy also emphasized the importance of intuition over reason. He argued that reason is primarily concerned with categorizing and analyzing phenomena, while intuition allows us to grasp the complexity and fluidity of reality. Intuition, according to Bergson, is a form of direct perception that enables us to understand the underlying dynamics of life processes.

#### 1.2.2 Wittgenstein's Philosophy

Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889-1951) was an Austrian-British philosopher whose work has had a profound impact on the philosophy of language, logic, and mathematics. His philosophical ideas evolved significantly throughout his career, and he is often divided into two distinct periods: the early (1920s) and the later (1940s and 1950s) periods.

In the early period, Wittgenstein's philosophy was characterized by the idea that meaning is not an inherent property of words or symbols but is rather determined by their use in language games. He argued that language is a tool for communication, and the meaning of words is derived from how they are used in specific contexts. This idea is encapsulated in his famous book "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus," which presents a systematic view of reality and language.

In the later period, Wittgenstein's philosophy became more interested in the everyday use of language and the nature of meaning. He proposed the concept of "language games" to illustrate how meaning is context-dependent and varies across different forms of language use. He argued that there is no one-size-fits-all definition of meaning, as it is shaped by the specific rules and conventions of different language games.

Wittgenstein also made significant contributions to the philosophy of mathematics. He argued that mathematical propositions are not statements about reality but are rather conventions that help us communicate and reason about complex concepts. His work "Philosophical Investigations" is a key text in this area, challenging many traditional views on the nature of mathematics and logic.

#### 1.2.3 Comparing and Contrasting Their Ideas

While Bergson and Wittgenstein had different philosophical orientations, their ideas share some common themes, particularly in their emphasis on the importance of context and individuality.

**Common Themes**

1. **Contextualism**: Both Bergson and Wittgenstein emphasized the role of context in determining meaning and understanding. Bergson's concept of qualitative multiplicity recognizes the unique qualities and contexts of different phenomena, while Wittgenstein's language games highlight the role of specific contexts in shaping meaning.

2. **Individuality**: Both philosophers recognized the importance of individuality and the unique characteristics of individuals and objects. For Bergson, individuality is a fundamental aspect of life and is crucial for understanding the evolution and development of societies. For Wittgenstein, individual language users play a central role in the creation and use of language.

**Differences**

1. **Metaphysical vs. Pragmatic Approach**: Bergson's philosophy has a more metaphysical orientation, with a focus on the intrinsic nature of life processes and the role of the vital force. In contrast, Wittgenstein's philosophy is more pragmatic, focusing on the everyday use of language and the practical aspects of communication.

2. **Intuition vs. Language Games**: Bergson emphasized the role of intuition in understanding the complexities of life processes, while Wittgenstein focused on the role of language games in shaping meaning and understanding.

3. **Scope of Application**: While both philosophers' ideas have broad applications in various fields, Bergson's philosophy is more commonly associated with the study of life sciences and psychology, while Wittgenstein's philosophy is more influential in philosophy, linguistics, and artificial intelligence.

In conclusion, Bergson's qualitative multiplicity and Wittgenstein's linguistic diversity offer valuable insights into the complexities of globalization.
